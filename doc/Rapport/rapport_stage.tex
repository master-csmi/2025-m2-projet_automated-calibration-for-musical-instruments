\begin{abstract}

This internship focuses on the development of a differentiable numerical framework for the automated calibration of physical models of single-reed musical instruments. The acoustic wave propagation inside the instrument is described by a one-dimensional hyperbolic system coupled with a nonlinear reed model and radiation boundary conditions. The governing equations are spatially discretized using the Discontinuous Galerkin (DG) method, while time integration combines an explicit Runge--Kutta scheme for the acoustic field with a Crank--Nicolson discretization for the reed dynamics. The nonlinear coupling at the mouthpiece is solved using a damped Newton method.

Beyond the numerical solver itself, particular emphasis is placed on inverse problems. By combining automatic differentiation with a multi-resolution spectral loss based on the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT), the proposed framework enables the estimation of unknown physical parameters directly from acoustic pressure signals. Numerical experiments performed on synthetic OpenWind data demonstrate the ability of the method to recover several reed parameters under both noise-free and noisy conditions. These results constitute a first step toward the automatic calibration of physics-based virtual musical instruments from experimental measurements.

\markboth{Introduction}{}

This Master's internship is a continuation of the project carried out during
the third semester of the CSMI Master's degree, devoted to the automated
calibration of physical models for reed wind instruments.

The internship was conducted under the supervision of Dr Emmanuel Franck,
Dr Laurent Navoret and Dr Victor Michel-Dansac from the Institut de Recherche
Mathématique Avancée (IRMA) in Strasbourg, together with Dr Juliette Chabassier
from Modartt.

Modartt (MOdels and Data for ARTs and Technology) \cite{Modartt} is a French
company specializing in physically based sound synthesis. Since the release of
\textit{Pianoteq} in 2006, the company has developed virtual musical instruments
based on physical modeling rather than sample playback, providing realistic
sound synthesis while preserving a strong physical interpretation of the
underlying models.

The mathematical model considered in this work describes sound production in
reed wind instruments through the coupling of one-dimensional acoustic wave
propagation with a nonlinear model of the reed. Although the geometry of the
instrument can generally be measured accurately, several parameters governing
the reed dynamics and its nonlinear coupling with the acoustic resonator remain
difficult or impossible to determine experimentally. These quantities must
therefore be inferred from acoustic observations, leading naturally to an
inverse problem.

The objective of this internship is to develop an automated calibration
framework capable of identifying both scalar physical parameters and, more
generally, unknown functions appearing in the governing equations. The proposed
methodology relies on differentiable numerical simulations implemented in JAX,
which makes it possible to compute gradients of acoustic objectives with
respect to the model parameters through automatic differentiation.

The methodology is primarily assessed in a controlled setting using synthetic
signals generated with OpenWind, for which the reference physical parameters
are known. This makes it possible to quantify the accuracy of the parameter
recovery procedure, investigate difficult calibration cases, and evaluate its
robustness to measurement noise. The extension of the framework to experimental
recordings provided by Modartt is also investigated. This study highlights
additional difficulties arising when moving from synthetic to real acoustic
data, in particular those related to pressure dimensionalization and signal
normalization, and provides directions for the future application of the
calibration framework to experimental measurements.

The remainder of this manuscript is organized as follows. The first part
introduces the physical and mathematical model of reed wind instruments and
presents its numerical discretization using a Discontinuous Galerkin method.
The resulting forward solver is then validated against both analytical
solutions and OpenWind simulations, and its computational performance is
analyzed. The second part introduces the differentiable formulation of the
solver, automatic differentiation and its relation to discrete adjoint methods,
before formulating the associated inverse problem and the spectral objective
used for calibration. The proposed optimization strategy is subsequently
evaluated on synthetic OpenWind datasets through parameter-recovery experiments,
statistical analyses and robustness studies. Finally, the identification of an
unknown reed-opening function and the challenges associated with the extension
to experimental measurements are discussed.

\section*{Reed Instruments}
\markright{Reed Instruments}

A reed is a thin, flexible lamina mounted at the mouthpiece or at the entrance
of an air column resonator that acts as a valve controlling the airflow generated
by the pressure difference between the player's mouth and the instrument bore
\cite{Universalis_Anche,chaigne:hal-05370840}.

\vspace{0.4cm}

The interaction between the vibrating reed and the acoustic resonator is
responsible for the self-sustained oscillations that produce sound.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{reed_column.png}
\caption{
Schematic representation of a single-reed musical instrument and its main
components. Saxophone image adapted from \cite{cultura_yas280}; reed image
adapted from \cite{bax_reed_guide}. Diagram assembled by the author.
}
\label{fig:reed_schematic}
\end{figure}
There are two principal categories of reed instruments:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Free reeds (harmonicas, accordions, \dots).
    \item Beating reeds (clarinets, saxophones, oboes, bassoons, \dots).
\end{itemize}

In beating reed instruments, which constitute the focus of this work, the reed periodically opens and closes under the combined action of 
the mouth pressure and the acoustic pressure inside the mouthpiece. This nonlinear interaction regulates the airflow entering the instrument 
and excites acoustic waves that propagate along the air column. The geometry of the resonator, together with the reed dynamics, determines 
the resulting sound.

Accurate numerical simulation of this coupled reed--resonator system requires modeling both the wave propagation inside the instrument and 
the nonlinear boundary conditions describing the reed motion. Several parameters governing the reed behavior cannot be directly measured 
and must therefore be estimated from acoustic observations. This motivates the inverse calibration problem addressed in this internship.

\section*{Objectives}

The main objective of this internship is to develop a differentiable
framework for the automated calibration of physical models of reed wind
instruments from acoustic observations.

More specifically, the work aims to:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Develop and validate a differentiable numerical solver for the
    coupled acoustic--reed model.

    \item Formulate the calibration of the physical model as an inverse
    problem based on acoustic pressure observations.

    \item Estimate unknown physical parameters of the reed model using
    gradient-based optimization and automatic differentiation.

    \item Extend the calibration framework from scalar parameter
    identification to the identification of an unknown functional
    relationship, namely the reed opening law $\ell(y)$.

    \item Assess the accuracy and robustness of the proposed calibration
    methodology on synthetic OpenWind data, including its behaviour in
    the presence of measurement noise.

    \item Investigate the requirements and limitations associated with
    transferring the proposed methodology to experimental recordings.
\end{enumerate}


\chapter{Physical Model}
\minitoc
\section{Sound generation mechanism}

Single-reed instruments, such as the saxophone or the clarinet, produce sound through the interaction between an airflow supplied by the musician, a vibrating reed, and the acoustic resonator formed by the instrument bore \cite{chaigne:hal-05370840}.

When the player blows into the mouthpiece, a pressure difference is established between the mouth cavity and the interior of the instrument. Air is forced through the narrow opening between the reed and the mouthpiece. Since the reed is flexible, the aerodynamic force causes it to oscillate, continuously modifying the opening through which the air flows.

The resulting oscillations periodically modulate the airflow entering the resonator. This modulation generates pressure fluctuations that propagate inside the air column in the form of acoustic waves. These waves travel along the bore, are reflected by the instrument boundaries, and interact again with the reed. The continuous exchange of energy between the reed and the resonator hopefully produces self-sustained oscillations, which are responsible for the generation of musical sound.

At the bell of the instrument, the acoustic wave is not entirely transmitted to the surrounding air. A portion of the wave is radiated outside the instrument, while the remaining part is reflected back into the bore. This radiation phenomenon modifies the acoustic impedance of the resonator and influences both the energy losses and the produced sound.

Figure~\ref{fig:reed_generation} summarizes the three physical mechanisms involved in the sound production process.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{generation_onde_anche.png}
    \caption{Illustration of the sound generation mechanism in a single-reed instrument (adapted from Chapter~2 of \cite{vergez2010analyse}).}
    \label{fig:reed_generation}
\end{figure}

The physical behaviour of the instrument results from the continuous interaction of three complementary mechanisms:

\begin{itemize}
    \item the mechanical oscillations of the reed,
    \item the propagation of acoustic waves inside the resonator,
    \item the radiation of acoustic energy at the bell.
\end{itemize}

These mechanisms constitute the basis of the mathematical model introduced in the following section.

\section{Mathematical model}

To reproduce the physical behaviour described above, each physical mechanism is represented by an appropriate mathematical model. Acoustic wave propagation inside the bore is governed by conservation laws, the reed motion is described by a mechanical oscillator, and both subsystems are coupled through nonlinear boundary conditions.

Following the adimensional formulation proposed in \cite{chabassier:hal-03794382}, the behaviour of a single-reed instrument is described by the coupled system

\begin{subequations}
\begin{empheq}[left=\empheqlbrace]{align}
\frac{S(x)}{c S^\ast}\,\partial_t p(x,t) + \partial_x v(x,t) &= 0 \label{eq:1a}\\[4pt]
\frac{S^\ast}{c S(x)}\,\partial_t v(x,t) + \partial_x p(x,t) &= 0 \label{eq:1b}\\[4pt]
Z^\ast_T\,\partial_t \phi + \sqrt{\alpha}\,p(L,t) &= 0 \label{eq:1c}\\[4pt]
-\frac{\beta}{Z^\ast_T}\,p(L,t) + v(L,t) + \sqrt{\alpha}\,\phi &= 0 \label{eq:1d}\\[4pt]
-v(0,t) + \zeta \ell(y)\,F(\gamma-p(0,t))
+ \varepsilon\frac{\kappa}{\omega_r}\partial_t y &=0
\label{eq:1e}\\[6pt]
\frac{1}{\omega_r^2}\partial_{tt}y
+\frac{1}{Q_r\omega_r}\partial_ty
+y-1
&=\varepsilon(\gamma-p(0,t))
\label{eq:1f}
\end{empheq}
\end{subequations}

where $p(x,t)$ denotes the dimensionless acoustic pressure, $v(x,t)$ the
acoustic volume flow, and $y(t)$ the dimensionless reed opening. The nonlinear
function
\begin{equation}
    F(\Delta p)
    =
    \operatorname{sign}(\Delta p)\sqrt{|\Delta p|}
\end{equation}
models the dependence of the airflow on the pressure difference across the reed.

The remaining parameters describe the geometry of the instrument, the reed
dynamics, and the radiation at the bell. Here, $S(x)$ is the bore
cross-sectional area, $S^\ast=S(0)$ is a reference area, $c$ is the speed of
sound, and $Z_T^\ast=S^\ast/S(L)$ is the dimensionless impedance scaling
appearing in the radiation condition. The parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$
characterize the radiation at the bell, while $\omega_r=2\pi f_r$, $Q_r$,
$\kappa$, and $\zeta$ characterize the reed dynamics and its coupling with the
airflow.

Since the model is written in dimensionless form, the acoustic pressure is
scaled by the reed closing pressure. Following the scaling introduced in
\cite{chabassier:hal-03794382}, this characteristic pressure is defined as
\begin{equation}
    P_{\mathrm{close}}
    =
    \frac{M_r}{S_r}\,y_0\,\omega_r^2,
    \label{eq:closing_pressure}
\end{equation}
where $M_r$ and $S_r$ denote the effective reed mass and surface,
respectively, and $y_0$ is the reed opening at rest. The physical and
dimensionless acoustic pressures are therefore related by
\begin{equation}
    p_{\mathrm{phys}}(x,t)
    =
    P_{\mathrm{close}}\,p(x,t).
    \label{eq:pressure_scaling}
\end{equation}

The dimensionless blowing pressure $\gamma$ is obtained using the same
characteristic pressure,
\begin{equation}
    \gamma
    =
    \frac{p_m}{P_{\mathrm{close}}},
    \label{eq:gamma_scaling}
\end{equation}
where $p_m$ denotes the physical mouth pressure.

Although written as a single system, Equations~(\ref{eq:1a})--(\ref{eq:1f})
naturally decompose into three interacting components: acoustic wave
propagation, reed dynamics, and boundary coupling.

\section{Interpretation of the model}

The mathematical model described in the system ~\ref{eq:1a}--\ref{eq:1f} combines equations 
of different nature in order to represent the physical processes occurring inside the instrument.

The propagation of acoustic waves inside the resonator is described by the first two equations ~\ref{eq:1a} and ~\ref{eq:1b}. 
Since the acoustic pressure and the acoustic volume flow evolve both in space and in time, they naturally form a system of partial 
differential equations (PDEs). Under the assumption of plane wave propagation, these equations express the conservation of mass and 
momentum inside the air column.

Unlike the acoustic field, the reed motion is modeled at the mouthpiece. Its displacement depends only on time and is therefore 
modeled by an ordinary differential equation (ODE) ~\ref{eq:1e}--~\ref{eq:1f}. In the present model, the reed is represented as a damped one-degree-of-freedom 
mechanical oscillator excited by the pressure difference across the mouthpiece.

These two subsystems cannot be considered independently. The reed controls the airflow entering the resonator, while the acoustic 
pressure generated inside the bore modifies the reed motion. Their interaction is introduced through the nonlinear boundary condition 
at the mouthpiece, creating the feedback mechanism responsible for self-sustained oscillations.

At the opposite end of the instrument ~\ref{eq:1c}--~\ref{eq:1d}, a radiation boundary condition models the interaction between the resonator and the surrounding air. 
Part of the acoustic energy is radiated outside the instrument, whereas the remaining part is reflected back into the bore. 
This dynamic boundary condition accounts for both radiation losses and the acoustic impedance seen at the bell.

The complete physical model is therefore obtained by coupling

\begin{itemize}
    \item a hyperbolic system of partial differential equations describing acoustic propagation,
    \item a nonlinear ordinary differential equation describing the reed motion,
    \item dynamic boundary conditions coupling the two subsystems.
\end{itemize}

\section{Mathematical properties}

Although each component of the model is relatively simple when considered separately, their coupling 
results in a nonlinear dynamical system whose numerical simulation is particularly challenging.

The acoustic equations constitute a first-order hyperbolic system. Such systems are characterized by 
the propagation of information along characteristic directions at finite speed, making them particularly 
suitable for describing wave propagation \cite{thibault}. Numerical methods must therefore preserve wave amplitudes and 
propagation speeds while limiting artificial numerical dissipation and dispersion.

The reed dynamics introduce an additional mechanical subsystem coupled to the acoustic field. Because 
the airflow depends nonlinearly on both the pressure difference and the reed opening, the interaction between the resonator and the reed remains strongly nonlinear throughout the simulation.

The boundary conditions also play a central role. At the mouthpiece, they provide the nonlinear coupling between the acoustic resonator and the reed oscillator. At the bell, the radiation condition introduces an additional dynamic equation accounting for acoustic radiation into the surrounding air. These boundary conditions govern the exchange of energy between the different physical components.

Consequently, the complete model consists of a coupled PDE--ODE system with nonlinear dynamic boundary conditions. Except for highly simplified configurations, no analytical solution is available. Numerical methods are therefore required to approximate the solution of the governing equations.

In the remainder of this work, the acoustic equations are discretized using the Discontinuous Galerkin method, while suitable time integration schemes are employed to advance both the acoustic field and the reed dynamics.
\chapter{Mathematical Reformulation}
\label{chap:mathematical_reformulation}

\minitoc

The governing equations introduced in the previous chapter provide an accurate
description of the physical mechanisms responsible for sound generation in reed
wind instruments. However, this formulation is primarily motivated by physical
considerations and is not directly suitable for the numerical method employed in
this work.

The Discontinuous Galerkin (DG) method is naturally designed for systems of
hyperbolic conservation laws (see Chapter~5 in \cite{HesthavenWarburton2008}). Consequently, before introducing the numerical
discretization, the acoustic equations must first be rewritten into a
conservative form. This reformulation also reveals the characteristic structure
of the system, which considerably simplifies the treatment of the nonlinear
boundary conditions.

The objective of this chapter is therefore threefold. First, we introduce a
change of variables that transforms the acoustic equations into a conservative
form compatible with the DG framework. Secondly, we derive the characteristic
variables associated with the resulting hyperbolic system. Finally, both the
radiation and reed boundary conditions are rewritten in terms of these
characteristic variables, leading to a formulation particularly well suited for
their numerical treatment.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\section{Conservative formulation}

The acoustic propagation inside the instrument is governed by the coupled
equations

\begin{equation}
\frac{S(x)}{cS^\ast}\,\partial_t p(x,t)
+
\partial_x v(x,t)
=
0,
\label{eq:cons_original_1}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\frac{S^\ast}{cS(x)}\,\partial_t v(x,t)
+
\partial_x p(x,t)
=
0,
\label{eq:cons_original_2}
\end{equation}

where \(p(x,t)\) denotes the acoustic pressure, \(v(x,t)\) the acoustic volume
velocity, \(S(x)\) the local cross-sectional area of the resonator and
\(S^\ast\) a reference section.

Although this formulation is physically meaningful, it presents a significant
difficulty for a DG discretization. Indeed, the coefficients multiplying the
time derivatives depend explicitly on the geometry of the instrument. A direct
spatial discretization would therefore produce local mass matrices depending on
the cross-sectional area, making the numerical implementation unnecessarily
complex.

To overcome this difficulty, we introduce the conservative variables

\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\tilde p \\
\tilde v
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\dfrac{S(x)}{cS^\ast}\,p \\
\dfrac{S^\ast}{cS(x)}\,v
\end{pmatrix}.
\label{eq:conservative_variables}
\end{equation}

Substituting these definitions into Equations
\eqref{eq:cons_original_1}--\eqref{eq:cons_original_2} yields

\begin{equation}
\partial_t \tilde{\mathbf{u}}
+
\partial_x
\left(
A(x)\tilde{\mathbf{u}}
\right)
=
0,
\label{eq:conservative_system}
\end{equation}

where the flux matrix is

\begin{equation}
A(x)
=
\begin{pmatrix}
0 &
\dfrac{cS(x)}{S^\ast}
\\[0.4em]
\dfrac{cS^\ast}{S(x)}
&
0
\end{pmatrix}.
\label{eq:flux_matrix}
\end{equation}

Equation \eqref{eq:conservative_system} is now written under the standard form
of a first-order conservation law,

\begin{equation}
\partial_t\tilde{\mathbf u}
+
\partial_x
\mathbf F(x,\tilde{\mathbf u})
=
0,
\end{equation}

with

\begin{equation}
\mathbf F(x,\tilde{\mathbf u})
=
A(x)\tilde{\mathbf u}.
\end{equation}

This reformulation constitutes the key step allowing the application of the
Discontinuous Galerkin method. Besides recovering the conservative structure of
the equations, it also leads to uniform mass matrices that no longer depend on
the instrument geometry.

It should nevertheless be emphasized that the flux matrix \(A(x)\) remains
spatially dependent when the bore cross-section varies. Consequently, although
its eigenvalues are constant and equal to \(\pm c\), its eigenvectors depend on
the local geometry. This point becomes important when introducing the
characteristic variables in the following section.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\section{Characteristic variables}
\label{sec:characteristic_variables}

The conservative formulation introduced in the previous section is the one
used to discretize and evolve the acoustic field with the Discontinuous
Galerkin method. Nevertheless, the eigenstructure of the acoustic system is
useful for analysing wave propagation and, more importantly in the present
work, for imposing the physical boundary conditions.

Indeed, at each boundary of the computational domain, the characteristic
decomposition makes it possible to distinguish the acoustic component leaving
the domain from the component entering it. The outgoing component is obtained
from the interior DG solution, whereas the incoming component is determined
from the corresponding physical boundary condition.

The characteristic structure is obtained from the eigenstructure of the flux
matrix \(A(x)\), defined in Equation~\eqref{eq:flux_matrix}. The eigenvalues
satisfy
\begin{equation}
\det\left(A(x)-\lambda I\right)=0,
\end{equation}
which gives
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\lambda^\pm=\pm c.
}
\label{eq:characteristic_eigenvalues}
\end{equation}

Thus, the local characteristic velocities are equal to \(+c\) and \(-c\).
Although these eigenvalues do not depend on the cross-sectional area \(S(x)\),
the corresponding eigenvectors do depend on the local geometry. Therefore,
for a non-uniform bore, the characteristic components cannot in general be
interpreted as two globally independent travelling waves.

The corresponding left eigenvectors satisfy
\begin{equation}
l^\pm A(x)
=
\lambda^\pm l^\pm.
\end{equation}

For the positive eigenvalue \(\lambda^+=c\), one obtains
\begin{equation}
l_2
=
\frac{S^\ast}{S(x)}\,l_1,
\end{equation}
whereas for \(\lambda^-=-c\),
\begin{equation}
l_2
=
-
\frac{S^\ast}{S(x)}\,l_1.
\end{equation}

Choosing \(l_1=1\) gives
\begin{equation}
l^+
=
\left(
1,
\frac{S^\ast}{S(x)}
\right),
\qquad
l^-
=
\left(
1,
-
\frac{S^\ast}{S(x)}
\right).
\end{equation}

Up to an arbitrary scaling and ordering of the eigenvectors, the corresponding
characteristic variables can be written as
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
w^\pm
=
\tilde v
\pm
\frac{S^\ast}{S(x)}
\tilde p.
}
\label{eq:riemann_variables}
\end{equation}

Introducing
\begin{equation}
k(x)=\frac{S^\ast}{S(x)},
\end{equation}
the inverse transformation is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\tilde v
=
\frac{w^++w^-}{2},
\qquad
\tilde p
=
\frac{w^+-w^-}{2k(x)}.
}
\label{eq:inverse_characteristic_transform}
\end{equation}

The conservative and characteristic representations therefore contain the
same local information. However, because \(k(x)\) depends on position, the
characteristic variables do not satisfy independent transport equations when
the bore cross-section varies.

Indeed, differentiating Equation~\eqref{eq:riemann_variables} and using the
conservative acoustic equations gives
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\partial_t w^+
+
c\,\partial_x w^+
=
-\frac{c}{2}
\frac{S'(x)}{S(x)}
\left(w^+ + w^-\right),
}
\label{eq:characteristic_plus}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\partial_t w^-
-
c\,\partial_x w^-
=
\frac{c}{2}
\frac{S'(x)}{S(x)}
\left(w^+ + w^-\right).
}
\label{eq:characteristic_minus}
\end{equation}

These equations clarify the influence of the bore geometry. For a uniform
cylindrical bore, \(S'(x)=0\), and the two characteristic variables satisfy
independent transport equations,
\begin{equation}
\partial_t w^+
+
c\,\partial_x w^+
=
0,
\qquad
\partial_t w^-
-
c\,\partial_x w^-
=
0.
\end{equation}
In this particular case, \(w^+\) and \(w^-\) can be directly interpreted as
waves travelling in opposite directions at the speed of sound.

For a spatially varying cross-section, however, the terms involving
\(S'(x)/S(x)\) couple the two characteristic components. The quantities
\(w^\pm\) should therefore be interpreted as \emph{local characteristic
components}, associated respectively with the characteristic velocities
\(+c\) and \(-c\), rather than as globally independent travelling waves.

This local interpretation is sufficient for the treatment of the boundary
conditions. At the mouthpiece \(x=0\), the \(w^-\) component is outgoing,
whereas \(w^+\) enters the computational domain and is determined by the reed
condition. Conversely, at the bell \(x=L\), \(w^+\) is outgoing and \(w^-\)
is determined by the radiation condition. These characteristic relations will
later be used to construct the ghost states required by the DG numerical flux.
The acoustic field itself remains discretized and evolved in conservative
variables.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{characteristics_propagation.png}
    \caption{
        Schematic representation of the local characteristic directions of the
        acoustic system. The characteristic component \(w^+\) is associated with the
        velocity \(+c\), whereas \(w^-\) is associated with the velocity \(-c\).
        For a non-uniform bore, the two components remain coupled through the spatial
        variation of the cross-sectional area.
        }
    \label{fig:characteristics_propagation}
\end{figure}

\section{Radiation boundary condition}

The right boundary of the computational domain corresponds to the bell of the
instrument, where the acoustic wave is partially radiated into the surrounding
air. Unlike a perfectly reflecting boundary, part of the acoustic energy leaves
the resonator while the remaining part is reflected back into the instrument.

Within the present model, this phenomenon is described by the coupled system

\begin{equation}
\begin{cases}
Z_T^\ast\,\partial_t\phi(t)
+
\sqrt{\alpha}\,p(L,t)
=
0,
\\[0.4em]
-\dfrac{\beta}{Z_T^\ast}p(L,t)
+
v(L,t)
+
\sqrt{\alpha}\phi(t)
=
0,
\end{cases}
\label{eq:rad_original}
\end{equation}

where the auxiliary variable $\phi(t)$ models the dynamic response of the
radiation impedance.

The second equation can be rewritten as

\begin{equation}
v(L,t)
=
\dfrac{\beta}{Z_T^\ast}p(L,t)
-
\sqrt{\alpha}\phi(t).
\label{eq:rad_velocity}
\end{equation}

To express this condition in terms of the conservative variables introduced
previously, we use

\begin{equation}
p
=
\dfrac{cS^\ast}{S_R}\tilde p,
\qquad
v
=
\dfrac{cS_R}{S^\ast}\tilde v,
\end{equation}

where $S_R=S(L)$ denotes the cross-sectional area at the bell.

Substituting these expressions into
Equation~\eqref{eq:rad_velocity} gives

\begin{equation}
\tilde v
=
\dfrac{\beta}{Z_T^\ast}
\left(
\dfrac{S^\ast}{S_R}
\right)^2
\tilde p
-
\dfrac{S^\ast}{cS_R}
\sqrt{\alpha}\phi.
\end{equation}

Introducing the characteristic variables

\begin{equation}
w^\pm
=
\tilde v
\pm
k_R\tilde p,
\qquad
k_R
=
\dfrac{S^\ast}{S_R},
\end{equation}

we have

\begin{equation}
\tilde v
=
\dfrac{w^++w^-}{2},
\qquad
\tilde p
=
\dfrac{w^+-w^-}{2k_R}.
\end{equation}

Substituting these expressions into the boundary condition yields

\begin{equation}
\dfrac{w^++w^-}{2}
=
\dfrac{\beta}{Z_T^\ast}
\left(
\dfrac{S^\ast}{S_R}
\right)^2
\dfrac{w^+-w^-}{2k_R}
-
\dfrac{S^\ast}{cS_R}
\sqrt{\alpha}\phi.
\end{equation}

For clarity, we introduce the coefficients

\begin{equation}
K
=
\dfrac{\beta}{Z_T^\ast}
\left(
\dfrac{S^\ast}{S_R}
\right)^2,
\qquad
r=\dfrac{K}{k_R},
\qquad
C
=
\dfrac{S^\ast}{cS_R}\sqrt{\alpha}.
\end{equation}

After straightforward algebraic manipulations, the incoming characteristic is
obtained as

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
w^-
=
\dfrac{r-1}{r+1}w^+
-
\dfrac{2C}{r+1}\phi.
}
\label{eq:rad_final}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:rad_final} expresses the incoming wave solely as a function
of the outgoing wave and the radiation state variable.
Consequently, the radiation boundary condition can be imposed explicitly during
the numerical resolution without requiring any iterative procedure.

Moreover, this characteristic formulation naturally provides the ghost state
required by the numerical flux of the DG method, making its implementation both
simple and physically consistent.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\section{Reed boundary condition}

The left boundary of the instrument differs fundamentally from the radiation
boundary. Instead of coupling the acoustic field with the surrounding air, it
models the interaction between the pressure waves inside the resonator and the
mechanical motion of the reed.

This interaction is responsible for the self-sustained oscillations that
generate the musical sound. Owing to the nonlinear airflow through the reed
channel, the corresponding boundary condition is intrinsically nonlinear.

The coupled reed model is given by

\begin{equation}
\begin{cases}
-v(0,t)
+
\zeta\ell(y)
F(\gamma-p(0,t))
+
\varepsilon\dfrac{\kappa}{\omega_r}\dot y
=
0,
\\[0.5em]
\dfrac{1}{\omega_r^2}\ddot y
+
\dfrac{1}{Q_r\omega_r}\dot y
+
y-1
=
\varepsilon(\gamma-p(0,t)).
\end{cases}
\label{eq:reed_original}
\end{equation}

The first equation prescribes the acoustic velocity at the entrance of the tube,
while the second governs the mechanical dynamics of the reed.

Using the conservative variables,

\begin{equation}
p
=
\dfrac{cS^\ast}{S_L}\tilde p,
\qquad
v
=
\dfrac{cS_L}{S^\ast}\tilde v,
\end{equation}

where $S_L=S(0)$, the acoustic boundary condition becomes

\begin{equation}
\tilde v
=
\dfrac{S^\ast}{cS_L}
\left[
\zeta\ell(y)
F\left(
\gamma
-
\dfrac{cS^\ast}{S_L}\tilde p
\right)
+
\varepsilon
\dfrac{\kappa}{\omega_r}\dot y
\right].
\end{equation}

Introducing the characteristic variables

\begin{equation}
w^\pm
=
\tilde v
\pm
k_L\tilde p,
\qquad
k_L
=
\dfrac{S^\ast}{S_L},
\end{equation}

together with

\begin{equation}
\tilde v
=
\dfrac{w^++w^-}{2},
\qquad
\tilde p
=
\dfrac{w^+-w^-}{2k_L},
\end{equation}

the boundary equation becomes

\begin{equation}
\dfrac{w^++w^-}{2}
=
\dfrac{S^\ast}{cS_L}
\left[
\zeta\ell(y)
F
\left(
\gamma
-
\dfrac{cS^\ast}{S_L}
\dfrac{w^+-w^-}{2k_L}
\right)
+
\varepsilon
\dfrac{\kappa}{\omega_r}\dot y
\right].
\end{equation}

Unlike the radiation condition, the unknown characteristic variable $w^+$
appears both outside and inside the nonlinear function $F$. Consequently, it
cannot be isolated analytically.

After rearranging the previous equation, the boundary state is defined as the
solution of the nonlinear scalar equation

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
R(w^+)
=
w^+
-
G(w^+)
=
0,
}
\label{eq:reed_scalar}
\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation}
G(w^+)
=
-w^-
+
2\dfrac{S^\ast}{cS_L}
\left[
\zeta\ell(y)
F
\left(
\gamma
-
\dfrac{cS^\ast}{S_L}
\dfrac{w^+-w^-}{2k_L}
\right)
+
\varepsilon
\dfrac{\kappa}{\omega_r}\dot y
\right].
\end{equation}

The reed boundary condition therefore differs fundamentally from the radiation
condition. Whereas the latter directly provides the incoming characteristic
variable through an explicit expression, the reed model requires the solution of
a nonlinear equation at every time step.

Fortunately, this problem remains one-dimensional, so that an iterative solver
can be employed with a negligible computational overhead compared with the cost
of advancing the acoustic field.

The numerical strategy adopted to solve Equation~\eqref{eq:reed_scalar}, together
with the complete DG discretization and the time integration procedure, are
presented in the next chapter.

\chapter{Numerical Resolution}
\label{chap:numerical_resolution}

\minitoc

The previous chapter reformulated the governing equations into a conservative
hyperbolic system and expressed both boundary conditions in terms of
characteristic variables. The resulting formulation is particularly well suited
for numerical approximation but still requires the design of an efficient
computational strategy capable of solving the coupled acoustic--mechanical
system.

The numerical solver developed in this work combines several complementary
numerical techniques. The acoustic field is discretized in space using the
Discontinuous Galerkin (DG) method, while the resulting coupled semi-discrete
system is advanced in time using an explicit second-order midpoint
Runge--Kutta scheme. The nonlinear boundary condition at the mouthpiece is
solved iteratively using a damped Newton method.

Rather than considering these components independently, they should be viewed
as different building blocks of a single numerical algorithm. At every Runge--Kutta stage, the acoustic pressure traces are reconstructed
from the current DG state, the physical boundary conditions are reevaluated,
the corresponding ghost states are constructed, and the right-hand sides of
the acoustic, radiation and reed equations are evaluated consistently.

Figure~\ref{fig:solver_pipeline} summarizes the overall numerical strategy
adopted throughout this work.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{solver_pipeline.png}
\caption{Overall organization of the numerical solver.}
\label{fig:solver_pipeline}

\end{figure}

The following sections detail each component of this numerical procedure,
starting with the spatial discretization of the acoustic equations.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\section{Discontinuous Galerkin discretization}

The acoustic propagation is governed by the conservative hyperbolic system

\begin{equation}
\partial_t\tilde{\mathbf u}
+
\partial_x
\left(
A(x)\tilde{\mathbf u}
\right)
=
0,
\label{eq:dg_system}
\end{equation}

where

\[
\tilde{\mathbf u}
=
(\tilde p,\tilde v)^T
\]

contains the conservative variables introduced in the previous chapter.

Among the different numerical methods available for the approximation of
hyperbolic conservation laws, the Discontinuous Galerkin (DG) method has become
one of the most popular approaches. It combines several desirable properties,
including high-order accuracy, local conservation, geometric flexibility and
excellent parallelization capabilities.

Unlike continuous finite element methods, the DG approximation is allowed to be
discontinuous across element interfaces. Information is exchanged only through
numerical fluxes, which naturally handle wave propagation and discontinuities.
\cite{HesthavenWarburton2008}
These properties make the method particularly attractive for acoustic
simulations, where propagating waves and boundary interactions play a central
role.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Weak formulation}

Let

\[
K_j=(x_{j-1},x_j)
\]

denote a generic element of the computational mesh.

Multiplying Equation~\eqref{eq:dg_system} by a vector-valued test function
$\boldsymbol\psi$ and integrating over $K_j$ gives

\begin{equation}
\int_{K_j}
\partial_t\tilde{\mathbf u}
\cdot
\boldsymbol\psi
\,dx
+
\int_{K_j}
\partial_x
(A\tilde{\mathbf u})
\cdot
\boldsymbol\psi
\,dx
=
0.
\end{equation}

The derivative acting on the flux is transferred onto the test function by
integration by parts,

\begin{equation}
\int_{K_j}
\partial_t\tilde{\mathbf u}
\cdot
\boldsymbol\psi
\,dx
-
\int_{K_j}
A\tilde{\mathbf u}
\cdot
\partial_x\boldsymbol\psi
\,dx
+
\left(
A\tilde{\mathbf u}
\cdot
\boldsymbol\psi
\right)
\Big|_{\partial K_j}
=
0.
\label{eq:weak_form}
\end{equation}

The appearance of boundary terms is a fundamental feature of the DG method.
Since the numerical approximation is discontinuous from one element to the
next, the physical flux cannot be evaluated uniquely at the interfaces.
Consequently, it must be replaced by a numerical flux that consistently couples
neighboring elements.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Approximation space}

The computational domain is divided into $N_x$ non-overlapping elements

\[
\Omega
=
\bigcup_{j=1}^{N_x}
K_j.
\]

Within each element, the conservative variables are approximated by local
polynomials of degree one,

\begin{equation}
V_h^1
=
\left\{
u\in L^2(\Omega)
\;\middle|\;
u|_{K_j}\in\mathbb P_1(K_j)
\right\}.
\end{equation}

The numerical solution therefore belongs to the discontinuous finite element
space

\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf u}_h
\in
(V_h^1)^2.
\end{equation}

Using first-order polynomials represents a compromise between numerical
accuracy and computational efficiency. Although higher-order approximations
could easily be considered within the DG framework, the present work focuses on
a $P^1$ discretization, which already provides accurate solutions while keeping
the computational cost moderate.

Inside every element, the solution is expanded onto the local basis functions

\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf u}_h(x,t)
=
\sum_{i=1}^{2}
\mathbf U_i(t)\,
\varphi_i(x),
\end{equation}

where $\varphi_i$ denote the usual linear Lagrange basis functions.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Element matrices}

Substituting the polynomial approximation into the weak formulation
(Equation~\eqref{eq:weak_form}) and choosing the basis functions as test
functions yields a semi-discrete system of ordinary differential equations for
the nodal coefficients.

The first term gives rise to the local mass matrix

\begin{equation}
M_{ij}
=
\int_{K_j}
\varphi_i(x)\varphi_j(x)\,dx.
\end{equation}

Since the conservative variables have removed the geometric coefficients from
the time derivatives, the mass matrix is identical for every element. For a
uniform mesh of size \(h\) and linear basis functions, it is given by

\begin{equation}
M
=
\frac{h}{6}
\begin{pmatrix}
2 & 1\\
1 & 2
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}

This is one of the main advantages of the conservative reformulation presented
in Chapter~\ref{chap:mathematical_reformulation}. Without this change of
variables, each element would possess its own geometry-dependent mass matrix,
leading to a more complex implementation and increased computational cost.

The second integral defines the local stiffness contribution

\begin{equation}
K(\mathbf U)
=
\int_{K_j}
A(x)\tilde{\mathbf u}_h
\cdot
\partial_x\boldsymbol\varphi
\,dx,
\end{equation}

where the flux matrix is evaluated at the quadrature points inside each
element.

Finally, the interface contributions introduce numerical fluxes evaluated at
the two boundaries of every cell. The resulting semi-discrete formulation may
therefore be written as

\begin{equation}
M
\frac{d\mathbf U_j}{dt}
=
K_j(\mathbf U)
-
\widehat{\mathbf F}_{j+\frac12}
+
\widehat{\mathbf F}_{j-\frac12},
\label{eq:semi_discrete}
\end{equation}

where
\(\widehat{\mathbf F}\)
denotes the numerical flux.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Numerical flux}

Because the approximation is discontinuous across element interfaces, the
physical flux cannot be evaluated uniquely at the cell boundaries.

Let

\[
\tilde{\mathbf u}^-
\]

and

\[
\tilde{\mathbf u}^+
\]

denote the left and right traces of the numerical solution at a given
interface.

The numerical flux must satisfy two fundamental properties:

\begin{itemize}
\item consistency with the physical flux,

\item numerical stability.
\end{itemize}

The corresponding physical fluxes are obtained by evaluating the flux function
on each trace:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf F^-
=
\mathbf F\!\left(\tilde{\mathbf u}^-\right),
\qquad
\mathbf F^+
=
\mathbf F\!\left(\tilde{\mathbf u}^+\right).
\end{equation}

More explicitly, since
\begin{equation}
\mathbf F(\tilde{\mathbf u},x)
=
A(x)\tilde{\mathbf u},
\end{equation}
one has
\begin{equation}
\mathbf F^-
=
A(x_{j+\frac12})\tilde{\mathbf u}^-,
\qquad
\mathbf F^+
=
A(x_{j+\frac12})\tilde{\mathbf u}^+.
\end{equation}

In the present work, these requirements are fulfilled by the Rusanov
(Lax--Friedrichs) flux

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\widehat{\mathbf F}
=
\frac12
\left(
\mathbf F^-
+
\mathbf F^+
\right)
-
\frac{s_{\max}}{2}
\left(
\tilde{\mathbf u}^+
-
\tilde{\mathbf u}^-
\right),
}
\label{eq:rusanov_flux}
\end{equation}

where

\[
s_{\max}
=
\max |\lambda|
\]

is the largest propagation speed.

Since the value of the  eigenvalues of the acoustic system,computed in Section~\ref{sec:characteristic_variables} are

\[
\lambda^\pm=\pm c,
\]

the Rusanov parameter simply becomes

\begin{equation}
s_{\max}=c.
\end{equation}

The first term in Equation~\eqref{eq:rusanov_flux} represents the average
physical flux across the interface, whereas the second introduces numerical
dissipation proportional to the jump of the conservative variables.

Although more sophisticated approximate Riemann solvers could be employed, the
Rusanov flux offers an excellent compromise between simplicity, robustness and
accuracy for one-dimensional acoustic wave propagation.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Boundary treatment through characteristic ghost states}
\label{subsec:boundary_ghost_states}

The numerical flux introduced above requires two states at every interface.
Inside the computational domain, these states are naturally provided by the
neighboring elements. At the two extremities of the tube, however, only the
interior DG trace is available. A second state must therefore be constructed
in order to evaluate the numerical flux.

The Discontinuous Galerkin method addresses this difficulty through the
introduction of \emph{ghost states} located outside the computational domain.
Figure~\ref{fig:ghost_cell} illustrates this construction.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1]
        \draw (0,0) rectangle (2,1);
        \draw (2,0) rectangle (4,1);
        \draw (4,0) rectangle (6,1);

        \node at (1,0.5) {Cell $N-1$};
        \node at (3,0.5) {Cell $N$};
        \node at (5,0.5) {Ghost};

        \draw[thick,dashed] (4,-0.3)--(4,1.3);
        \node[below] at (4,-0.3) {Boundary};
    \end{tikzpicture}
    \caption{Construction of a ghost state outside the computational domain.}
    \label{fig:ghost_cell}
\end{figure}

The ghost state is not an additional unknown of the problem. Instead, it is
reconstructed from the physical boundary conditions derived in the previous
chapter.

This is precisely where the characteristic variables introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:characteristic_variables} are used. They are not employed to
evolve the acoustic solution inside the computational domain: the DG
discretization remains entirely formulated in terms of the conservative
variables $\tilde{\mathbf u}$. The characteristic decomposition is only used
locally at the boundaries to distinguish the incoming and outgoing components
of the DG trace.

Let
\begin{equation}
k(x)=\frac{S^\ast}{S(x)}.
\end{equation}
The local characteristic variables are
\begin{equation}
w^\pm
=
\tilde v
\pm
k(x)\tilde p.
\end{equation}
Once $w^+$ and $w^-$ are known, the conservative variables can be recovered
through
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\tilde v
=
\frac{w^+ + w^-}{2},
\qquad
\tilde p
=
\frac{w^+ - w^-}{2k(x)}.
}
\label{eq:characteristic_inverse_boundary}
\end{equation}

At each boundary, the outgoing characteristic is obtained directly from the
interior DG solution, whereas the incoming characteristic is determined by the
corresponding physical boundary condition. The two characteristic components
are then combined using
Equation~\eqref{eq:characteristic_inverse_boundary} to construct the ghost
state required by the numerical flux.


\paragraph{Right boundary: radiation condition.}

At the bell, $x=L$, the interior DG trace
$\tilde{\mathbf u}_h(L^-)$ is projected onto the local characteristic
variables. Since the characteristic associated with the eigenvalue $+c$ is
directed out of the computational domain, the outgoing component
\begin{equation}
w^+_{\mathrm{int}}
=
\tilde v_h(L^-)
+
k_R \tilde p_h(L^-),
\qquad
k_R=\frac{S^\ast}{S(L)},
\end{equation}
is entirely determined by the interior DG solution.

The characteristic $w^-$ is directed into the computational domain and must
therefore be determined from the radiation condition. Denoting this incoming
component by $w^-_{\mathrm{bc}}$, the corresponding ghost state is reconstructed
as
\begin{equation}
\tilde v_{\mathrm{ghost}}
=
\frac{
w^+_{\mathrm{int}}
+
w^-_{\mathrm{bc}}
}{2},
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\tilde p_{\mathrm{ghost}}
=
\frac{
w^+_{\mathrm{int}}
-
w^-_{\mathrm{bc}}
}{
2k_R
}.
\end{equation}

The reconstructed state
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf u}_{\mathrm{ghost}}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\tilde p_{\mathrm{ghost}}\\
\tilde v_{\mathrm{ghost}}
\end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
is then combined with the interior DG trace to evaluate the numerical flux at
the right boundary:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\mathbf F_R^\ast
=
\mathbf F^\ast
\left(
\tilde{\mathbf u}_h(L^-),
\tilde{\mathbf u}_{\mathrm{ghost}}
\right).
}
\end{equation}


\paragraph{Left boundary: reed condition.}

The same principle is applied at the mouthpiece, $x=0$, with the roles of the
two characteristic directions reversed. The characteristic associated with
the eigenvalue $-c$ leaves the computational domain and is therefore determined
by the interior DG trace:
\begin{equation}
w^-_{\mathrm{int}}
=
\tilde v_h(0^+)
-
k_L \tilde p_h(0^+),
\qquad
k_L=\frac{S^\ast}{S(0)}.
\end{equation}

The characteristic $w^+$ enters the acoustic domain and must therefore be
determined from the reed boundary condition. Because this condition couples
the acoustic variables to the nonlinear reed dynamics, the incoming
characteristic cannot be obtained explicitly. Instead,
$w^+_{\mathrm{bc}}$ is computed by solving the nonlinear boundary equation
using the damped Newton method described in
Section~\ref{sec:damped_newton}.

Once $w^+_{\mathrm{bc}}$ has been determined, the ghost state is reconstructed
as
\begin{equation}
\tilde v_{\mathrm{ghost}}
=
\frac{
w^+_{\mathrm{bc}}
+
w^-_{\mathrm{int}}
}{2},
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\tilde p_{\mathrm{ghost}}
=
\frac{
w^+_{\mathrm{bc}}
-
w^-_{\mathrm{int}}
}{
2k_L
}.
\end{equation}

This reconstructed state is then used to evaluate the numerical flux at the
left boundary.


\paragraph{Summary of the boundary treatment.}

The characteristic decomposition therefore acts only as an intermediate step
between the physical boundary conditions and the DG numerical flux:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\begin{array}{ccccc}
\text{DG trace}
& \rightarrow &
\text{characteristics}
& \rightarrow &
\text{BC}
\\[0.5em]
&& \downarrow && \\
&& \text{ghost state}
& \rightarrow &
\text{numerical flux}
\end{array}
}
\end{equation}

Thus, the acoustic field remains expressed and evolved in conservative
variables throughout the DG solver, while the characteristic variables provide
a convenient local representation for imposing the physical boundary
conditions.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\section{Semi-discrete formulation}
\label{sec:semi_discrete_formulation}

The DG discretization presented in the previous section transforms the
continuous acoustic problem into a finite-dimensional system of ordinary
differential equations. At this stage, only the spatial dependence has been
discretized, while time remains continuous.

The DG approximation of the conservative acoustic state is denoted by

\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf u}_h(x,t)
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\tilde p_h(x,t)\\
\tilde v_h(x,t)
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}

This quantity represents the spatially dependent acoustic field reconstructed
from the DG approximation. Its time-dependent coefficients are gathered into
the global vector of DG degrees of freedom

\begin{equation}
\mathbf U(t).
\end{equation}

Therefore, \(\tilde{\mathbf u}_h(x,t)\) denotes the reconstructed acoustic
field, whereas \(\mathbf U(t)\) denotes the finite-dimensional vector
containing all the coefficients required to represent this field.

The semi-discrete acoustic problem can then be written in the abstract form

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\frac{d\mathbf U}{dt}
=
\mathcal L
\left(
\mathbf U,\phi,y,z,\gamma
\right),
}
\label{eq:semi_discrete_system}
\end{equation}

where \(\mathcal L\) denotes the DG spatial operator. It contains the volume
contributions together with the numerical fluxes evaluated at the internal
interfaces and at the physical boundaries.

The dependence of \(\mathcal L\) on the variables
\(\phi\), \(y\), \(z\), and \(\gamma\) results from the boundary conditions.
Indeed, the radiation condition at the bell and the reed condition at the
mouthpiece are used to reconstruct the ghost states required for the
evaluation of the boundary numerical fluxes.

In particular,

\begin{itemize}
    \item at the bell, the radiation variable \(\phi\), together with the
    acoustic trace, determines the incoming characteristic component and
    therefore the corresponding ghost state;

    \item at the mouthpiece, the reed displacement \(y\), its velocity \(z\),
    the blowing pressure \(\gamma\), and the acoustic trace enter the nonlinear
    reed boundary condition used to determine the incoming characteristic
    component.
\end{itemize}

Conversely, the boundary dynamics depend on the acoustic pressure at the two
extremities of the resonator. We denote these pressure traces by

\begin{equation}
p_L(t)=p_h(0^+,t),
\qquad
p_R(t)=p_h(L^-,t),
\label{eq:pressure_traces}
\end{equation}

where \(p_L\) corresponds to the pressure at the mouthpiece and \(p_R\) to the
pressure at the bell.

Since the DG solver evolves the conservative pressure variable

\begin{equation}
\tilde p_h(x,t)
=
\frac{S(x)}{cS^\ast}p_h(x,t),
\end{equation}

the physical pressure traces are recovered according to

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
p_L(t)
=
\frac{cS^\ast}{S(0)}
\tilde p_h(0^+,t),
\qquad
p_R(t)
=
\frac{cS^\ast}{S(L)}
\tilde p_h(L^-,t).
}
\label{eq:physical_pressure_traces}
\end{equation}

Introducing the reed velocity

\begin{equation}
z=\dot y,
\end{equation}

the complete semi-discrete coupled system can therefore be written
schematically as

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{d\mathbf U}{dt}
=
\mathcal L
\left(
\mathbf U,\phi,y,z,\gamma
\right),
\\[0.7em]
\dfrac{d\phi}{dt}
=
G(p_R),
\\[0.7em]
\dfrac{dy}{dt}
=
z,
\\[0.7em]
\dfrac{dz}{dt}
=
K(p_L,y,z,\gamma).
\end{cases}
}
\label{eq:complete_ode_system}
\end{equation}

Here, \(G\) represents the evolution equation associated with the radiation
condition, while \(K\) represents the reed acceleration obtained from the
mechanical oscillator equation. The blowing pressure \(\gamma=\gamma(t)\) is
a prescribed input of the model rather than an additional dynamical variable.

Equation~\eqref{eq:complete_ode_system} highlights the bidirectional coupling
between the acoustic field and the boundary dynamics. The DG acoustic state
provides the pressure traces \(p_L\) and \(p_R\) required by the reed and
radiation equations. Conversely, the variables \(\phi\), \(y\), and \(z\),
together with \(\gamma\), enter the boundary conditions used to construct the
ghost states and therefore modify the DG operator \(\mathcal L\).

The resulting finite-dimensional coupled system is advanced in time using the
explicit midpoint Runge--Kutta method described in the following section.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\section{Time integration}

Once the spatial discretization has been performed, the remaining problem
consists in integrating the coupled system of ordinary differential equations
given by Equation~\eqref{eq:complete_ode_system}.

In the present implementation, the acoustic degrees of freedom, the radiation
variable and the reed variables are advanced together using the explicit
midpoint Runge--Kutta method. This choice provides a uniform second-order
time-integration procedure for the complete semi-discrete system.

An important aspect of the algorithm is that the coupling terms are
reevaluated at both Runge--Kutta stages. In particular, the pressure traces at
the mouthpiece and at the bell are reconstructed from the current DG state
before evaluating the reed dynamics, the radiation equation and the boundary
numerical fluxes.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Runge--Kutta discretization of the coupled system}
\label{subsec:rk2_coupled}

The semi-discrete formulation introduced in
Equation~\eqref{eq:complete_ode_system} couples the acoustic field, the
radiation variable, and the reed dynamics.

The vector \(\mathbf U(t)\) gathers all DG degrees of freedom associated with
the conservative acoustic variables

\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf u}_h
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\tilde p_h\\
\tilde v_h
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}

The complete semi-discrete system can be written schematically as

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{d\mathbf U}{dt}
=
\mathcal L
\left(
\mathbf U,\phi,y,z,\gamma
\right),
\\[0.6em]
\dfrac{d\phi}{dt}
=
G(p_R),
\\[0.6em]
\dfrac{dy}{dt}
=
z,
\\[0.6em]
\dfrac{dz}{dt}
=
K(p_L,y,z,\gamma),
\end{cases}
}
\label{eq:rk2_coupled_system}
\end{equation}

where \(z=\dot y\), and

\begin{equation}
p_L(t)=p(0,t),
\qquad
p_R(t)=p(L,t)
\end{equation}

denote the acoustic pressure traces at the mouthpiece and at the bell,
respectively.

The operator \(\mathcal L\) contains the DG volume contributions and the
numerical fluxes at the internal interfaces and physical boundaries.
Consequently, it depends on the radiation and reed variables through the
boundary ghost states. Conversely, the evolution of the radiation variable
and the reed depends on the acoustic pressure traces \(p_R\) and \(p_L\).
This provides the coupling between the different components of the
semi-discrete system.

The complete system is advanced in time using the explicit midpoint
Runge--Kutta method. The pressure traces and boundary conditions are
reevaluated at each Runge--Kutta stage.

\paragraph{First Runge--Kutta stage.}

Starting from the state
\(
(\mathbf U^n,\phi^n,y^n,z^n)
\)
at time \(t^n\), the physical pressure traces are first reconstructed from
the DG pressure variable.

Since

\begin{equation}
\tilde p
=
\frac{S(x)}{cS^\ast}p,
\end{equation}

the physical pressure is recovered as

\begin{equation}
p
=
\frac{cS^\ast}{S(x)}\tilde p.
\end{equation}

Therefore, at the two boundaries,

\begin{equation}
p_L^n
=
\frac{cS^\ast}{S(0)}
\tilde p_h^n(0^+),
\qquad
p_R^n
=
\frac{cS^\ast}{S(L)}
\tilde p_h^n(L^-).
\label{eq:pressure_stage1}
\end{equation}

The prescribed blowing pressure is evaluated at the same time,

\begin{equation}
\gamma^n=\gamma(t^n).
\end{equation}

The first Runge--Kutta stage is then evaluated as

\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:rk2_stage1}
\begin{align}
k_{1,U}
&=
\mathcal L
\left(
\mathbf U^n,
\phi^n,
y^n,
z^n,
\gamma^n
\right),
\\
k_{1,\phi}
&=
G(p_R^n),
\\
k_{1,y}
&=
z^n,
\\
k_{1,z}
&=
K
\left(
p_L^n,
y^n,
z^n,
\gamma^n
\right).
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

These quantities are used to construct the intermediate state at

\begin{equation}
t^{n+\frac12}
=
t^n+\frac{\Delta t}{2},
\end{equation}

according to

\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:rk2_midpoint}
\begin{align}
\mathbf U^{n+\frac12}
&=
\mathbf U^n
+
\frac{\Delta t}{2}k_{1,U},
\\
\phi^{n+\frac12}
&=
\phi^n
+
\frac{\Delta t}{2}k_{1,\phi},
\\
y^{n+\frac12}
&=
y^n
+
\frac{\Delta t}{2}k_{1,y},
\\
z^{n+\frac12}
&=
z^n
+
\frac{\Delta t}{2}k_{1,z}.
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

\paragraph{Second Runge--Kutta stage.}

The acoustic pressure traces are then recomputed from the intermediate DG
state \(\mathbf U^{n+\frac12}\). This point is important: the pressure used
in the second-stage radiation and reed equations is not the pressure at
\(t^n\), but the pressure obtained from the intermediate acoustic state.

Thus,

\begin{equation}
p_L^{n+\frac12}
=
\frac{cS^\ast}{S(0)}
\tilde p_h^{\,n+\frac12}(0^+),
\qquad
p_R^{n+\frac12}
=
\frac{cS^\ast}{S(L)}
\tilde p_h^{\,n+\frac12}(L^-).
\label{eq:pressure_stage2}
\end{equation}

The blowing pressure is similarly evaluated at the intermediate time,

\begin{equation}
\gamma^{n+\frac12}
=
\gamma
\left(
t^n+\frac{\Delta t}{2}
\right).
\end{equation}

The second Runge--Kutta stage is then evaluated using the complete
intermediate state:

\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:rk2_stage2}
\begin{align}
k_{2,U}
&=
\mathcal L
\left(
\mathbf U^{n+\frac12},
\phi^{n+\frac12},
y^{n+\frac12},
z^{n+\frac12},
\gamma^{n+\frac12}
\right),
\\
k_{2,\phi}
&=
G
\left(
p_R^{n+\frac12}
\right),
\\
k_{2,y}
&=
z^{n+\frac12},
\\
k_{2,z}
&=
K
\left(
p_L^{n+\frac12},
y^{n+\frac12},
z^{n+\frac12},
\gamma^{n+\frac12}
\right).
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

Finally, the solution at time
\(t^{n+1}=t^n+\Delta t\) is obtained from

\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:rk2_final_update}
\begin{align}
\mathbf U^{n+1}
&=
\mathbf U^n
+
\Delta t\,k_{2,U},
\\
\phi^{n+1}
&=
\phi^n
+
\Delta t\,k_{2,\phi},
\\
y^{n+1}
&=
y^n
+
\Delta t\,k_{2,y},
\\
z^{n+1}
&=
z^n
+
\Delta t\,k_{2,z}.
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

At each Runge--Kutta stage, the current acoustic state therefore determines
the pressure traces at the two boundaries. These traces are used to evaluate
the radiation and reed dynamics. Simultaneously, the current values of
\(\phi\), \(y\), and \(z\) are used in the physical boundary conditions to
reconstruct the ghost states required by the DG numerical flux.

In particular, at the mouthpiece, the nonlinear boundary equation is solved
using the damped Newton method described in
Section~\ref{sec:damped_newton}. At the bell, the radiation condition
determines the corresponding incoming characteristic component. The resulting
ghost states are then used in the evaluation of the DG operator
\(\mathcal L\).

Consequently, the second Runge--Kutta stage is evaluated from the complete
intermediate state

\begin{equation}
\left(
\mathbf U^{n+\frac12},
\phi^{n+\frac12},
y^{n+\frac12},
z^{n+\frac12}
\right),
\end{equation}

together with the prescribed value
\(\gamma^{n+\frac12}\). The acoustic field, radiation variable, and reed
dynamics are thus advanced within the same explicit midpoint Runge--Kutta
procedure.



%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\section{Nonlinear solution of the reed boundary condition}
\label{sec:reed_nonlinear_boundary}

At the mouthpiece, however, the reed model defines the incoming
characteristic implicitly through a nonlinear equation. Consequently, a local
nonlinear problem must be solved before the ghost state can be reconstructed.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Implicit nonlinear equation}

The reed boundary condition obtained in the previous chapter can be written as

\begin{equation}
R(w^+)=0,
\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation}
R(w^+)
=
w^+
-
G(w^+).
\end{equation}

Unlike the radiation condition, no analytical expression of the incoming
characteristic variable is available because \(w^+\) appears inside the
nonlinear airflow function.

The computation of the boundary state therefore reduces to solving a scalar
nonlinear equation at every time step.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Newton iteration}

To determine the incoming characteristic variable, we employ Newton's method.

Starting from an initial approximation
\(w_0^+\),
the successive iterates are computed according to

\begin{equation}
w_{k+1}^+
=
w_k^+
-
\frac{R(w_k^+)}
{R'(w_k^+)}.
\end{equation}

Provided that the initial approximation is sufficiently close to the solution,
Newton's method converges quadratically.

In the present problem, an excellent initial guess is naturally provided by the
solution obtained at the previous time step,

\begin{equation}
w_0^+
=
w_{n-1}^+,
\end{equation}

since the acoustic variables evolve continuously in time.

As a result, only a small number of Newton iterations is generally required to
reach convergence.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Damped Newton method}

Although Newton's method exhibits quadratic convergence close to the solution,
its convergence may deteriorate when the initial approximation is farther from
the root.

To improve the robustness of the iterative process, a damping coefficient

\[
0<\theta_N\le1
\]

is introduced.

The Newton correction becomes

\begin{equation}
\Delta w
=
\frac{R(w)}
{R'(w)},
\end{equation}

and the iterative procedure reads

\begin{equation}
w_{k+1}
=
w_k
-
\theta_N
\Delta w.
\end{equation}

In addition, excessively large corrections are avoided by limiting the Newton
increment,

\begin{equation}
\Delta w
=
\mathrm{clip}
\left(
\Delta w,
-\Delta_{\max},
\Delta_{\max}
\right).
\end{equation}

The final update therefore becomes

\begin{equation}
w_{k+1}
=
w_k
-
\theta_N
\,
\mathrm{clip}
\left(
\frac{R(w_k)}
{R'(w_k)},
-\Delta_{\max},
\Delta_{\max}
\right).
\end{equation}

This damping strategy slightly reduces the asymptotic convergence rate but
greatly improves the robustness of the solver, particularly during the initial
transient of the simulation.


%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\section{Stability considerations}

The stability of the numerical solver is primarily governed by the explicit
integration of the acoustic field.

For a DG discretization of polynomial degree \(k\), the time step must satisfy
a Courant--Friedrichs--Lewy (CFL) condition of the form

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\Delta t
\le
C_{\mathrm{CFL}}
\,
\frac{h}
{(2k+1)c},
}
\end{equation}

where \(h\) denotes the element size and \(c\) the speed of sound.

Throughout this work, the value of the CFL number is chosen sufficiently small
to guarantee stable wave propagation while preserving the computational
efficiency of the solver.

The CFL condition controls the stability restriction associated with the
explicit DG--RK2 discretization. Since the complete semi-discrete system,
including the reed and radiation variables, is advanced using the same explicit
midpoint Runge--Kutta method, the chosen time step is applied to all components
of the coupled model.

The CFL restriction derived from the acoustic subsystem provides the time-step
criterion used throughout the simulations. However, satisfying this condition
does not constitute a stability proof for the complete nonlinear coupled
system, since the reed dynamics and nonlinear boundary conditions introduce
additional interactions. In the present work, the behaviour of the complete
numerical scheme is therefore assessed empirically through the numerical
validation experiments presented in Chapter~\ref{chap:validation}.
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\section{Implementation remarks}

Although the numerical method presented above follows the classical
Discontinuous Galerkin framework, several implementation choices were made to
improve both efficiency and robustness.

First, all geometric quantities depending on the instrument shape are computed
only once during the initialization stage. This includes the cross-sectional
areas, the flux matrices and the interpolation coefficients used during the
quadrature procedure. Consequently, the time integration only involves matrix
operations and numerical flux evaluations.

Secondly, the local characteristic decomposition simplifies the treatment of
the physical boundaries. The acoustic field itself remains represented and
evolved in conservative variables. At each boundary, the interior DG trace is
temporarily expressed in characteristic variables, the incoming component is
determined from the physical boundary model, and the corresponding ghost state
is reconstructed before evaluating the Rusanov flux.

Finally, the nonlinear problem associated with the reed boundary remains
computationally inexpensive since it only involves a single scalar unknown.
Compared with the DG discretization of the acoustic field, its computational
cost is therefore negligible.

These implementation choices lead to a compact numerical solver in which the
same DG formulation is employed throughout the computational domain, including
at the physical boundaries.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\section{Discussion}

The numerical strategy proposed in this chapter combines several complementary
methods, each addressing a specific aspect of the coupled acoustic problem.

The Discontinuous Galerkin method provides the spatial approximation of the
acoustic system in conservative variables. The local characteristic
decomposition introduced in the previous chapter is used to impose the physical
boundary conditions through ghost-state reconstruction. The nonlinear reed
boundary condition is solved using a damped Newton method, while the complete
semi-discrete coupled system is advanced in time using the explicit midpoint
Runge--Kutta scheme.

An important feature of this solver is that every component has been designed
with differentiability in mind. The numerical operations involved in the DG
discretization, the reconstruction of the ghost states and the time integration
are all compatible with automatic differentiation. As a consequence, the
complete forward solver can later be embedded into a gradient-based optimization
procedure without requiring any modification of the numerical algorithm itself.

The resulting solver therefore provides not only an accurate numerical
approximation of the physical model but also the computational foundation
required for the inverse calibration framework developed in the second part of
this manuscript.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\section*{Chapter summary}

In this chapter, a complete numerical strategy for solving the coupled
acoustic--mechanical model has been presented.

Starting from the conservative formulation derived previously, the acoustic
equations were discretized in space using the Discontinuous Galerkin method.
The radiation and reed boundary conditions were incorporated through
characteristic variables and ghost-state reconstruction, while the nonlinear reed boundary condition was solved using a damped Newton method
in order to determine the incoming characteristic variable.
Finally, the complete semi-discrete system, including the acoustic field,
radiation variable and reed dynamics, was integrated in time using the
second-order midpoint Runge--Kutta method. The pressure traces and physical
boundary conditions were reevaluated at each Runge--Kutta stage in order to
preserve a consistent treatment of the coupling.

The resulting solver constitutes the forward model used throughout the
remainder of this work. Before addressing the inverse calibration problem, its
accuracy and numerical properties are first assessed through several validation
experiments presented in the next chapter.


\chapter{Validation of the Forward Solver}
\label{chap:validation}

\minitoc

The previous chapters introduced the physical model governing sound generation
in reed wind instruments together with the numerical method developed to solve
the corresponding coupled acoustic--mechanical problem. Before employing this
solver within a gradient-based inverse calibration framework, its numerical
accuracy and robustness must first be carefully assessed.

The validation of the proposed solver is performed progressively through three
complementary stages. First, the Discontinuous Galerkin discretization is
validated on a simplified acoustic configuration for which an analytical
solution is available. The acoustic solver is validated on a simplified configuration in which
the nonlinear reed boundary condition is removed, while the radiation condition
at the bell is kept unchanged. Under these assumptions, an analytical solution
is available and provides an exact reference for assessing the accuracy of the
numerical method.

Once the acoustic solver has been validated, the complete coupled model is
compared with the OpenWind software, which provides a reliable reference for
the numerical simulation of wind instruments. Finally, the computational
performances of the proposed implementation are evaluated in order to assess
its suitability for the repeated forward simulations required by the inverse
calibration procedure developed in the second part of this manuscript.

model. To this end, the acoustic equations are solved on a simplified test

This benchmark serves two complementary purposes. First, it verifies that the
implementation correctly reproduces the exact solution of the continuous
problem. Secondly, it allows the experimental convergence order of the

\subsection{Comparison with a characteristic reference solution}

The first validation consists in comparing the numerical solution obtained

\partial_t w^+

=

-

\label{eq:uniform_characteristics}

The two characteristic components therefore propagate without deformation
along trajectories of velocities \(+c\) and \(-c\), respectively.

For the initial condition considered in the validation experiment, the
incident characteristic is given by

\begin{equation}
w^+(x,t)
=
p_0(x-ct),
\label{eq:exact_wplus}
\end{equation}

while the contribution associated with the initial left-going wave is

\begin{equation}
w^-_{\mathrm{init}}(x,t)
=
p_0(x+ct).
\label{eq:exact_wminus_initial}
\end{equation}

At the right boundary \(x=L\), the radiation condition relates the incoming
and outgoing characteristic components according to

\begin{equation}
w^-(L,t)
=
a\,w^+(L,t)
+
b\,\phi(t),
\label{eq:exact_radiation_characteristic}
\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation}
a
=
\frac{1-\beta/Z}{1+\beta/Z},
\qquad
b
=
\frac{2\sqrt{\alpha}}{1+\beta/Z}.
\end{equation}

The characteristic reaching the bell is directly obtained from the initial
condition,

\begin{equation}
w^+(L,t)
=
p_0(L-ct).
\end{equation}

The radiation variable satisfies the ordinary differential equation

\begin{equation}
\frac{d\phi}{dt}
=
-
\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}}{2Z}(1+a)\,
w^+(L,t)
-
\frac{\alpha}{Z(1+\beta/Z)}
\phi(t).
\label{eq:reference_radiation_ode}
\end{equation}

Once the reflected characteristic \(w^-(L,t)\) has been determined at the
bell, it propagates back into the tube with velocity \(-c\). Its contribution
at position \(x\) and time \(t\) is therefore

\begin{equation}
w^-_{\mathrm{ref}}(x,t)
=
w^-
\left(
L,
t-\frac{L-x}{c}
\right),
\label{eq:reflected_characteristic}
\end{equation}

provided that

\begin{equation}
t-\frac{L-x}{c}\geq0.
\end{equation}

Before the reflected wave reaches the point \(x\), this contribution is zero.

The pressure reference solution is finally reconstructed from the
characteristic components as

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
p_{\mathrm{ref}}(x,t)
=
\frac{1}{2}
\left[
w^+(x,t)
+
w^-_{\mathrm{init}}(x,t)
+
w^-_{\mathrm{ref}}(x,t)
\right].
}
\label{eq:reference_pressure_solution}
\end{equation}

The propagation inside the uniform tube is thus obtained directly from the
characteristic equations. The radiation variable \(\phi\), however, is
integrated numerically in the reference implementation. The resulting
solution should therefore be regarded as a characteristic-based
semi-analytical reference rather than a fully closed-form analytical solution.

Figure~\ref{fig:validation_uniform} compares this reference solution with the
pressure field obtained using the DG solver with Forward Euler and the
second-order midpoint Runge--Kutta scheme.

\subsection{Convergence analysis}
\label{subsec:convergence_analysis}

Beyond the visual agreement observed in the previous section, the numerical
accuracy of the acoustic solver is assessed quantitatively through a convergence
study.

The spatial discretization employed throughout this work is based on a
Discontinuous Galerkin approximation with piecewise linear basis functions
(DG P1). Let \(h\) denote the characteristic mesh size and \(\Delta t\) the
time step. Since the time step is chosen according to a CFL condition, the two
discretization parameters satisfy

\begin{equation}
\Delta t \propto h.
\label{eq:cfl_validation}
\end{equation}

% --------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Semi-discrete problem
% --------------------------------------------------------------------------

It is important to distinguish the spatial and temporal discretization errors.
After applying the DG discretization in space, the continuous acoustic problem
is replaced by a finite-dimensional semi-discrete system. Denoting its solution
by \(\mathbf{u}_h(t)\), the semi-discrete problem can be written schematically as

\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathrm d \mathbf{u}_h}{\mathrm dt}
=
\mathcal{L}_h(\mathbf{u}_h),
\end{equation}

where \(\mathcal{L}_h\) denotes the spatial DG operator.

For a sufficiently regular solution and under the usual consistency and
stability assumptions, a DG approximation using polynomials of degree \(k\)
is expected to exhibit a spatial error of order

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{O}(h^{k+1}).
\end{equation}

Since piecewise linear polynomials are used in the present work
(\(k=1\)), the expected spatial accuracy is therefore

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\mathcal{O}(h^2).
}
\label{eq:space_order}
\end{equation}

The convergence study presented below does not introduce a single combined norm
for the two-component acoustic state. Instead, the pressure and acoustic volume
velocity are evaluated separately, consistently with the numerical quantities
computed by the solver.

% --------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Temporal discretization
% --------------------------------------------------------------------------

The semi-discrete system must then be discretized in time. Let
\(\mathbf{u}_h^n\) denote the fully discrete approximation of
\(\mathbf{u}_h(t_n)\) at time \(t_n=n\Delta t\).

For the Forward Euler method, the temporal discretization is first-order
accurate, so that, for a fixed spatial discretization,

\begin{equation}
\left\|
\mathbf{u}_h(t_n)-\mathbf{u}_h^n
\right\|
=
\mathcal{O}(\Delta t).
\end{equation}

Combining the expected spatial and temporal contributions gives, formally,

\begin{equation}
\text{error}
=
\mathcal{O}(h^2)
+
\mathcal{O}(\Delta t).
\end{equation}

Since the CFL condition imposes \(\Delta t \propto h\), the temporal error
dominates asymptotically and the expected overall convergence rate becomes

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\text{error}_{\mathrm{Euler}}
=
\mathcal{O}(h).
}
\end{equation}

Thus, although the DG P1 spatial approximation is expected to be second-order
accurate, the overall convergence rate is limited to first order when Forward
Euler time integration is employed.

For the second-order Runge--Kutta method (RK2), the temporal discretization
satisfies

\begin{equation}
\left\|
\mathbf{u}_h(t_n)-\mathbf{u}_h^n
\right\|
=
\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2).
\end{equation}

The expected total error therefore behaves as

\begin{equation}
\text{error}
=
\mathcal{O}(h^2)
+
\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2).
\end{equation}

Using again \(\Delta t \propto h\), both contributions are of the same
asymptotic order, leading to

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\text{error}_{\mathrm{RK2}}
=
\mathcal{O}(h^2).
}
\end{equation}

The RK2 scheme is therefore expected to preserve the second-order accuracy of
the DG P1 spatial discretization.

% --------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Definition of the measured errors
% --------------------------------------------------------------------------

To verify these expected convergence rates, numerical simulations are performed
for a sequence of increasingly refined meshes and compared with the
characteristic-based reference solution introduced in the previous section.
This reference configuration consists of a uniform bore equipped with the
radiation boundary condition at \(x=L\).

The convergence is assessed separately for the acoustic pressure \(p\) and the
acoustic volume velocity \(v\). At a fixed comparison time \(T\), the pressure
error is defined as

\begin{equation}
E_p(h,T)
=
\left(
\int_0^L
\left|
p_h(x,T)-p_{\mathrm{ex}}(x,T)
\right|^2
\,\mathrm dx
\right)^{1/2},
\label{eq:error_pressure}
\end{equation}

while the acoustic volume velocity error is

\begin{equation}
E_v(h,T)
=
\left(
\int_0^L
\left|
v_h(x,T)-v_{\mathrm{ex}}(x,T)
\right|^2
\,\mathrm dx
\right)^{1/2}.
\label{eq:error_velocity}
\end{equation}

Here, \(p_h\) and \(v_h\) denote the reconstructed DG solutions, while
\(p_{\mathrm{ex}}\) and \(v_{\mathrm{ex}}\) denote the corresponding semi-analytical
solutions.

In practice, both solutions are evaluated on the same uniform reconstruction
grid

\begin{equation}
x_j=j\Delta x,
\qquad
j=0,\ldots,N_{\mathrm{plot}}-1,
\end{equation}

and the continuous \(L^2\)-norms are approximated numerically by

\begin{equation}
E_p(h,T)
\approx
\left(
\Delta x
\sum_{j=0}^{N_{\mathrm{plot}}-1}
\left|
p_h(x_j,T)-p_{\mathrm{ex}}(x_j,T)
\right|^2
\right)^{1/2},
\label{eq:discrete_error_pressure}
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
E_v(h,T)
\approx
\left(
\Delta x
\sum_{j=0}^{N_{\mathrm{plot}}-1}
\left|
v_h(x_j,T)-v_{\mathrm{ex}}(x_j,T)
\right|^2
\right)^{1/2}.
\label{eq:discrete_error_velocity}
\end{equation}

These are absolute \(L^2\)-errors rather than relative errors. The two variables
are deliberately considered separately, avoiding the introduction of an
arbitrary scaling or weighting between pressure and acoustic volume velocity.

% --------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Estimation of the convergence order
% --------------------------------------------------------------------------

For a uniform mesh of \(N\) cells,

\begin{equation}
h=\frac{L}{N}.
\end{equation}

If an error behaves asymptotically as

\begin{equation}
E(h)\simeq C h^r,
\end{equation}

then, equivalently,

\begin{equation}
E(N)\simeq C' N^{-r}.
\end{equation}

Taking the logarithm gives

\begin{equation}
\log E(N)
\simeq
\log C' - r\log N.
\end{equation}

The experimental convergence order can therefore be estimated from the slope
of the error curve in logarithmic scale. In the present implementation, a
linear regression of \(\log E\) against \(\log N\) is performed independently
for the pressure and acoustic volume velocity, yielding two estimated orders,

\begin{equation}
r_p
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
r_v.
\end{equation}

Figure~\ref{fig:validation_convergence} presents the resulting convergence
curves for both temporal integration schemes.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.70\linewidth]
    {../../experiments/convergence/results_exact/convergence_exact_euler_rk2.png}
    \caption{
    Convergence of the pressure and acoustic volume velocity errors for the
    DG P1 discretization using Forward Euler and RK2 time integration.
    The errors are evaluated with respect to the semi-analytical reference solution using
    discrete approximations of the spatial \(L^2\)-norm.
    }
    \label{fig:validation_convergence}
\end{figure}

The numerical results are consistent with the expected behaviour of the
space--time discretization. With Forward Euler time integration, the
first-order temporal error limits the overall convergence rate despite the
second-order spatial approximation. With RK2 time integration, both the spatial
and temporal contributions are expected to be second-order under the CFL
scaling, allowing the overall method to recover a second-order convergence
rate.

The fact that the expected convergence behaviour is observed independently for
both pressure and acoustic volume velocity provides an important validation of
the acoustic solver in this simplified configuration. In particular, it
supports the consistency of the DG spatial discretization, the numerical flux
implementation, the treatment of the radiation boundary condition at \(x=L\),
and the time integration schemes.

This validation is restricted to the uniform-bore configuration with the
radiation boundary condition imposed at \(x=L\), for which the
characteristic-based reference solution introduced in the previous section is
available. In particular, the test validates the acoustic propagation and its
treatment at the radiating end. It does not, however, constitute a general
convergence proof for the complete nonlinear reed--acoustic coupling considered
later in this work.

\section{Validation of the complete coupled solver}

The previous section validated the acoustic solver by comparing the numerical
solution with an analytical reference solution. This first benchmark confirmed
the correctness of the Discontinuous Galerkin discretization, the Runge--Kutta
time integration and the implementation of the radiation boundary condition.

The only component that has not yet been validated is the nonlinear reed
boundary condition. Once the reed dynamics are coupled with the acoustic
equations, the governing system becomes nonlinear and no closed-form analytical
solution is available.

To assess the accuracy of the complete forward solver, the numerical solution
is therefore compared with OpenWind, an open-source software dedicated to the
simulation of wind instruments. OpenWind \cite{OpenwindInria} implements the same physical model and
is widely used as a reference solver in computational musical acoustics. The
objective of this section is to verify that the proposed DG solver converges
towards the same physical solution as OpenWind for realistic instrument
configurations.

\subsection{Quantitative comparison}
\label{subsec:quantitative_openwind}

Beyond the visual agreement observed in the previous figures, the similarity
between the DG and OpenWind pressure signals is quantified using both
time-domain and time--frequency indicators.

\paragraph{Time-domain comparison.}

The pressure signals obtained with the DG solver and OpenWind are first aligned
using the time shift that maximizes their normalized cross-correlation. The
maximum correlation coefficient provides a measure of the similarity between
the temporal evolutions of the two signals.

After this alignment, the relative \(L^2\)-error is computed as

\begin{equation}
E_{\mathrm{rel}}
=
\frac{
\left\|
p_{\mathrm{DG}}-p_{\mathrm{OW}}
\right\|_2
}{
\left\|
p_{\mathrm{OW}}
\right\|_2
},
\label{eq:openwind_relative_error}
\end{equation}

where \(p_{\mathrm{DG}}\) and \(p_{\mathrm{OW}}\) denote the aligned pressure
signals obtained with the DG solver and OpenWind, respectively.

\paragraph{Time--frequency comparison.}

The comparison is also performed in the time--frequency domain using the
magnitude spectrograms of the pressure signals. The Short-Time Fourier
Transform (STFT) is computed using an FFT size of \(512\) samples and a hop
size of \(64\) samples.

Let

\begin{equation}
M_{\mathrm{DG}}(m,k)
=
\left|
\operatorname{STFT}
\left(
p_{\mathrm{DG}}
\right)(m,k)
\right|,
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
M_{\mathrm{OW}}(m,k)
=
\left|
\operatorname{STFT}
\left(
p_{\mathrm{OW}}
\right)(m,k)
\right|
\end{equation}

denote the corresponding magnitude spectrograms, where \(m\) and \(k\)
represent the time-frame and frequency-bin indices, respectively.

To preserve the relative amplitude between the two representations, both
spectrograms are normalized using the same reference value, namely the maximum
magnitude of the OpenWind spectrogram:

\begin{equation}
M_{\max}
=
\max_{m,k}
M_{\mathrm{OW}}(m,k),
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
\widetilde M_{\mathrm{DG}}
=
\frac{M_{\mathrm{DG}}}{M_{\max}},
\qquad
\widetilde M_{\mathrm{OW}}
=
\frac{M_{\mathrm{OW}}}{M_{\max}}.
\end{equation}

The first spectral indicator is the \emph{Spectral Convergence} (SC), defined
as

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\mathrm{SC}
=
\frac{
\left\|
\widetilde M_{\mathrm{DG}}
-
\widetilde M_{\mathrm{OW}}
\right\|_F
}{
\left\|
\widetilde M_{\mathrm{OW}}
\right\|_F
},
}
\label{eq:spectral_convergence_openwind}
\end{equation}

where \(\|\cdot\|_F\) denotes the Frobenius norm. This quantity measures the
global discrepancy between the two magnitude spectrograms. It is equal to zero
for identical spectrograms and increases as their global spectral distributions
differ.

A complementary comparison is performed on a logarithmic scale using the
\emph{Log Spectral Distance} (LSD). The normalized magnitude spectrograms are
first converted to decibels according to

\begin{equation}
S_{\mathrm{DG}}^{\mathrm{dB}}(m,k)
=
20\log_{10}
\left(
\widetilde M_{\mathrm{DG}}(m,k)+\varepsilon
\right),
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
S_{\mathrm{OW}}^{\mathrm{dB}}(m,k)
=
20\log_{10}
\left(
\widetilde M_{\mathrm{OW}}(m,k)+\varepsilon
\right),
\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation}
\varepsilon
=
10^{-80/20}
=
10^{-4}
\end{equation}

introduces a spectral floor at approximately \(-80\,\mathrm{dB}\) relative
to the maximum magnitude. This prevents numerical values close to zero from
producing arbitrarily large logarithmic discrepancies, while retaining
low-amplitude spectral components in the comparison. The \(80\,\mathrm{dB}\)
dynamic range is therefore a numerical choice rather than a parameter of the
physical model.

The Log Spectral Distance is then defined as

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\mathrm{LSD}
=
\sqrt{
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{m,k}
\left[
S_{\mathrm{DG}}^{\mathrm{dB}}(m,k)
-
S_{\mathrm{OW}}^{\mathrm{dB}}(m,k)
\right]^2
},
}
\label{eq:log_spectral_distance_openwind}
\end{equation}

where \(N\) denotes the total number of time--frequency bins.

The two spectral indicators provide complementary information. Spectral
Convergence measures the overall discrepancy between the magnitude
spectrograms on a linear scale and is therefore mainly influenced by dominant
spectral components. In contrast, the logarithmic representation used in the
LSD increases the relative importance of lower-amplitude components and
harmonics.

indicators obtained for the uniform and exponential geometries.

\subsection{Convergence towards the OpenWind solution}

Although no analytical solution is available for the complete coupled problem,
the convergence of the DG solver can still be assessed by comparing it with the
OpenWind solution while refining the spatial discretization.

Let \(u\) denote the exact solution of the continuous problem,
\(u_h^{DG}\) the DG approximation and
\(u_\eta^{OW}\) the OpenWind solution.

Assuming that both numerical methods are second-order accurate,

\begin{equation}
\|u_h^{DG}-u\|
\le
C_{DG}h^2,
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
\|u_\eta^{OW}-u\|
\le
C_{OW}\eta^2.
\end{equation}

Introducing the exact solution and applying the triangle inequality gives

\begin{equation}
\|u_h^{DG}-u_\eta^{OW}\|
\le
C_{DG}h^2
+
C_{OW}\eta^2.
\end{equation}

If both discretizations are refined simultaneously such that

\[
\eta\propto h,
\]

one obtains

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\|u_h^{DG}-u_\eta^{OW}\|
=
\mathcal O(h^2).
}
\end{equation}

The convergence curves obtained experimentally are presented in
Figure~\ref{fig:convergence_compare_shift_rk2}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering

The measured convergence rate is fully consistent with the theoretical
prediction. For both the cylindrical and the exponential geometries, the
difference between the two numerical solutions decreases quadratically as the
mesh is refined.

Since the DG implementation and OpenWind rely on independent numerical
frameworks, this agreement provides strong evidence that both solvers converge
towards the same continuous solution. Consequently, the proposed DG solver can
be considered as an accurate implementation of the complete physical model.

\subsection{Computational performance of the forward solver}
\label{subsec:forward_performance}

In addition to the numerical validation of the DG solver, its computational
performance is investigated as a function of the spatial discretization.
This analysis is particularly relevant in the context of the inverse problem,
since the forward model is evaluated repeatedly during the calibration
procedure. The computational cost of a single simulation therefore directly
affects the overall cost of the optimization.

The performance study is carried out for both the constant and exponential
geometries introduced previously. Two explicit time-integration schemes are
considered: the first-order Euler scheme and the second-order Runge--Kutta
(RK2) scheme. For each configuration, the number of DG cells $N$ is
progressively increased while the physical simulation duration and the CFL
number are kept fixed.

To reduce the influence of measurement fluctuations, the reported execution
times correspond to median wall-clock times obtained from repeated evaluations
of the compiled solver. In particular, the JAX compilation time is excluded
from these measurements. The resulting performance measurements are presented
in Figure~\ref{fig:forward_performance}.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]
    {../../experiments/performance/results/forward_performance_summary.png}
    \caption{
    Computational performance of the complete DG forward solver for constant
    and exponential geometries using Euler and RK2 time integration.
    Top: median wall-clock time required for a complete forward simulation.
    Middle: number of physical time steps imposed by the CFL condition.
    Bottom: median computational cost per physical time step.
    The exponents reported in the legends are obtained from log--log linear
    regressions over the investigated range of spatial resolutions.
    }
    \label{fig:forward_performance}
\end{figure}


\paragraph{Overall computational scaling.}

The total execution time increases regularly with the number of DG cells.
Over the investigated range, the measured wall-clock time can be approximated
by a power law

\begin{equation}
    T_{\mathrm{forward}}(N)
    \propto N^{\alpha},
\end{equation}

where $\alpha$ denotes an empirical scaling exponent. The measured values are

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_{\mathrm{Euler,const}} &\simeq 1.09,
&
\alpha_{\mathrm{Euler,exp}} &\simeq 1.11,
\\
\alpha_{\mathrm{RK2,const}} &\simeq 1.12,
&
\alpha_{\mathrm{RK2,exp}} &\simeq 1.10.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The four exponents are therefore very similar and indicate an approximately
$N^{1.1}$ increase of the measured execution time over the considered range.
These values should be interpreted as empirical wall-clock scaling exponents
rather than as an asymptotic complexity estimate, since they also depend on
the implementation, compilation strategy and hardware characteristics.


\paragraph{Influence of the CFL condition.}

The evolution of the total execution time can be understood by separating the
number of physical time steps from the computational cost of each step.

For an explicit discretization of the acoustic propagation problem, the time
step is constrained by the CFL stability condition. For a fixed physical
domain and a uniform mesh,

\begin{equation}
    \Delta x \propto \frac{1}{N}.
\end{equation}

Since the CFL number is kept constant throughout the experiment, the admissible
time step consequently satisfies

\begin{equation}
    \Delta t \propto \Delta x
    \propto \frac{1}{N}.
\end{equation}

For a fixed physical simulation duration $T$, the corresponding number of time
steps is therefore

\begin{equation}
    N_t
    =
    \frac{T}{\Delta t}
    \propto N.
\end{equation}

This behavior is directly observed in the middle panel of
Figure~\ref{fig:forward_performance}, where the measured number of time steps
closely follows the linear $N$ reference. Consequently, increasing the spatial
resolution not only increases the number of degrees of freedom, but also
requires a proportionally larger number of temporal iterations.


\paragraph{Cost of a physical time step.}

The bottom panel of Figure~\ref{fig:forward_performance} isolates the median
wall-clock time associated with one physical time step. Its variation with
$N$ is substantially weaker than that of the total simulation time. A
log--log fit gives approximately

\begin{equation}
    T_{\mathrm{step}}
    \propto N^{\alpha_{\mathrm{step}}},
\end{equation}

with empirical exponents between approximately $0.09$ and $0.12$ for the
different configurations.

Combining this observation with the CFL-induced scaling
$N_t\propto N$ gives

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
T_{\mathrm{forward}}
&=
N_t T_{\mathrm{step}}
\\
&\propto
N\,N^{\alpha_{\mathrm{step}}}
\\
&\approx
N^{1.1},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

which is consistent with the scaling measured directly for the complete
forward simulations.

The non-monotonic variations observed for the smallest and intermediate mesh
sizes in the cost per time step should not be interpreted as a numerical
property of the DG method. At these resolutions, the execution of individual
steps is very short, such that fixed runtime overheads, memory effects and
hardware utilization can represent a significant fraction of the measured
wall-clock time. For the largest meshes, the increase becomes more regular.


\paragraph{Influence of the geometry.}

An important observation is that the constant and exponential geometries
produce almost identical execution times for both integration schemes.
The spatial variation of the cross-sectional area therefore introduces no
significant additional computational cost in the present implementation.

This result is expected from the structure of the numerical method: changing
the geometry modifies the local coefficients entering the DG formulation but
does not alter the number of degrees of freedom or the overall sequence of
operations performed during one time step. The computational cost is thus
primarily controlled by the mesh resolution and the time-integration scheme
rather than by the considered geometry.


\paragraph{Euler and RK2 integration.}

Although Euler and RK2 exhibit similar empirical scaling exponents, their
absolute computational costs differ significantly. The RK2 scheme requires
additional evaluations of the semi-discrete system during each physical time
step and is consequently more expensive.

The performance results therefore highlight an important distinction between
scaling and absolute execution time. RK2 does not exhibit a less favorable
dependence on the number of cells: both schemes scale similarly with $N$.
Instead, RK2 introduces a larger multiplicative cost per physical time step,
which results in a higher total execution time for a given spatial
discretization.

This additional computational cost must be considered together with the
improved temporal accuracy provided by the second-order scheme. In the
remainder of this work, RK2 is retained when the increased accuracy is
required despite its larger computational cost.


\paragraph{Implications for the differentiable solver.}

These measurements characterize the computational cost of the forward model
independently of the differentiation procedure. This distinction becomes
particularly important for the inverse problem. Each optimization iteration
requires the evaluation of the forward numerical model together with the
propagation of sensitivities of the objective function with respect to the
parameters.

In the differentiable implementation considered in this work, these
sensitivities are obtained by reverse-mode automatic differentiation through
the complete discrete solver. The computational cost of the inverse problem
therefore contains two distinct contributions: the forward simulation and the
backward sensitivity propagation.

The present analysis provides a baseline for the first contribution. The
computational overhead associated with reverse-mode automatic differentiation
can subsequently be quantified by comparing the forward and backward execution
times as the spatial resolution increases.

\section{Discussion}

The numerical experiments presented throughout this chapter assess the
proposed forward solver from three complementary perspectives: numerical
accuracy, agreement with an independent reference implementation, and
computational performance.

The first benchmark, based on an analytical solution, confirms the correctness
of the spatial and temporal discretizations. In particular, the expected
first- and second-order convergence rates are recovered for the Forward Euler
and Runge--Kutta schemes, respectively. This validates the implementation of
the Discontinuous Galerkin formulation as well as the treatment of the
radiation boundary condition.

The second benchmark addresses the complete reed--resonator system, for which
no analytical solution is available. The excellent agreement observed with
OpenWind, both qualitatively and quantitatively, demonstrates that the proposed
solver accurately reproduces the coupled acoustic and mechanical dynamics of
the instrument. This comparison is particularly important since it validates
the complete numerical model, including the nonlinear reed interaction and the
radiation condition at the bell.

Beyond numerical accuracy, the performance study provides information about
the computational cost of the complete forward model. Over the investigated
range of spatial resolutions, the measured wall-clock time exhibits an
approximately $N^{1.1}$ empirical scaling for both the constant and
exponential geometries and for both time-integration schemes. The similarity
between the results obtained for the two geometries indicates that the
spatially varying cross-section does not introduce a significant additional
computational overhead in the present implementation.

The decomposition of the execution time also provides an interpretation of
this scaling. Because of the CFL stability condition, the number of physical
time steps increases linearly with the number of DG cells,
\[
N_t \propto N.
\]
The measured cost of an individual time step varies much more slowly with the
spatial resolution over the investigated range. The observed increase in total
execution time is therefore primarily driven by the larger number of time
steps required when the mesh is refined.

The Euler and RK2 schemes exhibit similar empirical scaling with respect to
$N$, but substantially different absolute computational costs. RK2 requires
additional evaluations of the semi-discrete system at each physical time step
and is consequently more expensive. This additional cost represents the
computational counterpart of the improved temporal accuracy obtained with the
second-order integration scheme.

Taken together, these results show that the proposed forward solver is both an
accurate numerical approximation of the physical model and computationally
well characterized over the range of resolutions considered in this work.
These two aspects are essential before addressing the inverse problem.
Indeed, parameter identification requires repeated evaluations of the forward
model, so that both numerical errors and computational cost directly affect
the calibration procedure.

The forward performance measurements also provide a useful baseline for the
differentiable solver considered in the following chapter. In that setting,
each optimization step involves not only the evaluation of the forward model
but also the backward propagation of sensitivities through the numerical
solver. Separating these two contributions will make it possible to quantify
the additional computational cost introduced by reverse-mode automatic
differentiation.


\section*{Chapter conclusion}

This chapter validated and characterized the forward solver developed in the
previous chapters through a series of complementary numerical experiments.

The comparison with an analytical solution confirmed the theoretical
convergence properties of the DG discretization on a simplified acoustic
problem, while the comparison with OpenWind demonstrated the accuracy of the
complete coupled reed--resonator model. These experiments establish that the
solver correctly reproduces both the underlying wave propagation and the
nonlinear interaction between the reed and the resonator.

The computational study complemented this numerical validation by
characterizing the execution cost of the complete solver. For both constant
and exponential geometries, an empirical scaling close to $N^{1.1}$ was
observed over the investigated range of spatial resolutions. This behavior is
mainly explained by the CFL condition, which imposes a number of physical time
steps proportional to the number of DG cells. The comparison between Euler
and RK2 further showed that the choice of time integrator primarily affects
the absolute cost per time step rather than the observed scaling with spatial
resolution.

The forward solver is therefore sufficiently validated and characterized to
serve as the numerical core of the inverse problem considered in the remainder
of this manuscript. The following chapter introduces the differentiable
calibration framework, including the formulation of the objective function,
automatic differentiation of the discrete solver, and its relation to
discrete adjoint methods. Particular attention will also be given to the
computational cost of the backward differentiation step relative to the
forward simulation.

\chapter{Differentiable Inverse Calibration}
\label{chap:inverse}

\minitoc

The previous chapters focused on the construction and validation of the
forward numerical solver for the simulation of reed wind instruments.
The present chapter introduces its use within a differentiable inverse
calibration framework, in which gradients of an objective function with
respect to the model parameters are computed through the numerical solver.

In practical applications, several quantities governing sound production are
not known with sufficient accuracy. The geometry of the resonator may be
available, whereas parameters associated with the excitation mechanism remain
effective quantities that are difficult to measure directly. The available
information is then an acoustic signal, either generated artificially with
OpenWind or obtained from experimental recordings supplied by Modartt.

The resulting inverse problem is generally nonlinear and non-convex because
the simulated solution depends nonlinearly on the parameters to be identified.
In the present model, this difficulty is further increased by the nonlinear
reed--acoustic coupling: parameter variations may modify not only the signal
amplitude but also the oscillation regime and the sounding frequency.
Moreover, different parameter combinations may produce similar acoustic
responses, leading to possible identifiability issues.

The proposed methodology is not restricted to the estimation of scalar physical
parameters. It also allows an unknown constitutive law, namely the reed opening
function \(\ell(y)\), to be represented by a differentiable parametric model and
learned from acoustic observations. The inverse problem may therefore combine
signal-dependent physical parameters with a function shared by the complete
dataset.

This chapter introduces the mathematical formulation of the inverse problem,
the losses used to compare acoustic signals, the differentiation of the complete
numerical solver and the gradient-based optimization strategy. The final
sections describe the representation and regularization of the reed opening law,
together with the datasets and experimental protocols used in the numerical
study.

The complete inverse calibration workflow is summarized in
Figure~\ref{fig:inverse_framework}. The unknown physical parameters and the
parameters defining the reed opening law are supplied to the differentiable
forward solver. The simulated pressure is compared with the observation through
an objective function. Automatic differentiation then propagates the loss
gradient through the complete simulation, and a gradient-based optimizer updates
the unknown quantities before the next forward evaluation.

\section{Formulation of the inverse problem}
\label{sec:inverse_formulation}

\subsection{Parameter identification}

Let
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\theta}
=
(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_{N_\theta})
\end{equation}
denote the vector collecting the physical parameters treated as unknown.
Depending on the experiment, this vector may contain a subset of
\begin{equation}
\left(
\gamma,\zeta,\kappa,\omega_r,Q_r
\right).
\end{equation}

For a fixed geometry, prescribed initial conditions and a given simulation
horizon, the numerical solver defines a discrete forward operator
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{F}_h:
\boldsymbol{\theta}
\longmapsto
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{sim}}
=
\left(
p_{\mathrm{sim}}(L,t_0),\ldots,
p_{\mathrm{sim}}(L,t_{N-1})
\right),
\label{eq:forward_map}
\end{equation}
where \(h\) collectively denotes the spatial and temporal discretization
parameters.

Given an observed pressure signal $\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{obs}}=\left(p_{\mathrm{obs}}(t_0),\ldots,p_{\mathrm{obs}}(t_{N-1})\right),$ the parameter-identification problem is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
\in
\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta}
\mathcal{L}
\left(
\mathcal{F}_h(\boldsymbol{\theta}),
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{obs}}
\right),
}
\label{eq:inverse_problem}
\end{equation}
where \(\Theta\) is the admissible parameter set and \(\mathcal{L}\) is a
discrepancy measure.

Two types of observations are considered:
\begin{itemize}
    \item synthetic signals generated with OpenWind, for which the reference
    parameters are known;
    \item experimental recordings provided by Modartt, for which the physical
    parameters must be inferred from measured sound.
\end{itemize}

Synthetic data make it possible to quantify reconstruction errors and evaluate
the robustness of the method. Experimental data constitute the final
application, but also introduce measurement noise, synchronization uncertainty
and model discrepancy.

\subsection{Identification of the reed law}

The reed-flow condition contains a constitutive function \(\ell(y)\) describing
the effective reed opening. In the synthetic reference model, this function is
prescribed by
\begin{equation}
\ell_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)
=
\max(y,0).
\label{eq:reference_reed_law}
\end{equation}

For the identification problem, the reed law is represented by a differentiable
parametric model
\begin{equation}
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(y),
\end{equation}
where
\(\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_\eta}\)
denotes the vector of trainable parameters defining the function.

For fixed physical parameters, the discrete forward operator can therefore be
viewed as a function of the reed-law parameters,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\mathcal{F}_h :
\boldsymbol{\eta}
\longmapsto
\mathcal{F}_h(\boldsymbol{\eta})
=
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{sim}}.
}
\label{eq:forward_operator_reed_law}
\end{equation}

The identification of the reed law is then formulated as the inverse problem
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
\in
\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathcal{E}}
\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\eta}),
}
\label{eq:reed_law_inverse_problem}
\end{equation}
where
\(\mathcal{E}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N_\eta}\)
denotes the admissible set of trainable parameters defining the reed law.

The objective function is written as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\eta})
=
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{obs}}(\boldsymbol{\eta})
+
\lambda_{\ell}
\mathcal{R}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\eta}),
\label{eq:reed_law_objective_general}
\end{equation}
where \(\lambda_\ell\geq0\) is a regularization weight controlling the
trade-off between agreement with the acoustic observations and prior
constraints imposed on the learned reed law.

Here, \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{obs}}\) measures the discrepancy between the
simulated and observed pressure signals, while \(\mathcal{R}_{\ell}\)
introduces prior information on the shape of the identified reed law.   

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\section{Objective functions}
\label{sec:objective_functions}

The objective function determines which discrepancies are emphasized during
calibration and directly shapes the optimization landscape. No single distance
is optimal for every experiment. This work therefore considers time-domain,
spectral and hybrid objectives.

\subsection{Time-domain discrepancy}

The most direct comparison is the mean squared error
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MSE}}
=
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}
\left(
p_{\mathrm{sim}}[n]
-
p_{\mathrm{obs}}[n]
\right)^2.
\label{eq:mse_loss}
\end{equation}

The MSE is inexpensive and preserves phase information. It is useful when the
signals are accurately synchronized and when the waveform itself is the
quantity of interest. However, a small phase shift or a slight difference in
oscillation frequency may produce a large pointwise error even when the two
sounds have similar harmonic structures.

In addition, the objective of acoustic calibration is not necessarily to
reproduce each pressure sample exactly, but also to recover signals with
similar perceptually relevant acoustic features. In particular, the sounding
frequency, harmonic structure and their evolution over time play an important
role in the perceived similarity between musical sounds. This motivates the
introduction of time--frequency representations in addition to purely
pointwise time-domain discrepancies.

\subsection{Short-Time Fourier Transform}

The Fourier transform provides a global frequency representation but does not
retain the temporal evolution of the spectrum. Musical sounds are
non-stationary: the attack, transient regime and established oscillation may
have different spectral contents.

The Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) applies a Fourier transform to
successive overlapping windows. As presented in \cite{allen1977stft} Given a discrete signal
\(x=(x_0,\ldots,x_{N-1})\), the STFT is defined by
\begin{equation}
X(m,k)
=
\sum_{n=0}^{L-1}
x[n+mH]\,
w[n]\,
e^{-2\mathrm{i}\pi kn/L},
\label{eq:stft}
\end{equation}
where \(L\) is the window length, \(H\) is the hop size, \(w\) is the analysis
window, \(m\) is the frame index and \(k\) is the frequency bin.

For each time frame, the STFT produces a local spectrum. The complete transform
is a complex-valued matrix
\begin{equation}
X\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times K}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Spectrogram representation}

The STFT coefficients contain both magnitude and phase information. In the
spectral objective considered in this work, only the magnitude is retained.
For a signal \(x\), its magnitude spectrogram is defined by

\begin{equation}
S_x(m,k)
=
\left|
\operatorname{STFT}(x)(m,k)
\right|.
\label{eq:spectrogram}
\end{equation}

Each column of \(S_x\) describes the frequency content of one temporal frame,
whereas each row represents the temporal evolution of a given frequency bin.
The STFT is computed using a Hann analysis window, with a prescribed window
length and hop size.

For visualization, the magnitude spectrogram is first normalized by its maximum
value,

\begin{equation}
\widetilde{S}_x(m,k)
=
\frac{
S_x(m,k)
}{
\displaystyle \max_{m,k} S_x(m,k) + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{norm}}
},
\label{eq:normalized_spectrogram}
\end{equation}

where \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{norm}}>0\) prevents division by zero. It is then
expressed in decibels as

\begin{equation}
S_{x,\mathrm{dB}}(m,k)
=
20\log_{10}
\left(
\widetilde{S}_x(m,k)
+
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{dB}}
\right).
\label{eq:log_spectrogram}
\end{equation}

The value of \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{dB}}\) is determined from a prescribed
dynamic range \(D\), expressed in decibels:

\begin{equation}
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{dB}}
=
10^{-D/20}.
\label{eq:dynamic_range_epsilon}
\end{equation}

In the experiments, \(D=60\,\mathrm{dB}\) unless stated otherwise. This lower
bound prevents the logarithm from becoming singular and limits the influence
of spectral components whose amplitudes lie far below the dominant components.
The logarithmic representation is also motivated by perceptual considerations:
human sensitivity to changes in sound amplitude is not linear, and expressing
spectral magnitudes in decibels provides a representation more closely related
to the relative perception of sound levels than a purely linear magnitude
scale.

The spectrogram also provides a representation that emphasizes acoustically
relevant features such as the fundamental frequency, harmonic structure,
transient phenomena, and resonance patterns. These features contribute
strongly to the perceived similarity between musical sounds. Consequently,
comparing signals in the time--frequency domain is motivated not only by
numerical considerations, but also by the objective of recovering signals
with similar perceptually relevant spectral characteristics.

Since phase is discarded, the magnitude spectrogram is less sensitive than a
pointwise time-domain discrepancy to small temporal or phase shifts. However,
the spectral discrepancy used here should not be interpreted as a complete
perceptual metric: perceptual similarity depends on phenomena that are not
fully captured by STFT magnitudes alone. Moreover, the optimization properties
of the resulting objective remain strongly dependent on the analysis window,
the time--frequency resolution, the magnitude compression, and the distance
used to compare the spectrograms.

\subsection{Single-resolution spectral discrepancy}
\label{subsec:single_resolution_spectral_loss}

The spectral objective used in this work follows the general framework of
multi-scale spectral losses commonly used for audio-signal comparison
\cite{schwaer2023multi}. These approaches compare magnitude spectrograms
and may combine different time--frequency resolutions, magnitude
compressions, and distances between spectral representations. In particular,
the use of logarithmic magnitude compression and $\ell^1$-type discrepancies
has been investigated as a way of accounting for spectral components spanning
a large dynamic range \cite{schwaer2023multi}.

For the \(i\)-th STFT configuration, characterized by the window length \(L_i\)
and hop size \(H_i\), the observed and simulated magnitude spectrograms are
defined as

\begin{equation}
S_i^{\mathrm{obs}}
=
\left|
\operatorname{STFT}_i
\left(
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{obs}}
\right)
\right|,
\qquad
S_i^{\mathrm{sim}}
=
\left|
\operatorname{STFT}_i
\left(
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{sim}}
\right)
\right|.
\label{eq:target_predicted_spectrograms}
\end{equation}

Both spectrograms are normalized using a common scale computed exclusively
from the observed signal:

\begin{equation}
a_i
=
\max_{m,k}
S_i^{\mathrm{obs}}(m,k)
+
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{norm}},
\label{eq:spectral_normalization_scale}
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
\widetilde{S}_i^{\mathrm{obs}}
=
\frac{S_i^{\mathrm{obs}}}{a_i},
\qquad
\widetilde{S}_i^{\mathrm{sim}}
=
\frac{S_i^{\mathrm{sim}}}{a_i}.
\label{eq:normalized_target_predicted_spectrograms}
\end{equation}

Using a common normalization factor preserves the relative amplitude
difference between the simulated and observed signals. Moreover, since the
normalization factor depends only on the observation, it remains independent
of the parameters being optimized and prevents the simulated signal from
independently rescaling its spectral representation during calibration.

The first component of the spectral objective compares the normalized
magnitudes on a linear scale:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{lin}}^{(i)}
=
\frac{1}{N_i}
\sum_{m,k}
\left|
\widetilde{S}_i^{\mathrm{sim}}(m,k)
-
\widetilde{S}_i^{\mathrm{obs}}(m,k)
\right|,
\label{eq:magnitude_loss}
\end{equation}

where \(N_i\) denotes the total number of time--frequency coefficients at the
\(i\)-th resolution.

This linear representation gives a relatively large contribution to spectral
components with high magnitude. Consequently, dominant components such as the
fundamental frequency and the strongest harmonics play an important role in
this term.

A complementary comparison is performed using logarithmically compressed
magnitudes. Such compression is commonly employed in spectral audio losses to
reduce the dynamic range of the representation and increase the relative
importance of lower-amplitude components \cite{schwaer2023multi}. It is
also motivated by perceptual considerations, since differences in sound level
are more naturally represented on a logarithmic than on a purely linear
amplitude scale.

The normalized spectrograms are therefore expressed in decibels as

\begin{align}
D_i^{\mathrm{sim}}(m,k)
&=
20\log_{10}
\left(
\widetilde{S}_i^{\mathrm{sim}}(m,k)
+
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{dB}}
\right),
\\
D_i^{\mathrm{obs}}(m,k)
&=
20\log_{10}
\left(
\widetilde{S}_i^{\mathrm{obs}}(m,k)
+
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{dB}}
\right),
\end{align}

where \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{dB}}\) introduces the spectral floor defined in
the previous section. The corresponding discrepancy is

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dB}}^{(i)}
=
\frac{1}{N_i}
\sum_{m,k}
\left|
D_i^{\mathrm{sim}}(m,k)
-
D_i^{\mathrm{obs}}(m,k)
\right|.
\label{eq:db_magnitude_loss}
\end{equation}

The two terms therefore provide complementary information. The linear
magnitude discrepancy gives greater importance to dominant spectral
components, whereas logarithmic compression reduces the spectral dynamic range
and increases the relative contribution of weaker harmonics and low-energy
structures. The latter may be particularly relevant when comparing musical
signals, for which components of substantially different amplitudes can
contribute to the perceived spectral structure.

Following the general principles of multi-scale spectral objectives
\cite{schwaer2023multi}, the particular spectral discrepancy adopted in
this work combines these two representations:

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{spec}}^{(i)}
=
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{lin}}^{(i)}
+
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dB}}^{(i)}.
}
\label{eq:single_resolution_spectral_loss}
\end{equation}

This particular combination is adopted for the present calibration problem in
order to retain sensitivity to both dominant and lower-amplitude spectral
components. It should nevertheless not be interpreted as a complete
perceptual metric: the objective only compares STFT magnitudes and therefore
does not account for all aspects of auditory perception.

Finally, both contributions are defined as mean absolute discrepancies.
Consequently, their magnitude does not increase simply because a given STFT
configuration contains a larger number of time--frequency coefficients. This
normalization also facilitates the subsequent combination of several STFT
resolutions.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Multi-resolution spectral loss}

The discrepancy defined above depends on the choice of a single STFT
configuration. However, a single analysis window cannot simultaneously provide
fine temporal localization and fine frequency resolution because of the
intrinsic time--frequency trade-off of the STFT. Following the multi-scale
spectral loss framework \cite{schwaer2023multi}, several STFT configurations
are therefore combined.

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MR}}
=
\frac{1}{M}
\sum_{i=1}^{M}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{spec}}^{(i)},
}
\label{eq:mrstft_loss}
\end{equation}

where \(M\) denotes the number of retained STFT resolutions.

The generic set of configurations implemented in the loss is

\begin{equation}
(L_i,H_i)
\in
\left\{
(64,32),
(128,64),
(256,128)
\right\}.
\label{eq:default_stft_configurations}
\end{equation}

Only configurations satisfying \(L_i\leq N\), where \(N\) is the signal length,
are retained when zero-padding is disabled. Alternatively, shorter signals may
be zero-padded to allow the use of larger analysis windows.

Depending on the experiment, a different subset of resolutions may be supplied
to the loss. In particular, the calibration experiments may use larger windows,
such as

\begin{equation}
(L_i,H_i)
\in
\left\{
(64,16),
(128,32),
(256,64),
(512,128)
\right\},
\label{eq:experimental_stft_configurations}
\end{equation}

when the signal duration is sufficient.

The shortest windows provide better temporal localization and are therefore
more sensitive to rapid variations and attack transients. Conversely, the
longest windows offer a finer frequency resolution and better distinguish nearby
harmonic components. Averaging the loss over several resolutions forces the
simulated signal to reproduce the observation at multiple time--frequency
scales simultaneously.

A spectral objective is not necessarily easier to optimize than a time-domain
objective. Parameters that directly or indirectly control the sounding
frequency may generate irregular and highly non-convex spectral-loss
landscapes. The STFT resolutions, the Hann window, the dynamic range and the
relative weighting of the linear and decibel terms must therefore be regarded
as integral components of the optimization method rather than as purely
signal-processing choices.

\section{Differentiable forward model}
\label{sec:differentiable_forward_model}

The calibration procedure repeatedly evaluates the direct solver and computes
the derivative of the loss with respect to the unknown quantities. The numerical
model must therefore be differentiable as a complete algorithm.

\subsection{Discrete forward operator}

Let \(\mathbf{U}^n\) denote the complete discrete state at time \(t^n\). It
contains the DG degrees of freedom for pressure and acoustic volume velocity,
the radiation variable, and the variables describing the reed motion.

One time step may be written abstractly as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{U}^{n+1}
=
\Phi_{\Delta t}
\left(
\mathbf{U}^n;
\boldsymbol{\theta},
\boldsymbol{\eta}
\right),
\label{eq:time_step_operator}
\end{equation}
where \(\Phi_{\Delta t}\) includes the evaluation of the DG residual, the
boundary states and the time-integration formulas.

The complete computation is the composition
\begin{equation}
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\eta})
\longrightarrow
\mathbf{U}^0
\longrightarrow
\mathbf{U}^1
\longrightarrow
\cdots
\longrightarrow
\mathbf{U}^{N}
\longrightarrow
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{sim}}
\longrightarrow
\mathcal{J}.
\label{eq:computational_graph}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Differentiability of the numerical components}

The DG spatial discretization, numerical fluxes and Runge--Kutta updates are
compositions of elementary algebraic operations. They are differentiable with
respect to both the discrete state and the physical parameters wherever the
underlying constitutive relations are differentiable.

For one residual evaluation
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{K}^n
=
\mathcal{R}
\left(
\mathbf{U}^n;
\boldsymbol{\theta},
\boldsymbol{\eta}
\right),
\end{equation}
the sensitivity to a parameter \(\theta_j\) formally satisfies
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \mathbf{K}^n}{\partial\theta_j}
=
\frac{\partial\mathcal{R}}{\partial\mathbf{U}}
\frac{\partial\mathbf{U}^n}{\partial\theta_j}
+
\frac{\partial\mathcal{R}}{\partial\theta_j}.
\label{eq:stage_sensitivity}
\end{equation}

In the implementation, these relations are not assembled by hand. JAX records
the elementary operations defining the solver and automatically applies the
chain rule through the complete computational graph.

\subsection{Nonlinear reed boundary condition}

The main difficulty is the nonlinear boundary relation at the reed. At each
time step, the boundary state must satisfy an algebraic equation involving the
incoming characteristic variable, the reed displacement, the pressure
difference and the nonlinear flow function
\begin{equation}
F(\Delta p)
=
\operatorname{sign}(\Delta p)
\sqrt{|\Delta p|}.
\end{equation}

The reed displacement is updated through the time discretization of the reed
oscillator equation. Separately, the nonlinear algebraic boundary relation is
solved with a damped Newton method. If \(z\) denotes the nonlinear boundary
unknown, one iteration reads
\begin{equation}
z^{(q+1)}
=
z^{(q)}
-
\lambda_q
\frac{
G(z^{(q)};\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\eta})
}{
\partial_z G(z^{(q)};\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\eta})
},
\label{eq:newton_iteration_diff}
\end{equation}
where \(\lambda_q\in(0,1]\) is a damping factor.

Because the Newton iterations are implemented with JAX-compatible operations,
the differentiated algorithm propagates derivatives through the finite sequence
of iterations.

Near $\Delta p=0$, the derivative of the original square-root law becomes
singular. To obtain a smooth function suitable for both the nonlinear solver
and automatic differentiation, the flow function itself is regularized in the
numerical implementation as

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
F_{\varepsilon_F}(\Delta p)
=
\frac{\Delta p}
{\left(\Delta p^2+\varepsilon_F^2\right)^{1/4}}
}
\label{eq:regularized_flow_function}
\end{equation}

with $\varepsilon_F=10^{-8}$.

For $|\Delta p|\gg\varepsilon_F$, this regularized expression approaches the
original flow law,

\begin{equation}
F_{\varepsilon_F}(\Delta p)
\simeq
\operatorname{sign}(\Delta p)\sqrt{|\Delta p|}.
\end{equation}

Unlike the original expression, $F_{\varepsilon_F}$ is smooth at
$\Delta p=0$, with
\begin{equation}
F_{\varepsilon_F}(0)=0.
\end{equation}

The regularization therefore removes the non-differentiability at zero while
only modifying the flow law in a small neighbourhood of
$\Delta p=0$.

The value $\varepsilon_F=10^{-8}$ is chosen sufficiently small so that the
regularization only affects the flow law in a very narrow neighbourhood of
$\Delta p=0$. Indeed, for $|\Delta p|\gg\varepsilon_F$,
\[
\frac{F_{\varepsilon_F}(\Delta p)}{F(\Delta p)}
=
\left(
1+\frac{\varepsilon_F^2}{\Delta p^2}
\right)^{-1/4}
\simeq 1.
\]
Consequently, the regularization removes the non-differentiability at zero
while leaving the original pressure-flow relation essentially unchanged away
from this neighbourhood.

A second non-smooth term appears in the reference reed-opening law,
\begin{equation}
\ell_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)=\max(y,0),
\end{equation}
which is not differentiable at \(y=0\).

In the synthetic experiments, however, this expression is used to define the
reference model and generate the target observations. No gradient is propagated
through this reference signal generation. Consequently, the non-differentiability
of \(\ell_{\mathrm{ref}}\) at zero does not affect the inverse optimization.

When the reed-opening law is learned, it is instead represented by the
parametric model \(\ell_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\) introduced previously. In the
present implementation, this model is a multilayer perceptron with hyperbolic
tangent activation functions and is therefore differentiable with respect to
both its input \(y\) and its trainable parameters
\(\boldsymbol{\eta}\).


\subsection{Differentiation through the nonlinear boundary solver}

The nonlinear reed boundary condition is solved numerically using the damped
Newton method described above. Denoting the boundary unknown by \(w\), the
nonlinear equation takes the form
\begin{equation}
G(w;\boldsymbol{\theta})=0,
\end{equation}
where \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) denotes the parameters involved in the boundary
condition.

In the present implementation, the sensitivity of the boundary state is not
computed by applying implicit differentiation to the converged nonlinear
equation. Instead, the Newton solver itself is included in the differentiable
computational graph.

For a prescribed number \(N_{\mathrm{Newton}}\) of iterations, the boundary
state is obtained through the sequence
\begin{equation}
w^{(0)}
\longrightarrow
w^{(1)}
\longrightarrow
\cdots
\longrightarrow
w^{(N_{\mathrm{Newton}})},
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
w^{(q+1)}
=
w^{(q)}
-
\lambda_q
\frac{
G(w^{(q)};\boldsymbol{\theta})
}{
\partial_w G(w^{(q)};\boldsymbol{\theta})
}.
\end{equation}

The final numerical boundary state is therefore
\begin{equation}
w_{\mathrm{num}}
=
w^{(N_{\mathrm{Newton}})}.
\end{equation}

Since each Newton update is implemented using differentiable JAX operations,
automatic differentiation is applied to this finite sequence of numerical
operations. Consequently, the derivative
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial w_{\mathrm{num}}}
     {\partial \boldsymbol{\theta}}
\end{equation}
is obtained by differentiating through the successive Newton iterations.



\subsection{Automatic differentiation}

Automatic differentiation evaluates derivatives of the implemented numerical
algorithm by systematically applying the chain rule to its elementary
operations \cite{griewank2008evaluating}. It differs both from
symbolic differentiation and from finite differences.

The scalar objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{J}
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\eta})
=
\mathcal{L}
\left(
\mathcal{F}_h
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\eta}),
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{obs}}
\right).
\end{equation}

The required gradients are
\begin{equation}
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{J},
\qquad
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\mathcal{J}.
\end{equation}

By the chain rule,
\begin{equation}
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{J}
=
\left(
\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_h}
     {\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}
\right)^{\!\top}
\nabla_{\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{sim}}}
\mathcal{L},
\label{eq:chain_rule_parameters}
\end{equation}
and similarly
\begin{equation}
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\mathcal{J}
=
\left(
\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_h}
     {\partial\boldsymbol{\eta}}
\right)^{\!\top}
\nabla_{\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{sim}}}
\mathcal{L}.
\label{eq:chain_rule_law}
\end{equation}

The Jacobians of the full solver are not constructed explicitly.
Reverse-mode automatic differentiation directly evaluates the required
vector--Jacobian products \cite{jax_autodiff}.

\subsection{Relation with adjoint methods}
\label{subsec:autodiff_adjoint}

The reverse-mode automatic differentiation procedure used here is closely
related to adjoint methods classically employed in PDE-constrained
optimization \cite{griewank2008evaluating}.

To illustrate this connection, consider the discrete forward problem written
abstractly as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{R}_h
    \left(
        \mathbf{U},
        \boldsymbol{\theta}
    \right)
    =
    \mathbf{0},
    \label{eq:discrete_forward_residual}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{U}$ denotes the complete discrete state and
$\boldsymbol{\theta}$ the parameters to be identified. The objective function
is written as
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{J}
    =
    \mathcal{J}
    \left(
        \mathbf{U},
        \boldsymbol{\theta}
    \right).
\end{equation}

A direct sensitivity approach would require computing the variation of the
state with respect to each parameter. When the number of parameters becomes
large, this can become computationally expensive. Adjoint methods instead
introduce an auxiliary variable, the adjoint state, allowing the gradient of
a scalar objective to be evaluated without explicitly constructing the full
state--parameter Jacobian.

Two related approaches can be distinguished. In the continuous-adjoint
approach, the adjoint equations are first derived from the continuous
PDE--ODE system and are subsequently discretized. This corresponds to a
``differentiate-then-discretize'' strategy.

Alternatively, one may first discretize the governing equations and then
derive the adjoint of the resulting discrete numerical system. This leads to
a discrete adjoint and corresponds to a
``discretize-then-differentiate'' strategy.

The automatic differentiation approach adopted in this work belongs to the
second category. The complete numerical solver, including the DG spatial
discretization, time integration, boundary conditions, and nonlinear Newton
iterations, defines a discrete computational graph. Reverse-mode automatic
differentiation traverses this graph backward and applies the transposed
linearized operations required to propagate the sensitivity of the scalar
objective \cite{griewank2008evaluating}.

Consequently, reverse-mode automatic differentiation is closely related to
the discrete adjoint approach: both rely on a backward propagation of
sensitivities through the discretized numerical model. In the present
implementation, this propagation is generated automatically from the
operations composing the forward solver, so that no explicit adjoint
equations need to be derived or implemented manually.

This distinction is important in the present work. The gradients obtained by
automatic differentiation correspond to the discretized objective
implemented in the code, rather than to an independently discretized
continuous adjoint problem. Therefore, the differentiation procedure remains
consistent with the numerical choices made in the forward solver.

\subsection{Computational cost of reverse-mode differentiation}
\label{subsec:backward_performance}

The relation between reverse-mode automatic differentiation and discrete
adjoint methods explains how gradients are propagated through the numerical
solver. From a practical perspective, however, the applicability of such an
approach to parameter identification also depends on the computational
overhead introduced by the backward pass. This aspect is particularly
important in the present framework, since each optimization iteration requires
both the evaluation of the physical model and the computation of its
sensitivities with respect to the parameters.

To quantify this additional cost, the computational performance of
reverse-mode automatic differentiation was evaluated using the complete
differentiable solver with the second-order Runge--Kutta time integration
scheme. The same constant and exponential geometries considered in the
forward-performance study were used, and the number of DG cells was varied
from $N=50$ to $N=400$.

For this benchmark, three computational times are distinguished. The forward
time $T_F$ corresponds to the evaluation of the objective function through one
complete simulation. The backward time $T_B$ corresponds to the reverse-mode
pullback alone, once the information generated during the forward evaluation
is available. Finally, $T_{F+B}$ denotes the complete cost of evaluating both
the objective function and its gradient. The latter is therefore the most
representative quantity for the actual cost of one gradient evaluation during
the calibration procedure.

The benchmark focuses on differentiation through the physical solver itself.
A simple normalized temporal mean-square error is therefore used as the
objective function,

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
    =
    \frac{
        \left\|
        p_h(\boldsymbol{\theta}) - p_{\mathrm{ref}}
        \right\|_2^2
    }{
        \left\|
        p_{\mathrm{ref}}
        \right\|_2^2
    },
\end{equation}

where $p_h(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ denotes the pressure signal produced by the
differentiable solver and $p_{\mathrm{ref}}$ the corresponding reference
signal. This choice avoids including the additional computational cost of the
multi-scale spectral transforms and therefore isolates more directly the cost
associated with differentiating the numerical solver.

The first panel of Figure~\ref{fig:backward_performance} shows that the two
geometries exhibit very similar computational behavior. This result is
consistent with the forward-performance analysis: introducing a spatially
varying cross-section does not significantly modify the computational
structure of the solver or of its differentiated counterpart.

Over the investigated range of spatial resolutions, the forward evaluation
follows an empirical power law

\begin{equation}
    T_F \propto N^{\alpha_F},
\end{equation}

with fitted exponents

\begin{equation}
    \alpha_F^{\mathrm{const}} = 1.18,
    \qquad
    \alpha_F^{\mathrm{exp}} = 1.21.
\end{equation}

These values are consistent with the scaling previously observed for the
direct solver over a comparable range of resolutions.

The isolated reverse-mode pullback exhibits a stronger dependence on the
number of cells,

\begin{equation}

\end{equation}

with

\begin{equation}
    \alpha_B^{\mathrm{const}} = 1.90,
    \qquad
    \alpha_B^{\mathrm{exp}} = 1.92.
\end{equation}

The complete forward--backward evaluation exhibits intermediate empirical
exponents,

\begin{equation}
    \alpha_{F+B}^{\mathrm{const}} = 1.39,
    \qquad
    \alpha_{F+B}^{\mathrm{exp}} = 1.34.
\end{equation}

These exponents should not be interpreted as asymptotic complexity estimates.
They correspond to empirical power-law fits over the finite range of mesh
sizes investigated here. In particular, the different operations do not
necessarily lie in the same computational regime over the complete interval.
The measurements nevertheless provide a useful characterization of the
practical scaling of the implemented differentiable solver.

\paragraph{Relative cost of the backward pass.}

For gradient-based calibration, the absolute execution time is less
informative than the additional cost introduced by differentiation. Two
relative quantities are therefore considered,

\begin{equation}
    r_B
    =
    \frac{T_B}{T_F},
    \qquad
    r_{\mathrm{AD}}
    =
    \frac{T_{F+B}}{T_F}.
\end{equation}

The middle panel of Figure~\ref{fig:backward_performance} shows that the
isolated backward pass is initially cheaper than the forward evaluation. For
the smallest meshes, $T_B/T_F$ is approximately $0.35$. Its relative cost
increases progressively with the spatial resolution and reaches approximately
$1.3$--$1.4$ for $N=400$.

More importantly, the total cost of obtaining both the objective and its
gradient remains limited throughout the investigated range. The ratio

\begin{equation}
    \frac{T_{F+B}}{T_F}
\end{equation}

lies approximately between $1.8$ and $2.8$. Consequently, one complete
objective-and-gradient evaluation requires only a few times the cost of a
single forward evaluation for the configurations considered here.

This result is particularly relevant for the inverse problem. Reverse-mode
automatic differentiation computes the gradient of a scalar objective with
respect to all differentiated parameters during a single backward
propagation. The computational cost is therefore not proportional to the
number of calibrated parameters in the same way as a parameter-by-parameter
finite-difference sensitivity analysis would be.

\paragraph{Cost per physical time step.}

The total execution time combines two effects: the number of physical time
steps required by the CFL condition and the computational cost of processing
each time step. To separate these contributions, the bottom panel of
Figure~\ref{fig:backward_performance} reports the execution times normalized
by the number of physical time steps.

For the forward evaluation, the cost per time step remains of the order of a
few tens of microseconds over the investigated meshes. The backward pass
exhibits a stronger increase for the largest resolutions, reaching values
larger than those of the forward pass. As a consequence, the complete
forward--backward cost per time step also increases with $N$, from
approximately $40$--$50~\mu\mathrm{s}$ for the smallest meshes to nearly
$100~\mu\mathrm{s}$ for $N=400$.

This behavior provides additional insight into the relatively large empirical
exponents obtained for the isolated backward pass. Its increasing total cost
does not result solely from the larger number of CFL-constrained time steps:
the computational work associated with the reverse propagation itself also
increases with the spatial resolution.

\paragraph{Implications for parameter calibration.}

Overall, these measurements show that reverse-mode automatic differentiation
introduces a significant but manageable computational overhead. For the
largest configurations considered here, the backward propagation becomes
slightly more expensive than the forward evaluation itself. Nevertheless, the
complete objective-and-gradient evaluation remains below approximately three
times the cost of a forward evaluation.

This property is particularly advantageous for the calibration framework
developed in this work. Once the forward solver has been expressed as a
differentiable numerical program, gradients with respect to several physical
parameters can be obtained simultaneously without deriving and implementing
the corresponding discrete adjoint equations manually. The measured
computational overhead therefore remains compatible with iterative
gradient-based optimization.

Together with the relation established in
Section~\ref{subsec:autodiff_adjoint}, these results show that reverse-mode
automatic differentiation provides both a convenient implementation of
discrete-adjoint sensitivity propagation and a computationally viable tool for
the inverse problems considered in the following chapters.

An important limitation of reverse-mode differentiation nevertheless concerns
memory usage. During the forward evaluation, intermediate quantities required
for the subsequent backward propagation must either be retained or recomputed.
For a time-dependent numerical solver, the associated memory requirements may
therefore increase substantially with the number of time steps and spatial
degrees of freedom. Long simulation windows and fine discretizations can thus
become expensive not only in terms of execution time, but also in terms of
memory consumption.

This limitation is particularly relevant when the complete time integration is
differentiated directly, as in the present implementation. Techniques such as
checkpointing could reduce the memory footprint by trading additional forward
computations for reduced storage. Such strategies are not investigated in the
present work, but constitute a possible improvement for extending the
calibration framework to longer simulations or finer spatial discretizations.

\section{Gradient-based optimization}
\label{sec:gradient_optimization}

\subsection{Optimization variables}

The framework distinguishes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item local physical parameters, which may vary from one signal to another;
    \item global parameters \(\boldsymbol{\eta}\) defining the shared reed law.
\end{itemize}

For a dataset of \(N_s\) signals, the local variables are
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(s)},
\qquad
s=1,\ldots,N_s,
\end{equation}
whereas a single vector \(\boldsymbol{\eta}\) is shared by all signals.

The dataset objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{data}}
=
\frac{1}{N_s}
\sum_{s=1}^{N_s}
\mathcal{L}_p
\left(
\mathcal{F}_h
(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(s)},\boldsymbol{\eta}),
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{obs}}^{(s)}
\right)
+
\lambda_\ell
\mathcal{R}_\ell(\boldsymbol{\eta}).
\label{eq:dataset_objective}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Multi-start initialization}

Because the inverse problem is non-convex, the final estimate may depend
strongly on the initialization of the optimization algorithm. A single
initial guess may therefore lead the optimizer toward an unsatisfactory
local minimum. To reduce this sensitivity, a multi-start strategy is used
before the main optimization stage.

Let
\[
\left\{
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0,r}
\right\}_{r=1}^{N_{\mathrm{start}}}
\]
denote a prescribed set of initial parameter vectors. For each starting
point \(r\), a short Adam optimization is performed,
\[
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{r}^{(s)}
=
\operatorname{Adam}_{N_{\mathrm{MS}}}
\left(
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0,r};
\mathcal{J}^{(s)}
\right),
\]
where \(N_{\mathrm{MS}}\) denotes the number of exploratory optimization
iterations and \(\mathcal{J}^{(s)}\) is the objective function associated
with signal \(s\).

After all starting points have been explored, the candidate yielding the
smallest individual loss is retained for each signal:
\[
r_s^\star
=
\arg\min_{1\leq r\leq N_{\mathrm{start}}}
\mathcal{J}^{(s)}
\left(
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{r}^{(s)}
\right),
\]
and the selected estimate is
\[
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{selected}}^{(s)}
=
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{r_s^\star}^{(s)}.
\]

Hence, although the different signals are processed simultaneously in a
vectorized implementation, the selection of the initial basin of attraction
is performed independently for each signal. Different signals may therefore
retain candidates originating from different starting points. The selected
parameter vectors are then used as initial conditions for the main Adam
optimization.

After this first optimization stage, an additional targeted multi-start
procedure can be applied to signals that remain poorly fitted. Let
\(\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{th}}\) denote a prescribed loss threshold. A signal
\(s\) is considered difficult when
\[
\mathcal{J}^{(s)}
\left(
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{current}}^{(s)}
\right)
>
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{th}}.
\]

For these signals, new starting points are generated locally around the
current estimate:
\[
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0,r}^{(s)}
=
\operatorname{clip}
\left(
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{current}}^{(s)}
+
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r},
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\min},
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\max}
\right),
\]
where \(\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}\) denotes a prescribed perturbation of the
physical parameters, while
\(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\min}\) and
\(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\max}\) define admissible parameter bounds.

Each of these local starting points is again optimized for a small number
of Adam iterations. The candidate giving the lowest individual loss is
retained and may subsequently undergo a low-learning-rate refinement.
Importantly, the new estimate replaces the previous one only if it decreases
the objective function:
\[
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{new}}^{(s)}
=
\begin{cases}
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(s)},
&
\mathcal{J}^{(s)}
\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(s)}\right)
<
\mathcal{J}^{(s)}
\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{current}}^{(s)}\right),
\\[1ex]
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{current}}^{(s)},
&
\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]

This two-level strategy combines a global exploration of several prescribed
initializations with a local re-exploration of difficult cases. Its purpose
is not to guarantee convergence toward the global minimum, but to reduce
the dependence of the calibration on a single initialization while limiting
the additional computational cost.


\subsection{Initialization and local minima}

The objective is non-convex and may contain several attraction basins.
Initialization therefore has a major influence on the recovered parameters.

For low-dimensional calibration, a multi-start strategy consists in:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item generating several admissible initial parameter vectors;
    \item performing a short optimization from each vector;
    \item ranking the candidates according to their final loss;
    \item continuing the optimization from the best candidates.
\end{enumerate}

For poorly calibrated signals, additional initializations can be generated
relative to the best current estimate rather than repeating the same global
grid. This concentrates the computational effort on difficult cases.

\section{Learning the reed opening law}
\label{sec:learning_reed_law}

\subsection{Neural representation}

The function \(\ell(y)\) is represented by a small multilayer perceptron
\begin{equation}
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}:
\mathbb{R}
\longrightarrow
\mathbb{R}.
\end{equation}

The architecture used in the experiments contains one scalar input, two hidden
layers and one scalar output:
\begin{equation}
1
\longrightarrow
8
\longrightarrow
8
\longrightarrow
1.
\label{eq:lfunc_architecture}
\end{equation}
Hyperbolic tangent activation functions are used in the hidden layers.

\subsection{Anchoring and scale ambiguity}

The product
\begin{equation}
\zeta\,\ell(y)
\end{equation}
appears in the reed-flow boundary condition. Simultaneous optimization of
\(\zeta\) and \(\ell\) therefore introduces an approximate scale ambiguity.

To reduce this ambiguity, the law is anchored at a reference displacement:
\begin{equation}
\ell(1)=1.
\label{eq:lfunc_anchor}
\end{equation}

The associated penalty is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{anchor}}
=
\left(
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(1)-1
\right)^2.
\label{eq:anchor_regularization}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Positivity and shape regularization}

The reed opening should remain non-negative in the physically relevant regime.
A positivity penalty is introduced:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{pos}}
=
\frac{1}{N_y}
\sum_{j=1}^{N_y}
\left[
\max
\left(
-\ell_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(y_j),0
\right)
\right]^2.
\label{eq:positivity_regularization}
\end{equation}

A monotonicity prior may additionally be imposed:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{mono}}
=
\frac{1}{N_y}
\sum_{j=1}^{N_y}
\left[
\max
\left(
-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y}
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(y_j),0
\right)
\right]^2.
\label{eq:monotonicity_regularization}
\end{equation}

The complete regularization is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{R}_\ell
=
\lambda_{\mathrm{anchor}}
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{anchor}}
+
\lambda_{\mathrm{pos}}
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{pos}}
+
\lambda_{\mathrm{mono}}
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{mono}}.
\label{eq:lfunc_regularization}
\end{equation}

where
\(\lambda_{\mathrm{anchor}}\),
\(\lambda_{\mathrm{pos}}\), and
\(\lambda_{\mathrm{mono}}\)
are non-negative hyperparameters controlling the relative contribution of the
anchoring, positivity, and monotonicity constraints, respectively.

\subsection{Identifiability from acoustic observations}

The reed displacement is not observed in the experimental setting. The
available data consist only of pressure recordings. Learning \(\ell(y)\) is
therefore indirect: the law is inferred through its influence on reed flow,
acoustic propagation and pressure at the bell.

A single signal explores only a limited range of reed displacements and cannot
identify the complete law over a broad interval. Multiple signals with
different excitation parameters are therefore required.

\section{Conclusion}

This chapter formulated the calibration of reed-instrument models as a
differentiable inverse problem. The unknown quantities may consist of scalar
physical parameters, a constitutive reed-opening law, or both.

Time-domain and multi-resolution spectral losses were introduced to compare
simulated and observed acoustic signals. The complete DG--ODE solver was then
interpreted as a discrete computational graph and differentiated with
reverse-mode automatic differentiation in JAX.

The optimization framework distinguishes local parameters associated with each
signal from a global reed law shared across the dataset. Physical constraints
are enforced through differentiable transformations, while anchoring,
positivity and monotonicity regularizations reduce the ambiguity of the learned
function.

Finally, the methodology was organized in two stages. Synthetic OpenWind data
provide controlled tests with known parameters and a known reed law. The
resulting strategy is then transferred to experimental recordings supplied by
Modartt, initially through calibration of the mouth-pressure parameter
\(\gamma\).

The next chapter presents the numerical calibration results and analyses the
accuracy, robustness and limitations of the proposed approach.

\label{chap:results}

\minitoc

This chapter presents the numerical validation of the proposed calibration

The experiments are organized according to increasing levels of realism.
First, the method is evaluated on synthetic signals generated with

\section{Sensitivity analysis}

Before evaluating the complete calibration procedure, it is useful to study
the behavior of the objective function with respect to variations of the
physical parameters.

The purpose of this preliminary analysis is twofold.

First, it provides insight into the local geometry of the optimization
landscape. Parameters associated with sharp minima are expected to be more
easily identifiable than parameters producing flatter loss profiles.

Second, it allows the influence of each physical parameter on the acoustic
response to be analyzed independently before considering the complete
inverse problem involving several unknowns simultaneously.

For each parameter, the reference OpenWind signal is generated using the
nominal physical configuration described in Chapter~3. One parameter is
then varied while all the others are kept fixed, and the multi-resolution
spectral loss introduced in Chapter~5 is evaluated with respect to the
reference signal.

\subsection{One-dimensional Scans}

The resulting one-dimensional loss scans are presented in
Figure~\ref{fig:parameter_scans}.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../../experiments/gradient/results/scans/scan_1D_MSTS_4params_openwind.png}
    \caption{One-dimensional scans of the multi-resolution spectral loss with respect to variations of the physical parameters. 
    Each curve corresponds to a single parameter, while all other parameters are kept fixed.}
    \label{fig:parameter_scans}

The loss associated with the parameter $\kappa$ exhibits a sharp and well
localized minimum around the reference value. Small deviations from the optimum
produce a rapid increase of the objective function, indicating that this
parameter has a strong influence on the generated acoustic signal. Such a
landscape is favorable for gradient-based optimization since the gradients
remain informative over a large neighborhood of the solution.

The mouth pressure parameter $\gamma$ also produces a well-defined minimum.
However, the loss increases much more rapidly for values larger than the
reference pressure than for smaller values. A noticeable discontinuity appears
around $\gamma \approx 0.6$, suggesting the existence of a transition between
two oscillation regimes of the reed model. This behavior reflects the
nonlinear nature of the excitation mechanism.

A similar behavior is observed for the parameter $\zeta$. Around the optimum,
the objective function presents a narrow valley that should facilitate the
identification process. For larger values of $\zeta$, the loss exhibits a
sudden increase before continuing its progression. This phenomenon is likely
associated with a qualitative modification of the reed dynamics.

The behavior of the quality factor $Q_r$ is markedly different. Although a
minimum is clearly visible near the reference value, the objective function
remains relatively flat over a broad interval. Consequently, large variations
of $Q_r$ only induce limited modifications of the acoustic signal, making this
parameter intrinsically more difficult to identify from pressure measurements
alone.

This observation is consistent with the statistical calibration experiments
presented later in this chapter, where $Q_r$ systematically exhibits larger
estimation errors than the other physical parameters.

function possesses well-defined minima for the four investigated parameters.
However, the curvature of the objective strongly depends on the considered
parameter. While $\gamma$, $\kappa$ and $\zeta$ generate comparatively steep

$Q_r$, suggesting a lower sensitivity of the radiated pressure to this
parameter. The spectrogram analysis further shows that, for $\gamma$ and

is varied independently while all remaining parameters are fixed at their
reference values. They therefore characterize the local sensitivity of the
objective to individual parameters, but do not reveal possible interactions,
correlations, or compensation effects between parameters. The optimization
landscape of the joint inverse problem may consequently be substantially more
complex than suggested by the individual scans.

\section{Calibration on synthetic OpenWind signals}

\subsection{Experimental Protocol}
Using a dataset of 300 synthetic OpenWind signals, the calibration procedure is 
evaluated on a controlled setting where the true physical parameters are known. 
The signals are generated by varying the parameters \(\gamma\), \(\kappa\), and \(\zeta\)
within a prescribed range, while keeping the quality factor \(Q_r\) and the other 
parameters fixed.
All the simulations are initialized with the same reference reed law $l(y)=max(y,0)$ and 
the same set of trainable parameters $(\gamma_0,\kappa_0,\zeta_0)$.

\subsection{Optimization strategy}

The calibration problem considered in this work is nonlinear and non-convex.
Consequently, the optimization process may converge towards different local
minima depending on the initial parameter values. Although the sensitivity
analysis presented in the previous section revealed well-defined minima for the
considered parameters, gradient-based methods remain sensitive to their
initialization, particularly when several parameters are estimated
simultaneously.

To improve the robustness of the optimization, a preliminary multi-start stage
is performed before the main calibration procedure. A set of physically
plausible initial parameter vectors is defined from prior knowledge of the
instrument. Starting from each initialization, a short Adam optimization is
performed on the long observation window in order to move the parameters toward
a nearby local minimum.

In the following, the term \emph{observation window} denotes the temporal
interval of the pressure signal used to evaluate the objective function.
If the simulated and observed signals are denoted respectively by
$p_{\mathrm{sim}}(t)$ and $p_{\mathrm{obs}}(t)$, the long observation window
corresponds to the complete interval
\[
t\in[0,T_{\mathrm{obs}}],
\]
used for the main calibration stage. This should not be confused with the
Hann analysis windows used internally in the STFT computation.

Rather than selecting a single initialization for the whole dataset, the
selection is carried out independently for every signal. For each candidate,
the objective function is evaluated individually, and each signal retains the
parameter vector producing the lowest loss. Consequently, the subsequent
optimization does not start from a common initialization but from the
best-performing candidate associated with each individual signal. This strategy
reduces the probability of converging toward poor local minima while preserving
the statistical independence of the calibration problems.

As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:calibration_pipeline}, once the initialization phase has been completed, each signal is associated
with the candidate parameter vector that produced the smallest objective
value during the initial multi-start procedure.

These selected parameters are then used as the starting points of the main
optimization stage. In this stage, Adam is applied using the complete
observation interval $[0,T_{\mathrm{obs}}]$. The purpose is to refine the
three parameters $(\gamma,\kappa,\zeta)$ by minimizing the multi-resolution
spectral discrepancy between the simulated and reference pressure signals.

Although the optimization of the 300 signals is implemented in a vectorized
form for computational efficiency, each signal defines an independent inverse
problem: it has its own reference pressure, physical parameters, initial
candidate and objective value.

After this full-window optimization, signals whose final objective value is
below the prescribed threshold are considered sufficiently well calibrated and
their current estimates are retained. For the remaining signals, an additional
local refinement stage is performed.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{pipeline_calibration.png}
    \caption{Schematic representation of the calibration pipeline.}
    \label{fig:calibration_pipeline}
\end{figure}
\newpage 
During the Adam optimization, three Short-Time Fourier Transform resolutions are 
simultaneously considered. Each STFT is computed using a Hann analysis window with the 
corresponding window length and hop size. The objective function is the average of the 
associated multi-resolution spectral losses

\subsection{Statistical analysis of parameter recovery}
\label{subsec:openwind_parameter_results}

The calibration procedure is evaluated on the 300 synthetic OpenWind signals
introduced previously. Since the reference values are known for every signal,
the estimation accuracy can be measured using the individual relative error

\begin{equation}
e_{\theta}^{(i)}
=
\frac{
\left|
\widehat{\theta}^{(i)}
-
\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(i)}
\right|
}{
\left|
\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(i)}
\right|
},
\qquad
\theta \in \{\gamma,\kappa,\zeta\},
\label{eq:individual_relative_error}
\end{equation}

where $\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(i)}$ and
$\widehat{\theta}^{(i)}$ respectively denote the reference and estimated
values for signal $i$.

The three calibrated quantities are dimensionless parameters of the scaled
reed model. In particular, $\gamma$ represents the normalized steady mouth
pressure, $\zeta$ characterizes the reed opening and flow contribution, and
$\kappa$ accounts for the flow induced by the reed motion. This
parameterization reduces the number of effective coefficients required to
describe the reed dynamics and provides quantities of comparable orders of
magnitude.

Figure~\ref{fig:openwind_error_histograms} presents the distributions of the
relative errors obtained for the three parameters over the complete dataset.

The results demonstrate that the proposed calibration method accurately
recovers the three physical parameters for a large majority of the signals.
The distributions are strongly concentrated near zero, although they exhibit
right-skewed tails associated with a limited number of more difficult
calibration cases.

The mouth-pressure parameter $\gamma$ is recovered with the highest overall
accuracy. Its mean relative error is

\begin{equation}
\overline{e}_{\gamma}=1.10\%,
\end{equation}

while its median error is only $0.22\%$. Moreover, $95\%$ of the calibrations
produce an error below $4.86\%$. The large difference between the mean and
median shows that most values of $\gamma$ are estimated very precisely, while
the average is increased by a relatively small number of less accurate
calibrations.

The parameter $\kappa$ presents a mean relative error of

\begin{equation}
\overline{e}_{\kappa}=1.80\%,
\end{equation}

and a median error of $1.14\%$. Its $95$th percentile reaches $6.04\%$.
Although the distribution remains concentrated at low error values, it is
slightly more dispersed than that obtained for $\gamma$. This indicates that
the acoustic response contains sufficient information to identify $\kappa$,
but that its estimation is somewhat more sensitive to the particular
configuration of the signal.

The parameter $\zeta$ is associated with the largest mean error,

\begin{equation}
\overline{e}_{\zeta}=2.11\%,
\end{equation}

despite a low median value of $0.49\%$. This apparently contrasting result is
explained by the longer right tail of its distribution. While a majority of
the signals lead to very accurate estimates, a limited number of calibrations
produce substantially larger errors. Accordingly, the $95$th percentile
reaches $9.39\%$, which is higher than for both $\gamma$ and $\kappa$.

The standard deviations confirm this increasing dispersion:

\begin{equation}

\qquad
\sigma_{\kappa}=2.11\%,
\qquad
\sigma_{\zeta}=3.16\%.
\label{eq:openwind_error_std}
\end{equation}

These values suggest the following hierarchy in terms of robustness of the
estimation:

\begin{equation}
\gamma
\quad \text{is the most accurately recovered, followed by} \quad
\kappa,
\quad \text{then} \quad
\zeta.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Acoustic reconstruction for representative calibration cases.}

To investigate this relationship, a global parameter error is associated with
each calibration. It is defined as the root mean square of the three individual
relative errors,

\begin{equation}
e_{\mathrm{glob}}^{(i)}
=
\sqrt{
\frac{
\left(e_{\gamma}^{(i)}\right)^2
+
\left(e_{\kappa}^{(i)}\right)^2
+
\left(e_{\zeta}^{(i)}\right)^2
}{3}
}.
\label{eq:global_parameter_error}
\end{equation}

Three signals are then selected to represent different levels of calibration
accuracy. They correspond approximately to the $10$th, $50$th and $95$th
percentiles of the distribution of $e_{\mathrm{glob}}$ and are referred to as
the well-calibrated, median and difficult cases, respectively. This selection
makes it possible to examine the acoustic consequences of progressively less
accurate parameter recovery without restricting the analysis to either the
best or worst individual realization.

For each selected case, the OpenWind target signal is compared with the
pressure generated by the DG model using the calibrated parameters.
Figure~\ref{fig:representative_spectrograms} compares their time--frequency
representations.

For the well-calibrated case, the OpenWind and calibrated DG spectrograms are
visually almost indistinguishable. Both the dominant low-frequency components
and the higher-frequency structures are accurately reproduced. The agreement
also remains very good for the median case, although small differences in the
magnitude of some spectral components become visible.

A clearer degradation appears for the difficult case. The main organization
of the spectrum remains reproduced by the calibrated model, but discrepancies
in the amplitudes of several time--frequency components become more
pronounced. Thus, even when the estimated parameters deviate more strongly
from their reference values, the calibrated model can preserve the main
spectral structure of the target signal, while losing accuracy in its detailed
amplitude distribution.

To quantify these observations, two complementary spectral indicators are
considered. First, the spectral convergence is defined by

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SC}
=
\frac{
\left\|
S^{\mathrm{DG}}-S^{\mathrm{OW}}
\right\|_F
}{
\left\|
S^{\mathrm{OW}}
\right\|_F
},
\label{eq:spectral_convergence_representative}
\end{equation}

where $S^{\mathrm{OW}}$ and $S^{\mathrm{DG}}$ denote the magnitude
spectrograms of the OpenWind and calibrated DG signals, respectively.
This quantity measures the global discrepancy between the two magnitude
spectrograms and vanishes for identical representations.

A complementary mean absolute spectral error is computed in the decibel
domain,

\begin{equation}
E_{\mathrm{dB}}
=
\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{TF}}}
\sum_{m,k}
\left|
D^{\mathrm{DG}}(m,k)
-
D^{\mathrm{OW}}(m,k)
\right|,
\label{eq:spectral_mae_representative}
\end{equation}

where $D^{\mathrm{OW}}$ and $D^{\mathrm{DG}}$ are the corresponding
log-magnitude spectrograms and $N_{\mathrm{TF}}$ denotes the total number of
time--frequency coefficients. Contrary to spectral convergence, this second
indicator directly measures the average discrepancy on the logarithmic scale
used for visualization.

larger discrepancies. At the same time, the main spectral structures remain

necessarily destroy the global acoustic structure, although they can

300 signals and studying their relationship with the corresponding parameter
errors.

Overall, these results validate the ability of the differentiable calibration
framework to recover the reed parameters from the radiated pressure signal
alone under controlled, noise-free conditions. The low median errors show that
highly accurate parameter estimates are obtained for most signals. The
representative time--frequency comparisons further indicate that this
parameter accuracy translates into an accurate reconstruction of the acoustic
response for typical calibration cases.

Nevertheless, the asymmetric error distributions and the presence of a
limited number of more difficult cases indicate that the inverse problem
remains locally challenging for some parameter configurations. In these cases,
the deterioration of parameter recovery can also be accompanied by noticeable
differences in the reconstructed pressure and its spectral representation.
These observations motivate the multi-start and targeted-refinement strategies
introduced in the previous subsection.

\subsection{Identification of the reed opening function $\ell(y)$}
\label{subsec:l_function_identification}

The previous experiments assumed that the reed opening law $\ell(y)$ was known
and fixed to its analytical expression
\begin{equation}
    \ell_{\mathrm{ref}}(y) = \max(y,0).
    \label{eq:l_reference}
\end{equation}

The reference law is continuous but not differentiable at $y=0$. This does
not prevent its use for generating the synthetic observations, since no
gradient with respect to the reference law is required during dataset
generation. During the inverse problem, however, the unknown law is represented
by the differentiable neural-network approximation $\ell_{\mathrm{NN}}$.
Gradients required by the optimization are therefore computed through
$\ell_{\mathrm{NN}}$, rather than through the analytical reference function.


Under this assumption, the calibration procedure was shown to accurately
recover the physical parameters $\gamma$, $\kappa$ and $\zeta$ from the
radiated pressure signal.

However, for experimental measurements, the exact relation between the reed
displacement and the effective opening may not be known a priori. The
calibration framework is therefore extended by representing $\ell(y)$ with a
trainable function $\ell_{\mathrm{NN}}(y)$. This experiment investigates
whether the differentiable solver can recover not only a finite set of
physical parameters, but also an unknown constitutive relation from acoustic
observations.

In order to evaluate this capability in a controlled setting, the same
noise-free synthetic OpenWind signals are considered. Since these signals were
generated using the analytical law~\eqref{eq:l_reference}, the learned function
can be directly compared with the exact reference.

The function $\ell_{\mathrm{NN}}$ is represented by a neural network whose
parameters are optimized by gradient descent through the differentiable
acoustic solver. The optimization is driven by the same multi-resolution
spectral objective used for the physical parameter calibration. Consequently,
the reference values of $\ell(y)$ are never directly provided to the optimizer:
the function is inferred indirectly from its influence on the simulated
pressure signal.

Because the reed opening function appears in the nonlinear flow relation
through the product
\begin{equation}
    \zeta\,\ell(y)\,F\!\left(\gamma-p(0,t)\right),
\end{equation}
its absolute scale cannot be determined independently of $\zeta$ without an
additional constraint. Indeed, multiplying $\ell$ by a constant while
appropriately rescaling $\zeta$ may produce a similar acoustic response.
To remove this scaling ambiguity, the physical normalization
\begin{equation}
    \ell(1)=1
    \label{eq:l_anchor}
\end{equation}
is introduced as an anchoring condition during training.

Figure~\ref{fig:l_function_training} shows the evolution of the spectral
objective together with the function obtained after optimization.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../../experiments/gradient/results/train_l_only_msts_openwind_matched/clean/training_summary.png}
    \caption{
    Identification of the reed opening function $\ell(y)$ from noise-free
    synthetic pressure signals. Left: evolution of the multi-resolution
    spectral loss during optimization. The vertical dotted line indicates
    the beginning of the fine optimization stage. Right: comparison between
    the reference law $\ell_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)=\max(y,0)$ and the learned
    function $\ell_{\mathrm{NN}}(y)$.
    }
    \label{fig:l_function_training}
\end{figure}

The optimization exhibits a clear decrease of the spectral objective during
the first part of the training. The loss decreases by more than one order of
magnitude before reaching a plateau around $10^{-1}$. The subsequent fine
optimization stage produces only limited variations, indicating that the
learned function has already reached a stable solution.

More importantly, the learned function closely reproduces the analytical law
used to generate the synthetic dataset. Over the considered interval, the
relative reconstruction error is

\begin{equation}
    e_{\ell}
    =
    \frac{
        \left\|
        \ell_{\mathrm{NN}}-\ell_{\mathrm{ref}}
        \right\|_2
    }{
        \left\|
        \ell_{\mathrm{ref}}
        \right\|_2
    }
    =
    0.840\%,
    \label{eq:l_relative_error}
\end{equation}

while the maximum absolute discrepancy is

\begin{equation}
    e_{\ell,\infty}
    =
    \max_y
    \left|
        \ell_{\mathrm{NN}}(y)
        -
        \ell_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)
    \right|
    =
    2.183\times10^{-2}.
    \label{eq:l_max_error}
\end{equation}

The anchoring condition is also accurately satisfied, with

\begin{equation}
    \ell_{\mathrm{NN}}(1)=0.9963,
\end{equation}

compared with the theoretical value $\ell_{\mathrm{ref}}(1)=1$.

These results show that the pressure observations contain sufficient
information, in this controlled synthetic configuration, to recover the
reference reed opening law with high accuracy. In particular, the optimization
does not merely reduce the acoustic reconstruction error: it also retrieves a
constitutive relation that is very close to the one used to generate the
observations.

This experiment therefore extends the parameter-identification results of the
previous subsection. The differentiable formulation makes it possible to
optimize both conventional physical parameters and a functional component of
the model through the same acoustic objective. This property is particularly
relevant for the subsequent application to experimental signals, for which the
exact reed opening law may not be available.


\section{Calibration from noisy OpenWind signals}
\label{sec:noisy_openwind_results}

The previous section evaluated the calibration procedure under idealized
conditions, using noise-free pressure signals generated with OpenWind. Although
this setting makes it possible to assess the intrinsic accuracy of the inverse
method, measured acoustic signals are inevitably affected by perturbations.
These may originate from the recording system, the surrounding environment,
unmodelled physical phenomena but also from the variability in time of some 
of the system's parameters ($\gamma$ for instance). 

The second numerical experiment therefore investigates the robustness of the
calibration procedure when the observations are contaminated by additive
noise. The objective is to determine whether the physical parameters remain
identifiable when the target pressure signal no longer corresponds exactly to
the output of the reference model.

\subsection{Noise model and experimental protocol}

The noisy experiments are constructed from the same collection of
\(300\) OpenWind signals as in the noise-free study. Consequently, the exact
values of the physical parameters

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(s)}
=
\left(
\gamma^{(s)},
\kappa^{(s)},
\zeta^{(s)}
\right),
\qquad
s=1,\ldots,300,
\end{equation}

remain known for every signal.

Using the same signals in both experiments makes it possible to isolate the
effect of the observation noise. In particular, differences in calibration
accuracy cannot be attributed to different parameter realizations or different
oscillation regimes.

For each clean pressure signal
\(\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{clean}}^{(s)}\), a noisy observation is generated as

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{noisy}}^{(s)}
=
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{clean}}^{(s)}
+
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{(s)},
\label{eq:noisy_observation}
\end{equation}

where \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{(s)}\) is an additive white-noise realization.
Its amplitude is adjusted to obtain a prescribed signal-to-noise ratio (SNR).

The SNR, expressed in decibels, is defined by

\begin{equation}
\operatorname{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}}
=
10\log_{10}
\left(
\frac{
\left\|
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{clean}}
\right\|_2^2
}{
\left\|
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
\right\|_2^2
}
\right).
\label{eq:snr_definition}
\end{equation}

Four noise levels are considered:

\begin{equation}
\operatorname{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}}
\in
\left\{
10,\,
20,\,
30,\,
40
\right\}.
\label{eq:considered_snr}
\end{equation}

A low SNR corresponds to a strongly contaminated observation, whereas a high
SNR corresponds to a signal closer to the noise-free reference. Thus, the
\(10\,\mathrm{dB}\) case represents the most difficult configuration and the
\(40\,\mathrm{dB}\) case the least noisy one.

For each SNR value, the same calibration strategy as in the noise-free
experiment is applied. The same multi-start initialization, Adam optimization,
long observation window and targeted refinement procedure are retained. The
quality of the reconstruction is therefore evaluated under identical numerical
conditions.

The optimization objective remains the multi-resolution spectral loss. At each
STFT resolution, the normalized linear-magnitude discrepancy and the decibel
discrepancy are combined. Since the spectral representation distributes the
comparison over time and frequency, it is expected to be less sensitive than a
pointwise waveform loss to local high-frequency perturbations. Nevertheless,
the logarithmic component may increase the relative contribution of weak
spectral components, including those introduced by noise.

For each calibrated parameter
\(\theta\in\{\gamma,\kappa,\zeta\}\), the relative estimation error is computed
as

\begin{equation}
e_{\theta}^{(s)}
=
\frac{
\left|
\widehat{\theta}^{(s)}
-
\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(s)}
\right|
}{
\left|
\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(s)}
\right|
}.
\label{eq:noisy_relative_error}
\end{equation}

The distributions of these errors are then compared across the four SNR levels
and with the results obtained from the clean signals.

ratios.

\subsection{Effect of noise on the optimization dynamics}
\label{subsec:noise_loss_dynamics}

The evolution of the objective function provides further insight into the
previous results. Figure~\ref{fig:loss_noise_training} compares the complete
training trajectories for the four noisy configurations.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[
        width=\textwidth
    ]{all_loss_ell_y_train_w_noise.png}

    \caption{
    Evolution of the multi-resolution spectral loss during the identification
    procedure for $40$, $30$, $20$ and $10$~dB SNR. The light curves represent
    the individual mini-batch losses, while the orange and green curves show
    the epoch mean and median, respectively; the vertical dotted line indicates
    the beginning of the fine optimization phase.
    }
    \label{fig:loss_noise_training}
\end{figure}

A common behaviour is observed for all noise levels. During the first part of
the optimization, the loss decreases rapidly as the model approaches a region
that reproduces the main acoustic characteristics of the observations. Once
this region has been reached, the loss enters a plateau and the fine
optimization phase produces only comparatively small corrections.

The value of this plateau, however, strongly depends on the SNR. For the
least noisy observations, the optimizer is able to reduce the spectral
discrepancy to relatively small values. When the amount of noise increases,
the residual loss becomes progressively larger.

This behaviour is expected. The forward solver is deterministic and produces
only the acoustic response associated with the physical model. It cannot
reproduce the random broadband component added to the OpenWind observations.
Consequently, even if the underlying physical parameters or constitutive law
were recovered exactly, a non-zero spectral discrepancy would remain between
the deterministic simulation and the noisy target.

The important observation is therefore not that the final loss increases with
noise, but that the correct physical information can still be recovered in
spite of this increasing irreducible discrepancy. This is precisely what is
observed for the identification of $\ell(y)$ down to $20$~dB: although the
absolute value of the spectral loss increases significantly, the reconstructed
constitutive law remains accurate to within approximately one percent.

At $10$~dB, this separation between physical signal and observation noise
becomes considerably more difficult. The higher loss plateau is accompanied by
a strong degradation of the identified law, indicating that the optimizer
starts compensating for part of the spectral perturbation by modifying the
physical model itself. In other words, the optimization no longer solely
reconstructs the deterministic acoustic behaviour but partially absorbs the
observation noise into the estimated constitutive relation.

This result highlights an important distinction between \emph{signal
reconstruction} and \emph{physical identification}. A low reconstruction loss
is not, by itself, sufficient to guarantee that the inferred model parameters
or constitutive laws are physically accurate. Conversely, in the presence of
noise, a relatively high residual loss may coexist with an accurate
identification because part of the discrepancy is intrinsically
unreproducible by the deterministic model.

Overall, these experiments indicate that the proposed differentiable
calibration framework remains robust under moderate observation noise, both for
finite-dimensional parameter estimation and for the identification of an
unknown constitutive relation. The $10$~dB configuration nevertheless reveals
a clear limitation of the current spectral objective and motivates the
investigation of more noise-robust loss functions and STFT configurations.

\section{Conclusion}

This chapter evaluated the proposed differentiable calibration framework on
synthetic acoustic observations under both noise-free and noisy conditions.

The preliminary sensitivity analysis first provided insight into the
identifiability of the physical parameters from the radiated pressure signal.
The multi-resolution spectral objective exhibits well-defined minima with
respect to $\gamma$, $\kappa$, and $\zeta$, whereas a substantially lower
sensitivity is observed for the quality factor $Q_r$. This observation
motivated the choice to focus the subsequent calibration experiments on
$\gamma$, $\kappa$, and $\zeta$, while assuming $Q_r$ to be known.

The statistical study conducted on 300 synthetic OpenWind signals then
demonstrated that these three parameters can be recovered accurately from
pressure observations alone. Most calibration errors remain small, although
the asymmetric error distributions reveal a limited number of more difficult
configurations. These results also highlight the importance of the multi-start
initialization and refinement strategy for dealing with the non-convexity of
the inverse problem.

Beyond the identification of a finite-dimensional parameter vector, the
framework was successfully extended to the reconstruction of the unknown reed
opening law $\ell(y)$. In the controlled noise-free configuration, the learned
function closely reproduces the analytical reference law
$\ell(y)=\max(y,0)$. This result shows that differentiation through the
physical solver can be used not only to estimate conventional model
parameters, but also to identify an unknown constitutive relation indirectly
from acoustic observations.

The experiments with additive noise provide a more contrasted picture.
Parameter estimation progressively deteriorates as the SNR decreases,
confirming the increased difficulty of recovering individual physical
parameters from strongly perturbed observations. The identification of the
reed opening law is comparatively robust for moderate noise levels, with no
significant degradation observed down to $20$~dB in the present experiments.
At $10$~dB, however, the reconstructed law becomes significantly biased,
revealing a limit of the current calibration procedure.

These experiments also emphasize the importance of the spectral representation
used in the objective function. Modifying the STFT configurations substantially
improves parameter recovery in the most strongly contaminated case, showing
that the choice of time--frequency resolutions is an integral part of the
inverse problem rather than a purely technical signal-processing parameter.

Overall, the results establish the feasibility of identifying both physical
parameters and an unknown constitutive law through a differentiable
physics-based acoustic model. At the same time, they highlight two main
limitations: the unequal identifiability of the model parameters and the
sensitivity of the inverse problem to strong observation noise. These
limitations provide natural directions for improving the robustness of the
calibration framework and must be kept in mind when moving from controlled
synthetic observations to experimental recordings.

\chapter{General Conclusion}

The objective of this work was to investigate the automated calibration of
physical models of musical reed wind instruments using differentiable numerical
simulation. The central idea was to combine a physics-based description of a
reed instrument with gradient-based optimization in order to infer unknown
model quantities directly from acoustic pressure observations.

The first part of this work focused on the construction of the forward acoustic
model. The propagation of acoustic waves inside the resonator was described by
a one-dimensional hyperbolic system coupled to a dynamical model of the reed
and to a radiation condition at the bell. Particular attention was given to
the mathematical reformulation of the equations and boundary conditions in a
form suitable for numerical discretization.

The acoustic equations were then discretized using a Discontinuous Galerkin
method with piecewise linear basis functions. Numerical fluxes and appropriate
boundary states were introduced to account for wave propagation and for the
coupling with the reed and radiation models. Time integration was performed
using explicit schemes for the acoustic field together with the treatment of
the additional boundary variables.

The resulting solver was first validated independently of the inverse problem.
Comparisons with an analytical solution in a simplified configuration confirmed
the expected convergence behaviour of the numerical discretization.
Comparisons with OpenWind subsequently showed that the solver reproduces the
reference acoustic dynamics with good agreement in both the time and frequency
domains. These validation steps were essential before using the numerical model
as the forward operator of an inverse problem.

The second major contribution of this work was to make this physical solver
differentiable. Its implementation with JAX enables derivatives of acoustic
observables with respect to physical parameters to be propagated through the
complete numerical simulation. The calibration problem can consequently be
formulated as the minimization of an acoustic discrepancy using gradient-based
optimization.

Because direct waveform comparisons can be highly sensitive to phase and
temporal shifts, particular attention was devoted to the definition of the
objective function. A multi-resolution spectral loss based on several
Short-Time Fourier Transform representations was adopted. By combining
different time--frequency resolutions and linear and logarithmic magnitude
representations, the objective captures both dominant spectral components and
lower-amplitude structures of the acoustic response.

The calibration framework was evaluated statistically on a dataset of
300 synthetic signals generated with OpenWind. A preliminary sensitivity
analysis revealed important differences in the identifiability of the physical
parameters. While the objective function is strongly sensitive to
$\gamma$, $\kappa$ and $\zeta$, the quality factor $Q_r$ produces a much
flatter optimization landscape and is consequently more difficult to recover
from radiated pressure observations alone.

Focusing on the three identifiable parameters, the calibration experiments
demonstrated that the differentiable approach can recover their reference
values with good accuracy for the large majority of the synthetic signals.
The remaining outliers nevertheless confirmed the non-convex nature of the
inverse problem and motivated the use of multi-start initialization and local
refinement strategies.

An important extension of the approach consisted in considering not only
scalar physical parameters but also an unknown constitutive relation. The reed
opening law $\ell(y)$ was represented by a trainable differentiable function
and inferred indirectly from acoustic observations. In the controlled
synthetic experiment, the learned law accurately recovered the analytical
reference $\ell(y)=\max(y,0)$. This result illustrates one of the main
advantages of the differentiable formulation: unknown components of the
physical model can be optimized through their effect on the simulated acoustic
response without requiring direct observations of these quantities.

The robustness experiments further showed that the conclusions depend on the
level and nature of the observation noise. Parameter recovery progressively
deteriorates as the signal-to-noise ratio decreases, whereas the identification
of the reed opening law remains accurate under moderate noise levels in the
experiments considered here. At high noise levels, however, the estimated
physical quantities may begin to compensate for spectral components that
cannot be reproduced by the deterministic forward model.

These experiments also demonstrated that the success of the inverse problem
depends not only on the physical model and optimization algorithm, but also on
the representation used to compare simulated and observed signals. The
substantial improvement obtained by modifying the STFT configurations in the
most strongly contaminated case emphasizes the importance of designing an
objective function adapted to the acoustic information relevant for
identification.

Overall, this work establishes a complete framework connecting physical
modelling, numerical simulation, automatic differentiation and inverse
optimization for musical acoustics. The results demonstrate that differentiable
physics-based simulation provides a viable approach for recovering both
physical parameters and unknown constitutive relations from acoustic
observations.

At the same time, the study highlights several challenges that remain before
such methods can be applied routinely to experimental instruments. Parameter
identifiability, observation noise, model discrepancy and the limited amount
of experimental information must all be taken into account. Addressing these
issues, in particular through multi-observation calibration, more robust
spectral objectives and physically constrained representations of unknown
constitutive laws, constitutes a natural continuation of the present work.

\chapter{Perspectives}
\label{chap:perspectives}
\minitoc

The results obtained in this work demonstrate the potential of differentiable
physical modelling for the calibration of self-sustained musical instrument
models. At the same time, the numerical experiments highlight several
limitations of the present formulation and suggest a number of possible
extensions. In particular, the identification of an unknown constitutive law
from acoustic observations suggests that the differentiable framework could
eventually be extended beyond the calibration of a finite-dimensional set of
physical parameters.

%==============================================================================
\section{Improving the robustness of the inverse problem}
%==============================================================================

A first perspective concerns the robustness of the calibration procedure.
Although the physical parameters are accurately recovered for most synthetic
signals, the statistical experiments reveal a limited number of configurations
associated with substantially larger errors. Furthermore, the noise experiments
show a progressive degradation of parameter recovery as the signal-to-noise
ratio decreases.

The present results indicate that the design of the objective function plays
an important role in this robustness. In particular, modifying the STFT
resolutions substantially improves the calibration results in the most strongly
contaminated configuration. The time--frequency representation should therefore
be regarded as an integral component of the inverse method rather than as a
purely technical signal-processing choice.

Future work could investigate spectral objectives better adapted to noisy
observations. Another promising direction would be to exploit more explicitly
the quasi-periodic structure of the acoustic signal once the self-sustained
oscillation regime is established. Information shared across several
oscillation periods could potentially help emphasize coherent harmonic
structures while reducing the influence of uncorrelated observation noise.

The optimization strategy itself could also be extended. The current approach
relies mainly on Adam together with a multi-start initialization and an
additional refinement stage. Hybrid strategies combining first-order gradient
methods with second-order or quasi-Newton approaches could be investigated,
particularly for the final refinement of the estimated parameters.

%==============================================================================
\section{From parameter identification to model identification}
%==============================================================================

One of the most promising results of this work is the identification of the
reed opening law $\ell(y)$ from acoustic pressure observations. In the
synthetic experiments, the differentiable solver successfully recovers the
reference constitutive relation

\begin{equation}
    \ell_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)=\max(y,0).
\end{equation}

This result suggests a broader use of differentiable physical modelling.
Instead of restricting the inverse problem to a small number of scalar
parameters, uncertain components of the physical model could themselves be
identified through their influence on the acoustic response.

A natural continuation would first consist in considering more complex reed
opening laws. The simple reference function used in this work provides a
controlled validation case, whereas the actual relation between reed
displacement and effective opening may be more complex.

More generally, the same idea could be extended beyond the static constitutive
law $\ell(y)$. Some components of the reed dynamics could also be regarded as
uncertain and potentially inferred from acoustic observations. This would move
the problem from conventional parameter calibration towards a more general
identification of the excitation model itself.

Such extensions would naturally raise additional questions concerning
identifiability. Increasing the flexibility of the model may allow different
unknown quantities to compensate for one another while producing similar
acoustic responses. Physical constraints and appropriate regularization would
therefore become increasingly important.

%==============================================================================
\section{Time-dependent excitation conditions}
%==============================================================================

Another possible extension concerns the assumption that the excitation
parameters remain constant during a simulated note. In the present experiments,
the mouth-pressure parameter $\gamma$ is represented by a single scalar value
for each signal. While this assumption provides a convenient setting for the
validation of the calibration procedure, it is restrictive when considering
real playing conditions.

In practice, the pressure applied by the musician evolves during the attack
and may continue to vary throughout the note. A natural perspective would
therefore be to consider a time-dependent mouth-pressure parameter
$\gamma(t)$ and attempt to recover its evolution from acoustic observations.

Such a formulation would provide greater flexibility for reproducing
experimental recordings, particularly during transient regimes. However, it
would also make the inverse problem considerably more difficult, since temporal
variations of the excitation could compensate for errors in other physical
parameters or in the forward model.

%==============================================================================
\section{Uncertainty in the instrument geometry}
%==============================================================================

The geometry of the resonator constitutes another possible source of
uncertainty. Throughout this work, the bore profile and the geometrical
parameters of the instrument are assumed to be perfectly known. This assumption
is appropriate for the synthetic validation experiments, but may not hold when
dealing with experimental instruments whose internal geometry is known only
approximately.

Since the acoustic response strongly depends on the resonator geometry, a
natural extension of the present framework would be to include some geometrical
quantities among the unknowns of the calibration problem. The differentiable
solver could then be used not only to estimate excitation and reed parameters,
but also to adjust an imperfectly known description of the instrument from
acoustic observations.

Ultimately, one could also investigate whether information about the bore
profile itself can be recovered or refined from measured acoustic signals.
This would constitute a substantially more challenging inverse problem,
particularly because modifications of the geometry may produce acoustic effects
that can also be explained by variations of other model parameters.

%==============================================================================
\section{Joint identification from multiple observations}
%==============================================================================

Several of the previous extensions naturally motivate the use of multiple
acoustic observations. A single recording may not contain enough independent
information to distinguish all unknown quantities, especially when several
parameters or model components have similar effects on the radiated pressure.

A natural continuation would therefore consist in jointly calibrating several
recordings corresponding to different notes, excitation levels or playing
conditions. Some quantities could then be regarded as common to all
observations, such as properties of the instrument geometry or of the reed,
whereas excitation quantities could remain specific to each recording.

Such a formulation could be particularly valuable for the identification of
the reed opening law. Different excitation conditions would explore different
regions of the reed dynamics and could therefore provide complementary
information about the unknown constitutive relation.

More generally, combining several observations could help distinguish
quantities associated with the instrument itself from those associated with
the musician or with a particular playing condition, thereby improving the
identifiability of the inverse problem.

%==============================================================================
\section{Towards calibration from experimental recordings}
%==============================================================================

Ultimately, the main continuation of this work is the application of the
proposed calibration framework to experimental measurements. The synthetic
OpenWind experiments provide a controlled setting in which the reference
parameters and constitutive law are known. Experimental recordings introduce
additional sources of uncertainty, including measurement noise, imperfect
knowledge of the instrument geometry, unknown excitation conditions, and
physical phenomena that are not fully represented by the forward model.

This transition also changes the interpretation of the inverse problem. For
synthetic data, the recovered quantities can be directly compared with known
reference values. For experimental data, a small discrepancy between simulated
and measured pressures does not necessarily guarantee that the estimated
parameters correspond to their actual physical values. Parameter
identifiability and model uncertainty therefore become central issues.

The different perspectives discussed in this chapter are closely related to
this objective. Exploiting the periodic structure of the acoustic signal could
improve robustness to measurement noise; considering time-dependent excitation
conditions could provide a more realistic description of the musician;
uncertain geometrical quantities could be included in the calibration; and the
identification of constitutive laws or dynamical components could progressively
account for physical effects that are not sufficiently described by the
initial model.

The differentiable solver developed in this work therefore provides not only
a tool for parameter calibration, but also a framework in which progressively
richer hypotheses about the instrument and its excitation can be confronted
with acoustic observations. Extending this framework from controlled synthetic
experiments to experimental recordings constitutes the main perspective opened
by the present work.
