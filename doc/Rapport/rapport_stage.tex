\begin{abstract}
This project focuses on the automated calibration of physical models for reed musical instruments. 
We develop a numerical framework to simulate wave propagation inside instruments with variable cross-sectional areas using the Discontinuous Galerkin Method (DGM). 
Special attention is given to the implementation of boundary conditions that model radiation and reed dynamics. 
The framework is validated against analytical solutions for uniform cross-sections and extended to non-uniform geometries, such as exponential and conical profiles. 
Results demonstrate accurate wave propagation, proper handling of complex boundaries, and convergence consistent with theoretical expectations. 
The proposed approach provides a foundation for efficient parameter calibration in virtual instrument models.



\vspace{3em}
Ce projet porte sur la calibration automatique de modèles physiques pour les instruments de musique à anche. 
Nous développons un cadre numérique pour simuler la propagation des ondes à l'intérieur d'instruments présentant des sections transversales variables, en utilisant la méthode de Galerkin discontinu (DGM). 
Une attention particulière est portée à la mise en œuvre des conditions aux limites modélisant la radiation et la dynamique de l'anche. 
Le cadre est validé par rapport à des solutions analytiques pour des sections uniformes et étendu à des géométries non uniformes, telles que les profils exponentiels et coniques. 
Les résultats montrent une propagation des ondes précise, une gestion correcte des conditions aux limites complexes et une convergence conforme aux prédictions théoriques. 
Cette approche fournit une base solide pour la calibration efficace des paramètres dans les modèles d'instruments virtuels.
\end{abstract}

\chapter*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Introduction}

This Master's internship is a continuation of the project carried out during the third semester of the CSMI Master's degree, 
devoted to the automated calibration of physical models for reed wind instruments.

The internship was conducted under the supervision of Dr Emmanuel Franck, Dr Laurent Navoret and Dr Victor Michel-Dansac 
from the Institut de Recherche Mathématique Avancée (IRMA) in Strasbourg, together with Dr Juliette Chabassier from Modartt.

Modartt (MOdels and Data for ARTs and Technology) \cite{Modartt} is a French company specialized in physically based sound 
synthesis. Since the release of \textit{Pianoteq} in 2006, the company has developed virtual musical instruments based on physical 
modeling rather than sample playback, providing realistic sound synthesis while preserving a strong physical interpretability of 
the underlying models.

The mathematical model considered in this work describes the generation of sound waves in reed wind instruments through the 
coupling of a one-dimensional acoustic wave propagation model with a nonlinear reed model. Although the geometry of the instrument 
can generally be measured accurately, several parameters governing the reed dynamics and the nonlinear coupling remain difficult 
or impossible to determine experimentally. Consequently, these parameters must be estimated from recorded acoustic signals through 
the solution of an inverse problem.

The objective of this internship is therefore to develop an automatic calibration framework capable of identifying both scalar 
physical parameters and, more generally, unknown functions appearing in the governing equations of the model. The proposed methodology 
relies on differentiable numerical simulations implemented in JAX, allowing gradients to be computed efficiently through 
automatic differentiation.

To assess the proposed approach under controlled conditions, synthetic datasets generated with the OpenWind software are first used, 
where the ground-truth parameters are known. This enables a quantitative evaluation of the accuracy and robustness of the calibration 
procedure. In a second stage, the methodology is applied to experimental recordings provided by Modartt in order to evaluate its 
applicability on real musical data.

The remainder of this manuscript is organized as follows. First, the mathematical model describing sound production in reed instruments 
is introduced together with the associated numerical solver developed during the previous project. We then formulate the inverse problem 
and present the optimization framework used for parameter and function estimation. Finally, we evaluate the proposed approach on both 
synthetic and experimental datasets and discuss its performance and limitations.

\section*{Reed Instruments}

A reed is a thin, flexible lamina mounted at the mouthpiece or at the entrance
of an air column resonator that acts as a valve controlling the airflow generated
by the pressure difference between the player's mouth and the instrument bore
\cite{Universalis_Anche,chaigne:hal-05370840}.

\vspace{0.4cm}

The interaction between the vibrating reed and the acoustic resonator is
responsible for the self-sustained oscillations that produce sound.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{reed_column.png}
\caption{Reed and saxophone.}
\label{fig:reed_schematic}
\end{figure}
There are two principal categories of reed instruments:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Free reeds (harmonicas, accordions, \dots).
    \item Beating reeds (clarinets, saxophones, oboes, bassoons, \dots).
\end{itemize}

In beating reed instruments, which constitute the focus of this work, the reed periodically opens and closes under the combined action of 
the mouth pressure and the acoustic pressure inside the mouthpiece. This nonlinear interaction regulates the airflow entering the instrument 
and excites acoustic waves that propagate along the air column. The geometry of the resonator, together with the reed dynamics, determines 
the resulting sound.

Accurate numerical simulation of this coupled reed--resonator system requires modeling both the wave propagation inside the instrument and 
the nonlinear boundary conditions describing the reed motion. Several parameters governing the reed behavior cannot be directly measured 
and must therefore be estimated from acoustic observations. This motivates the inverse calibration problem addressed in this internship.

\section*{Objectives}

The main objectives of this internship are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Develop a differentiable numerical solver for the mathematical model describing sound production in reed wind instruments.
    
    \item Formulate the calibration of the physical model as an inverse problem.
    
    \item Estimate unknown physical parameters of the model from acoustic pressure measurements.
    
    \item Extend the calibration procedure to the estimation of unknown functions involved in the governing equations.
    
    \item Validate the proposed methodology on synthetic datasets generated with OpenWind and on experimental recordings provided by Modartt.
\end{enumerate}

\chapter{Physical Model}

\section{Sound generation mechanism}

Single-reed instruments, such as the saxophone or the clarinet, produce sound through the interaction between an airflow supplied by the musician, a vibrating reed, and the acoustic resonator formed by the instrument bore \cite{chaigne:hal-05370840}.

When the player blows into the mouthpiece, a pressure difference is established between the mouth cavity and the interior of the instrument. Air is forced through the narrow opening between the reed and the mouthpiece. Since the reed is flexible, the aerodynamic force causes it to oscillate, continuously modifying the opening through which the air flows.

The resulting oscillations periodically modulate the airflow entering the resonator. This modulation generates pressure fluctuations that propagate inside the air column in the form of acoustic waves. These waves travel along the bore, are reflected by the instrument boundaries, and interact again with the reed. The continuous exchange of energy between the reed and the resonator produces self-sustained oscillations, which are responsible for the generation of musical sound.

At the bell of the instrument, the acoustic wave is not entirely transmitted to the surrounding air. A portion of the wave is radiated outside the instrument, while the remaining part is reflected back into the bore. This radiation phenomenon modifies the acoustic impedance of the resonator and influences both the energy losses and the produced sound.

Figure~\ref{fig:reed_generation} summarizes the three physical mechanisms involved in the sound production process.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{generation_onde_anche.png}
    \caption{Illustration of the sound generation mechanism in a single-reed instrument (adapted from Chapter~2 of \cite{vergez2010analyse}).}
    \label{fig:reed_generation}
\end{figure}

The physical behaviour of the instrument results from the continuous interaction of three complementary mechanisms:

\begin{itemize}
    \item the mechanical oscillations of the reed,
    \item the propagation of acoustic waves inside the resonator,
    \item the radiation of acoustic energy at the bell.
\end{itemize}

These mechanisms constitute the basis of the mathematical model introduced in the following section.

\section{Mathematical model}

To reproduce the physical behaviour described above, each physical mechanism is represented by an appropriate mathematical model. Acoustic wave propagation inside the bore is governed by conservation laws, the reed motion is described by a mechanical oscillator, and both subsystems are coupled through nonlinear boundary conditions.

Following the adimensional formulation proposed in \cite{chabassier:hal-03794382}, the behaviour of a single-reed instrument is described by the coupled system \cite{MAMAMIA2024}

\begin{subequations}
\begin{empheq}[left=\empheqlbrace]{align}
\frac{S(x)}{c S^\ast}\,\partial_t p(x,t) + \partial_x v(x,t) &= 0 \label{eq:1a}\\[4pt]
\frac{S^\ast}{c S(x)}\,\partial_t v(x,t) + \partial_x p(x,t) &= 0 \label{eq:1b}\\[4pt]
Z^\ast_T\,\partial_t \phi + \sqrt{\alpha}\,p(L,t) &= 0 \label{eq:1c}\\[4pt]
-\frac{\beta}{Z^\ast_T}\,p(L,t) + v(L,t) + \sqrt{\alpha}\,\phi &= 0 \label{eq:1d}\\[4pt]
-v(0,t) + \zeta \ell(y)\,F(\gamma-p(0,t))
+ \varepsilon\frac{\kappa}{\omega_r}\partial_t y &=0
\label{eq:1e}\\[6pt]
\frac{1}{\omega_r^2}\partial_{tt}y
+\frac{1}{Q_r\omega_r}\partial_ty
+y-1
&=\varepsilon(\gamma-p(0,t))
\label{eq:1f}
\end{empheq}
\end{subequations}

where $p(x,t)$ denotes the acoustic pressure, $v(x,t)$ the acoustic volume flow, and $y(t)$ the dimensionless reed opening. The nonlinear function

\[
F(\Delta p)=\operatorname{sign}(\Delta p)\sqrt{|\Delta p|}
\]

models the dependence of the airflow on the pressure difference across the reed.

The remaining parameters describe the geometry of the instrument and the reed dynamics: $S(x)$ is the bore cross-sectional area, $S^\ast$ a reference area, $c$ the speed of sound, $Z_T^\ast$ a reference acoustic impedance, $\alpha$ and $\beta$ characterize the radiation condition at the bell, $\gamma$ is the dimensionless blowing pressure, $\omega_r=2\pi f_r$ is the reed natural angular frequency, $Q_r$ its quality factor, $\kappa$ characterizes the reed dynamics, and $\zeta \ell(y)$ models the coupling between the reed opening and the airflow.

Although written as a single system, Equations~(\ref{eq:1a})--(\ref{eq:1f}) naturally decompose into three interacting components: acoustic wave propagation, reed dynamics, and boundary coupling.

\section{Interpretation of the model}

The mathematical model combines equations of different nature in order to represent the physical processes occurring inside the instrument.

The propagation of acoustic waves inside the resonator is described by the first two equations. Since the acoustic pressure and the acoustic volume flow evolve both in space and in time, they naturally form a system of partial differential equations (PDEs). Under the assumption of plane wave propagation, these equations express the conservation of mass and momentum inside the air column.

Unlike the acoustic field, the reed motion is localized at the mouthpiece. Its displacement depends only on time and is therefore modeled by an ordinary differential equation (ODE). In the present model, the reed is represented as a damped one-degree-of-freedom mechanical oscillator excited by the pressure difference across the mouthpiece.

These two subsystems cannot be considered independently. The reed controls the airflow entering the resonator, while the acoustic pressure generated inside the bore modifies the reed motion. Their interaction is introduced through the nonlinear boundary condition at the mouthpiece, creating the feedback mechanism responsible for self-sustained oscillations.

At the opposite end of the instrument, a radiation boundary condition models the interaction between the resonator and the surrounding air. Part of the acoustic energy is radiated outside the instrument, whereas the remaining part is reflected back into the bore. This dynamic boundary condition accounts for both radiation losses and the acoustic impedance seen at the bell.

The complete physical model is therefore obtained by coupling

\begin{itemize}
    \item a hyperbolic system of partial differential equations describing acoustic propagation,
    \item a nonlinear ordinary differential equation describing the reed motion,
    \item dynamic boundary conditions coupling the two subsystems.
\end{itemize}

\section{Mathematical properties}

Although each component of the model is relatively simple when considered separately, their coupling results in a nonlinear dynamical system whose numerical simulation is particularly challenging.

The acoustic equations constitute a first-order hyperbolic system. Such systems are characterized by the propagation of information along characteristic directions at finite speed, making them particularly suitable for describing wave propagation. Numerical methods must therefore preserve wave amplitudes and propagation speeds while limiting artificial numerical dissipation.

The reed dynamics introduce an additional mechanical subsystem coupled to the acoustic field. Because the airflow depends nonlinearly on both the pressure difference and the reed opening, the interaction between the resonator and the reed remains strongly nonlinear throughout the simulation.

The boundary conditions also play a central role. At the mouthpiece, they provide the nonlinear coupling between the acoustic resonator and the reed oscillator. At the bell, the radiation condition introduces an additional dynamic equation accounting for acoustic radiation into the surrounding air. These boundary conditions govern the exchange of energy between the different physical components.

Consequently, the complete model consists of a coupled PDE--ODE system with nonlinear dynamic boundary conditions. Except for highly simplified configurations, no analytical solution is available. Numerical methods are therefore required to approximate the solution of the governing equations.

In the remainder of this work, the acoustic equations are discretized using the Discontinuous Galerkin method, while suitable time integration schemes are employed to advance both the acoustic field and the reed dynamics.
\chapter{Mathematical Reformulation}
\label{chap:mathematical_reformulation}

\minitoc

The governing equations introduced in the previous chapter provide an accurate
description of the physical mechanisms responsible for sound generation in reed
wind instruments. However, this formulation is primarily motivated by physical
considerations and is not directly suitable for the numerical method employed in
this work.

The Discontinuous Galerkin (DG) method is naturally designed for systems of
hyperbolic conservation laws (see Chapter~5 in \cite{HesthavenWarburton2008}). Consequently, before introducing the numerical
discretization, the acoustic equations must first be rewritten into a
conservative form. This reformulation also reveals the characteristic structure
of the system, which considerably simplifies the treatment of the nonlinear
boundary conditions.

The objective of this chapter is therefore threefold. First, we introduce a
change of variables that transforms the acoustic equations into a conservative
form compatible with the DG framework. Secondly, we derive the characteristic
variables associated with the resulting hyperbolic system. Finally, both the
radiation and reed boundary conditions are rewritten in terms of these
characteristic variables, leading to a formulation particularly well suited for
their numerical treatment.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\section{Conservative formulation}

The acoustic propagation inside the instrument is governed by the coupled
equations

\begin{equation}
\frac{S(x)}{cS^\ast}\,\partial_t p(x,t)
+
\partial_x v(x,t)
=
0,
\label{eq:cons_original_1}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\frac{S^\ast}{cS(x)}\,\partial_t v(x,t)
+
\partial_x p(x,t)
=
0,
\label{eq:cons_original_2}
\end{equation}

where \(p(x,t)\) denotes the acoustic pressure, \(v(x,t)\) the acoustic volume
velocity, \(S(x)\) the local cross-sectional area of the resonator and
\(S^\ast\) a reference section.

Although this formulation is physically meaningful, it presents a significant
difficulty for a DG discretization. Indeed, the coefficients multiplying the
time derivatives depend explicitly on the geometry of the instrument. A direct
spatial discretization would therefore produce local mass matrices depending on
the cross-sectional area, making the numerical implementation unnecessarily
complex.

To overcome this difficulty, we introduce the conservative variables

\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\tilde p \\
\tilde v
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\dfrac{S(x)}{cS^\ast}\,p \\
\dfrac{S^\ast}{cS(x)}\,v
\end{pmatrix}.
\label{eq:conservative_variables}
\end{equation}

Substituting these definitions into Equations
\eqref{eq:cons_original_1}--\eqref{eq:cons_original_2} yields

\begin{equation}
\partial_t \tilde{\mathbf{u}}
+
\partial_x
\left(
A(x)\tilde{\mathbf{u}}
\right)
=
0,
\label{eq:conservative_system}
\end{equation}

where the flux matrix is

\begin{equation}
A(x)
=
\begin{pmatrix}
0 &
\dfrac{cS(x)}{S^\ast}
\\[0.4em]
\dfrac{cS^\ast}{S(x)}
&
0
\end{pmatrix}.
\label{eq:flux_matrix}
\end{equation}

Equation \eqref{eq:conservative_system} is now written under the standard form
of a first-order conservation law,

\begin{equation}
\partial_t\tilde{\mathbf u}
+
\partial_x
\mathbf F(\tilde{\mathbf u})
=
0,
\end{equation}

with

\begin{equation}
\mathbf F(\tilde{\mathbf u})
=
A(x)\tilde{\mathbf u}.
\end{equation}

This reformulation constitutes the key step allowing the application of the
Discontinuous Galerkin method. Besides recovering the conservative structure of
the equations, it also leads to uniform mass matrices that no longer depend on
the instrument geometry.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\section{Characteristic variables}

Beyond its conservative form, System
\eqref{eq:conservative_system} possesses a simple characteristic structure.

Characteristic variables describe waves travelling in opposite directions along
the instrument bore. Their introduction considerably simplifies the treatment
of boundary conditions since, at each boundary, only one characteristic enters
the computational domain while the other leaves it. Consequently, prescribing
the incoming characteristic variable automatically defines the boundary state.

The characteristic variables are obtained from the eigenstructure of the flux
matrix \(A(x)\).

The eigenvalues satisfy

\begin{equation}
\det(A-\lambda I)=0,
\end{equation}

which immediately gives

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\lambda^\pm=\pm c.
}
\end{equation}

An important observation is that the propagation speed is independent of the
cross-sectional area. Although the geometry modifies the amplitudes of the
acoustic variables, waves always propagate at the speed of sound.

The corresponding left eigenvectors satisfy

\begin{equation}
l^\pm A
=
\lambda^\pm l^\pm.
\end{equation}

For the positive eigenvalue \(\lambda^+=c\), one obtains

\begin{equation}
l_2
=
\frac{S^\ast}{S(x)}\,l_1,
\end{equation}

whereas for \(\lambda^-=-c\),

\begin{equation}
l_2
=
-
\frac{S^\ast}{S(x)}\,l_1.
\end{equation}

Choosing \(l_1=1\) gives the normalized eigenvectors

\begin{equation}
l^+
=
\left(
1,
\frac{S^\ast}{S(x)}
\right),
\qquad
l^-
=
\left(
1,
-
\frac{S^\ast}{S(x)}
\right).
\end{equation}

The characteristic variables are then obtained by projecting the conservative
state vector onto these eigenvectors,

\begin{equation}
w^\pm
=
l^\pm\tilde{\mathbf u}.
\end{equation}

Finally,

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
w^\pm
=
\tilde v
\pm
\frac{S^\ast}{S(x)}
\tilde p.
}
\label{eq:riemann_variables}
\end{equation}

These quantities are commonly referred to as the \emph{Riemann invariants} of
the acoustic system.

Physically, \(w^+\) represents the acoustic wave travelling from the reed
towards the bell, whereas \(w^-\) corresponds to the wave reflected from the
bell towards the reed as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:characteristics_propagation}. 
Throughout the remainder of this work, boundary
conditions are expressed in terms of these characteristic variables rather than
directly in terms of pressure and volume velocity. This choice considerably
simplifies both the mathematical analysis and the numerical implementation of
the coupled problem.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{characteristics_propagation.png}
    \caption{Illustration of the characteristic variables \(w^+\) and \(w^-\) representing waves propagating in opposite directions along the instrument bore. 
    The positive characteristic \(w^+\) travels from the reed towards the bell, while the negative characteristic \(w^-\) travels from the bell towards the reed.}
    \label{fig:characteristics_propagation}
\end{figure}

\section{Radiation boundary condition}

The right boundary of the computational domain corresponds to the bell of the
instrument, where the acoustic wave is partially radiated into the surrounding
air. Unlike a perfectly reflecting boundary, part of the acoustic energy leaves
the resonator while the remaining part is reflected back into the instrument.

Within the present model, this phenomenon is described by the coupled system

\begin{equation}
\begin{cases}
Z_T^\ast\,\partial_t\phi(t)
+
\sqrt{\alpha}\,p(L,t)
=
0,
\\[0.4em]
-\dfrac{\beta}{Z_T^\ast}p(L,t)
+
v(L,t)
+
\sqrt{\alpha}\phi(t)
=
0,
\end{cases}
\label{eq:rad_original}
\end{equation}

where the auxiliary variable $\phi(t)$ models the dynamic response of the
radiation impedance.

The second equation can be rewritten as

\begin{equation}
v(L,t)
=
\dfrac{\beta}{Z_T^\ast}p(L,t)
-
\sqrt{\alpha}\phi(t).
\label{eq:rad_velocity}
\end{equation}

To express this condition in terms of the conservative variables introduced
previously, we use

\begin{equation}
p
=
\dfrac{cS^\ast}{S_R}\tilde p,
\qquad
v
=
\dfrac{cS_R}{S^\ast}\tilde v,
\end{equation}

where $S_R=S(L)$ denotes the cross-sectional area at the bell.

Substituting these expressions into
Equation~\eqref{eq:rad_velocity} gives

\begin{equation}
\tilde v
=
\dfrac{\beta}{Z_T^\ast}
\left(
\dfrac{S^\ast}{S_R}
\right)^2
\tilde p
-
\dfrac{S^\ast}{cS_R}
\sqrt{\alpha}\phi.
\end{equation}

Introducing the characteristic variables

\begin{equation}
w^\pm
=
\tilde v
\pm
k_R\tilde p,
\qquad
k_R
=
\dfrac{S^\ast}{S_R},
\end{equation}

we have

\begin{equation}
\tilde v
=
\dfrac{w^++w^-}{2},
\qquad
\tilde p
=
\dfrac{w^+-w^-}{2k_R}.
\end{equation}

Substituting these expressions into the boundary condition yields

\begin{equation}
\dfrac{w^++w^-}{2}
=
\dfrac{\beta}{Z_T^\ast}
\left(
\dfrac{S^\ast}{S_R}
\right)^2
\dfrac{w^+-w^-}{2k_R}
-
\dfrac{S^\ast}{cS_R}
\sqrt{\alpha}\phi.
\end{equation}

For clarity, we introduce the coefficients

\begin{equation}
K
=
\dfrac{\beta}{Z_T^\ast}
\left(
\dfrac{S^\ast}{S_R}
\right)^2,
\qquad
r=\dfrac{K}{k_R},
\qquad
C
=
\dfrac{S^\ast}{cS_R}\sqrt{\alpha}.
\end{equation}

After straightforward algebraic manipulations, the incoming characteristic is
obtained as

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
w^-
=
\dfrac{r-1}{r+1}w^+
-
\dfrac{2C}{r+1}\phi.
}
\label{eq:rad_final}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:rad_final} expresses the incoming wave solely as a function
of the outgoing wave and the radiation state variable.
Consequently, the radiation boundary condition can be imposed explicitly during
the numerical resolution without requiring any iterative procedure.

Moreover, this characteristic formulation naturally provides the ghost state
required by the numerical flux of the DG method, making its implementation both
simple and physically consistent.

\section{Reed boundary condition}

The left boundary of the instrument differs fundamentally from the radiation
boundary. Instead of coupling the acoustic field with the surrounding air, it
models the interaction between the pressure waves inside the resonator and the
mechanical motion of the reed.

This interaction is responsible for the self-sustained oscillations that
generate the musical sound. Owing to the nonlinear airflow through the reed
channel, the corresponding boundary condition is intrinsically nonlinear.

The coupled reed model is given by

\begin{equation}
\begin{cases}
-v(0,t)
+
\zeta\ell(y)
F(\gamma-p(0,t))
+
\varepsilon\dfrac{\kappa}{\omega_r}\dot y
=
0,
\\[0.5em]
\dfrac{1}{\omega_r^2}\ddot y
+
\dfrac{1}{Q_r\omega_r}\dot y
+
y-1
=
\varepsilon(\gamma-p(0,t)).
\end{cases}
\label{eq:reed_original}
\end{equation}

The first equation prescribes the acoustic velocity at the entrance of the tube,
while the second governs the mechanical dynamics of the reed.

Using the conservative variables,

\begin{equation}
p
=
\dfrac{cS^\ast}{S_L}\tilde p,
\qquad
v
=
\dfrac{cS_L}{S^\ast}\tilde v,
\end{equation}

where $S_L=S(0)$, the acoustic boundary condition becomes

\begin{equation}
\tilde v
=
\dfrac{S^\ast}{cS_L}
\left[
\zeta\ell(y)
F\left(
\gamma
-
\dfrac{cS^\ast}{S_L}\tilde p
\right)
+
\varepsilon
\dfrac{\kappa}{\omega_r}\dot y
\right].
\end{equation}

Introducing the characteristic variables

\begin{equation}
w^\pm
=
\tilde v
\pm
k_L\tilde p,
\qquad
k_L
=
\dfrac{S^\ast}{S_L},
\end{equation}

together with

\begin{equation}
\tilde v
=
\dfrac{w^++w^-}{2},
\qquad
\tilde p
=
\dfrac{w^+-w^-}{2k_L},
\end{equation}

the boundary equation becomes

\begin{equation}
\dfrac{w^++w^-}{2}
=
\dfrac{S^\ast}{cS_L}
\left[
\zeta\ell(y)
F
\left(
\gamma
-
\dfrac{cS^\ast}{S_L}
\dfrac{w^+-w^-}{2k_L}
\right)
+
\varepsilon
\dfrac{\kappa}{\omega_r}\dot y
\right].
\end{equation}

Unlike the radiation condition, the unknown characteristic variable $w^+$
appears both outside and inside the nonlinear function $F$. Consequently, it
cannot be isolated analytically.

After rearranging the previous equation, the boundary state is defined as the
solution of the nonlinear scalar equation

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
R(w^+)
=
w^+
-
G(w^+)
=
0,
}
\label{eq:reed_scalar}
\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation}
G(w^+)
=
-w^-
+
2\dfrac{S^\ast}{cS_L}
\left[
\zeta\ell(y)
F
\left(
\gamma
-
\dfrac{cS^\ast}{S_L}
\dfrac{w^+-w^-}{2k_L}
\right)
+
\varepsilon
\dfrac{\kappa}{\omega_r}\dot y
\right].
\end{equation}

The reed boundary condition therefore differs fundamentally from the radiation
condition. Whereas the latter directly provides the incoming characteristic
variable through an explicit expression, the reed model requires the solution of
a nonlinear equation at every time step.

Fortunately, this problem remains one-dimensional, so that an iterative solver
can be employed with a negligible computational overhead compared with the cost
of advancing the acoustic field.

The numerical strategy adopted to solve Equation~\eqref{eq:reed_scalar}, together
with the complete DG discretization and the time integration procedure, are
presented in the next chapter.

\chapter{Numerical Resolution}
\label{chap:numerical_resolution}

\minitoc

The previous chapter reformulated the governing equations into a conservative
hyperbolic system and expressed both boundary conditions in terms of
characteristic variables. The resulting formulation is particularly well suited
for numerical approximation but still requires the design of an efficient
computational strategy capable of solving the coupled acoustic--mechanical
system.

The numerical solver developed in this work combines several complementary
numerical techniques. The acoustic field is discretized in space using the
Discontinuous Galerkin (DG) method, while the temporal integration relies on a
combination of an explicit second-order Runge--Kutta scheme for the acoustic
variables and a Crank--Nicolson discretization for the reed dynamics. The
nonlinear boundary condition at the mouthpiece is solved iteratively using a
damped Newton method.

Rather than considering these components independently, they should be viewed
as different building blocks of a single numerical algorithm. At every time
step, the boundary conditions are first evaluated, the corresponding ghost
states are reconstructed, the acoustic field is advanced using the DG solver,
and finally the reed variables are updated.

Figure~\ref{fig:solver_pipeline} summarizes the overall numerical strategy
adopted throughout this work.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{solver_pipeline.png}
\caption{Overall organization of the numerical solver.}
\label{fig:solver_pipeline}

\end{figure}

The following sections detail each component of this numerical procedure,
starting with the spatial discretization of the acoustic equations.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\section{Discontinuous Galerkin discretization}

The acoustic propagation is governed by the conservative hyperbolic system

\begin{equation}
\partial_t\tilde{\mathbf u}
+
\partial_x
\left(
A(x)\tilde{\mathbf u}
\right)
=
0,
\label{eq:dg_system}
\end{equation}

where

\[
\tilde{\mathbf u}
=
(\tilde p,\tilde v)^T
\]

contains the conservative variables introduced in the previous chapter.

Among the different numerical methods available for the approximation of
hyperbolic conservation laws, the Discontinuous Galerkin (DG) method has become
one of the most popular approaches. It combines several desirable properties,
including high-order accuracy, local conservation, geometric flexibility and
excellent parallelization capabilities.

Unlike continuous finite element methods, the DG approximation is allowed to be
discontinuous across element interfaces. Information is exchanged only through
numerical fluxes, which naturally handle wave propagation and discontinuities.
\cite{HesthavenWarburton2008}
These properties make the method particularly attractive for acoustic
simulations, where propagating waves and boundary interactions play a central
role.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Weak formulation}

Let

\[
K_j=(x_{j-1},x_j)
\]

denote a generic element of the computational mesh.

Multiplying Equation~\eqref{eq:dg_system} by a vector-valued test function
$\boldsymbol\psi$ and integrating over $K_j$ gives

\begin{equation}
\int_{K_j}
\partial_t\tilde{\mathbf u}
\cdot
\boldsymbol\psi
\,dx
+
\int_{K_j}
\partial_x
(A\tilde{\mathbf u})
\cdot
\boldsymbol\psi
\,dx
=
0.
\end{equation}

The derivative acting on the flux is transferred onto the test function by
integration by parts,

\begin{equation}
\int_{K_j}
\partial_t\tilde{\mathbf u}
\cdot
\boldsymbol\psi
\,dx
-
\int_{K_j}
A\tilde{\mathbf u}
\cdot
\partial_x\boldsymbol\psi
\,dx
+
\left(
A\tilde{\mathbf u}
\cdot
\boldsymbol\psi
\right)
\Big|_{\partial K_j}
=
0.
\label{eq:weak_form}
\end{equation}

The appearance of boundary terms is a fundamental feature of the DG method.
Since the numerical approximation is discontinuous from one element to the
next, the physical flux cannot be evaluated uniquely at the interfaces.
Consequently, it must be replaced by a numerical flux that consistently couples
neighboring elements.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Approximation space}

The computational domain is divided into $N_x$ non-overlapping elements

\[
\Omega
=
\bigcup_{j=1}^{N_x}
K_j.
\]

Within each element, the conservative variables are approximated by local
polynomials of degree one,

\begin{equation}
V_h^1
=
\left\{
u\in L^2(\Omega)
\;\middle|\;
u|_{K_j}\in\mathbb P_1(K_j)
\right\}.
\end{equation}

The numerical solution therefore belongs to the discontinuous finite element
space

\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf u}_h
\in
(V_h^1)^2.
\end{equation}

Using first-order polynomials represents a compromise between numerical
accuracy and computational efficiency. Although higher-order approximations
could easily be considered within the DG framework, the present work focuses on
a $P^1$ discretization, which already provides accurate solutions while keeping
the computational cost moderate.

Inside every element, the solution is expanded onto the local basis functions

\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf u}_h(x,t)
=
\sum_{i=1}^{2}
\mathbf U_i(t)\,
\varphi_i(x),
\end{equation}

where $\varphi_i$ denote the usual linear Lagrange basis functions.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Element matrices}

Substituting the polynomial approximation into the weak formulation
(Equation~\eqref{eq:weak_form}) and choosing the basis functions as test
functions yields a semi-discrete system of ordinary differential equations for
the nodal coefficients.

The first term gives rise to the local mass matrix

\begin{equation}
M_{ij}
=
\int_{K_j}
\varphi_i(x)\varphi_j(x)\,dx.
\end{equation}

Since the conservative variables have removed the geometric coefficients from
the time derivatives, the mass matrix is identical for every element. For a
uniform mesh of size \(h\) and linear basis functions, it is given by

\begin{equation}
M
=
\frac{h}{6}
\begin{pmatrix}
2 & 1\\
1 & 2
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}

This is one of the main advantages of the conservative reformulation presented
in Chapter~\ref{chap:mathematical_reformulation}. Without this change of
variables, each element would possess its own geometry-dependent mass matrix,
leading to a more complex implementation and increased computational cost.

The second integral defines the local stiffness contribution

\begin{equation}
K(\mathbf U)
=
\int_{K_j}
A(x)\tilde{\mathbf u}_h
\cdot
\partial_x\boldsymbol\varphi
\,dx,
\end{equation}

where the flux matrix is evaluated at the quadrature points inside each
element.

Finally, the interface contributions introduce numerical fluxes evaluated at
the two boundaries of every cell. The resulting semi-discrete formulation may
therefore be written as

\begin{equation}
M
\frac{d\mathbf U_j}{dt}
=
K_j(\mathbf U)
-
\widehat{\mathbf F}_{j+\frac12}
+
\widehat{\mathbf F}_{j-\frac12},
\label{eq:semi_discrete}
\end{equation}

where
\(\widehat{\mathbf F}\)
denotes the numerical flux.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Numerical flux}

Because the approximation is discontinuous across element interfaces, the
physical flux cannot be evaluated uniquely at the cell boundaries.

Let

\[
\tilde{\mathbf u}^-
\]

and

\[
\tilde{\mathbf u}^+
\]

denote the left and right traces of the numerical solution at a given
interface.

The numerical flux must satisfy two fundamental properties:

\begin{itemize}
\item consistency with the physical flux,

\item numerical stability.
\end{itemize}

In the present work, these requirements are fulfilled by the Rusanov
(Lax--Friedrichs) flux

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\widehat{\mathbf F}
=
\frac12
\left(
\mathbf F^-
+
\mathbf F^+
\right)
-
\frac{s_{\max}}{2}
\left(
\tilde{\mathbf u}^+
-
\tilde{\mathbf u}^-
\right),
}
\label{eq:rusanov_flux}
\end{equation}

where

\[
s_{\max}
=
\max |\lambda|
\]

is the largest propagation speed.

Since the eigenvalues of the acoustic system are

\[
\lambda^\pm=\pm c,
\]

the Rusanov parameter simply becomes

\begin{equation}
s_{\max}=c.
\end{equation}

The first term in Equation~\eqref{eq:rusanov_flux} represents the average
physical flux across the interface, whereas the second introduces numerical
dissipation proportional to the jump of the conservative variables.

Although more sophisticated approximate Riemann solvers could be employed, the
Rusanov flux offers an excellent compromise between simplicity, robustness and
accuracy for one-dimensional acoustic wave propagation.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Boundary treatment through ghost states}

The numerical flux introduced above requires two states at every interface.

Inside the computational domain, these states are naturally provided by the
neighboring elements.

At the two extremities of the tube, however, only one physical state is
available. A second state must therefore be constructed in order to evaluate
the numerical flux.

The Discontinuous Galerkin method addresses this difficulty through the
introduction of \emph{ghost states} located outside the computational domain.

Figure~\ref{fig:ghost_cell} illustrates this construction.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering

\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1]

\draw (0,0) rectangle (2,1);
\draw (2,0) rectangle (4,1);
\draw (4,0) rectangle (6,1);

\node at (1,0.5){Cell $N-1$};
\node at (3,0.5){Cell $N$};
\node at (5,0.5){Ghost};

\draw[thick,dashed] (4,-0.3)--(4,1.3);

\node[below] at (4,-0.3){Boundary};

\end{tikzpicture}

\caption{Construction of a ghost state outside the computational domain.}
\label{fig:ghost_cell}

\end{figure}

The ghost state is not an additional unknown of the problem. Instead, it is
reconstructed from the physical boundary conditions derived in the previous
chapter.

At the right boundary, the radiation model explicitly provides the incoming
characteristic variable. The corresponding ghost state is therefore obtained by
combining the outgoing characteristic computed by the DG solution with the
incoming characteristic determined by the radiation model.

Similarly, at the left boundary, the reed model supplies the incoming
characteristic after solving the nonlinear boundary equation. Once both
characteristic variables are known, the conservative variables are recovered
through

\begin{equation}
\tilde v
=
\frac{w^++w^-}{2},
\qquad
\tilde p
=
\frac{w^+-w^-}{2k},
\end{equation}

which completely defines the ghost state.

This characteristic reconstruction allows the same numerical flux to be used
both inside the computational domain and at the physical boundaries. As a
result, no special treatment of the DG discretization is required at the
extremities of the mesh.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\section{Semi-discrete formulation}

The DG discretization presented in the previous section transforms the
continuous acoustic problem into a finite-dimensional system of ordinary
differential equations. At this stage, only the spatial variables have been
discretized, while time remains continuous.

Denoting by

\[
\mathbf U(t)
\]

the vector gathering all DG degrees of freedom, the acoustic problem may be
written under the abstract form

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\frac{d\mathbf U}{dt}
=
\mathcal L
(\mathbf U,\phi,y,\dot y),
}
\label{eq:semi_discrete_system}
\end{equation}

where the nonlinear operator
\(\mathcal L\)
contains the DG volume contributions together with the numerical fluxes
evaluated at every interface.

Unlike a standard hyperbolic conservation law, the operator
\(\mathcal L\)
also depends on the boundary variables.
Indeed,

\begin{itemize}

\item the radiation variable \(\phi\) determines the ghost state at the bell,

\item the reed displacement \(y\) and its velocity \(\dot y\) determine the
ghost state at the mouthpiece.

\end{itemize}

Consequently, the complete model consists of the coupled system

\begin{equation}
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{d\mathbf U}{dt}
=
\mathcal L
(\mathbf U,\phi,y,\dot y),
\\[0.5em]
\dfrac{d\phi}{dt}
=
G(\mathbf U,\phi),
\\[0.5em]
\dfrac{dy}{dt}
=
z,
\\[0.5em]
\dfrac{dz}{dt}
=
K(\mathbf U,y,z),
\end{cases}
\label{eq:complete_ode_system}
\end{equation}

where \(z=\dot y\).

The numerical resolution of this coupled system requires an appropriate time
integration scheme capable of preserving the accuracy of the DG
discretization while remaining computationally efficient.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\section{Time integration}

Once the spatial discretization has been performed, the remaining problem
consists in integrating the coupled system of ordinary differential equations
given by Equation~\eqref{eq:complete_ode_system}.

Because the different variables exhibit distinct dynamical behaviors, a single
time integration scheme is not necessarily optimal for all components of the
system.

The acoustic field evolves according to a hyperbolic conservation law and is
therefore integrated explicitly using a second-order Runge--Kutta method.
Conversely, the reed behaves as a damped harmonic oscillator, whose stiffness
may lead to stability issues for explicit schemes. For this reason, the reed
equation is discretized using a semi-implicit Crank--Nicolson method.

This hybrid strategy combines the simplicity and efficiency of explicit time
integration for wave propagation with the improved stability of implicit
methods for oscillatory mechanical systems.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Runge--Kutta discretization of the acoustic field}

The semi-discrete acoustic system

\begin{equation}
\frac{d\mathbf U}{dt}
=
\mathcal L(\mathbf U)
\end{equation}

is advanced in time using the explicit midpoint Runge--Kutta scheme.

Starting from the solution at time \(t^n\), an intermediate state is first
computed,

\begin{equation}
\mathbf U^{n+\frac12}
=
\mathbf U^n
+
\frac{\Delta t}{2}
\mathcal L(\mathbf U^n).
\end{equation}

The solution is then updated according to

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\mathbf U^{n+1}
=
\mathbf U^n
+
\Delta t
\,
\mathcal L
(
\mathbf U^{n+\frac12}
).
}
\end{equation}

Compared with the explicit Euler method, the midpoint Runge--Kutta scheme
provides second-order accuracy in time while preserving a relatively simple
implementation.

Moreover, all boundary conditions are recomputed at the intermediate stage,
ensuring a consistent second-order treatment of the coupled acoustic problem.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Crank--Nicolson discretization of the reed dynamics}

The reed displacement satisfies the second-order differential equation

\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{\omega_r^2}
\ddot y
+
\frac{1}{Q_r\omega_r}
\dot y
+
y-1
=
\varepsilon
(\gamma-p).
\end{equation}

Introducing the auxiliary variable

\[
z=\dot y,
\]

the equation is rewritten as

\begin{equation}
\dot y
=
z,
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\dot z
=
\omega_r^2
\left[
\varepsilon(\gamma-p)
+
1-y
\right]
-
\frac{\omega_r}{Q_r}
z.
\end{equation}

Since the reed behaves as a damped oscillator, an explicit discretization would
require very small time steps when the resonance frequency becomes large.

To improve the numerical stability, the reed variables are therefore integrated
using the Crank--Nicolson method.

Assuming that the acoustic pressure remains constant during one reed update,
the discretization leads to

\begin{equation}
z^{n+1}
=
\frac{Bz^n+R}{A},
\end{equation}

with

\begin{equation}
A
=
1
+
\frac{\Delta t}{2}
\frac{\omega_r}{Q_r}
+
\frac{\Delta t^2}{4}
\omega_r^2,
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
B
=
1
-
\frac{\Delta t}{2}
\frac{\omega_r}{Q_r}
-
\frac{\Delta t^2}{4}
\omega_r^2,
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
R
=
\Delta t
\omega_r^2
\left[
\varepsilon(\gamma-p)
+
1-y^n
\right].
\end{equation}

Finally, the reed opening is updated according to

\begin{equation}
y^{n+1}
=
y^n
+
\frac{\Delta t}{2}
\left(
z^n
+
z^{n+1}
\right).
\end{equation}

This semi-implicit discretization considerably improves the robustness of the
solver while preserving second-order temporal accuracy.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\section{Boundary reconstruction}

The numerical flux introduced previously requires the conservative variables to
be known on both sides of every interface. While this information is directly
available inside the computational domain, additional states must be
constructed at the physical boundaries.

These so-called \emph{ghost states} are reconstructed from the characteristic
variables obtained in Chapter~\ref{chap:mathematical_reformulation}. The
procedure differs at the two extremities of the tube.

At the bell, the radiation model directly provides the incoming characteristic
variable through the explicit relation derived previously. The ghost state is
therefore reconstructed without any additional computation.

At the mouthpiece, however, the reed model defines the incoming
characteristic implicitly through a nonlinear equation. Consequently, a local
nonlinear problem must be solved before the ghost state can be reconstructed.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Implicit nonlinear equation}

The reed boundary condition obtained in the previous chapter can be written as

\begin{equation}
R(w^+)=0,
\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation}
R(w^+)
=
w^+
-
G(w^+).
\end{equation}

Unlike the radiation condition, no analytical expression of the incoming
characteristic variable is available because \(w^+\) appears inside the
nonlinear airflow function.

The computation of the boundary state therefore reduces to solving a scalar
nonlinear equation at every time step.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Newton iteration}

To determine the incoming characteristic variable, we employ Newton's method.

Starting from an initial approximation
\(w_0^+\),
the successive iterates are computed according to

\begin{equation}
w_{k+1}^+
=
w_k^+
-
\frac{R(w_k^+)}
{R'(w_k^+)}.
\end{equation}

Provided that the initial approximation is sufficiently close to the solution,
Newton's method converges quadratically.

In the present problem, an excellent initial guess is naturally provided by the
solution obtained at the previous time step,

\begin{equation}
w_0^+
=
w_{n-1}^+,
\end{equation}

since the acoustic variables evolve continuously in time.

As a result, only a small number of Newton iterations is generally required to
reach convergence.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Damped Newton method}

Although Newton's method exhibits quadratic convergence close to the solution,
its convergence may deteriorate when the initial approximation is farther from
the root.

To improve the robustness of the iterative process, a damping coefficient

\[
0<\theta_N\le1
\]

is introduced.

The Newton correction becomes

\begin{equation}
\Delta w
=
\frac{R(w)}
{R'(w)},
\end{equation}

and the iterative procedure reads

\begin{equation}
w_{k+1}
=
w_k
-
\theta_N
\Delta w.
\end{equation}

In addition, excessively large corrections are avoided by limiting the Newton
increment,

\begin{equation}
\Delta w
=
\mathrm{clip}
\left(
\Delta w,
-\Delta_{\max},
\Delta_{\max}
\right).
\end{equation}

The final update therefore becomes

\begin{equation}
w_{k+1}
=
w_k
-
\theta_N
\,
\mathrm{clip}
\left(
\frac{R(w_k)}
{R'(w_k)},
-\Delta_{\max},
\Delta_{\max}
\right).
\end{equation}

This damping strategy slightly reduces the asymptotic convergence rate but
greatly improves the robustness of the solver, particularly during the initial
transient of the simulation.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Ghost-state reconstruction}

Once the nonlinear equation has converged, both characteristic variables are
known at the reed boundary.

The conservative variables are then reconstructed through

\begin{equation}
\tilde v
=
\frac{w^++w^-}{2},
\qquad
\tilde p
=
\frac{w^+-w^-}{2k_L}.
\end{equation}

These quantities define the ghost state located outside the computational
domain.

The same reconstruction procedure is applied at the bell using the explicit
radiation condition.

Consequently, the numerical flux can be evaluated exactly as at any interior
interface, ensuring a uniform implementation of the DG method over the entire
computational domain.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\section{Stability considerations}

The stability of the numerical solver is primarily governed by the explicit
integration of the acoustic field.

For a DG discretization of polynomial degree \(k\), the time step must satisfy
a Courant--Friedrichs--Lewy (CFL) condition of the form

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\Delta t
\le
C_{\mathrm{CFL}}
\,
\frac{h}
{(2k+1)c},
}
\end{equation}

where \(h\) denotes the element size and \(c\) the speed of sound.

Throughout this work, the value of the CFL number is chosen sufficiently small
to guarantee stable wave propagation while preserving the computational
efficiency of the solver.

The semi-implicit Crank--Nicolson discretization employed for the reed dynamics
further improves the stability of the coupled simulation by preventing the
stiff mechanical oscillator from imposing excessively restrictive time-step
constraints.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\section{Implementation remarks}

Although the numerical method presented above follows the classical
Discontinuous Galerkin framework, several implementation choices were made to
improve both efficiency and robustness.

First, all geometric quantities depending on the instrument shape are computed
only once during the initialization stage. This includes the cross-sectional
areas, the flux matrices and the interpolation coefficients used during the
quadrature procedure. Consequently, the time integration only involves matrix
operations and numerical flux evaluations.

Secondly, the characteristic formulation considerably simplifies the
implementation of the boundary conditions. Regardless of the considered
boundary, the numerical flux is always evaluated from two conservative states.
The only difference lies in the construction of the ghost state, which is
obtained either explicitly from the radiation model or implicitly from the reed
model.

Finally, the nonlinear problem associated with the reed boundary remains
computationally inexpensive since it only involves a single scalar unknown.
Compared with the DG discretization of the acoustic field, its computational
cost is therefore negligible.

These implementation choices lead to a compact numerical solver in which the
same DG formulation is employed throughout the computational domain, including
at the physical boundaries.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\section{Discussion}

The numerical strategy proposed in this chapter combines several complementary
methods, each addressing a specific aspect of the coupled acoustic problem.

The Discontinuous Galerkin method provides a high-order spatial approximation
particularly well suited for hyperbolic wave propagation. The characteristic
reformulation introduced in the previous chapter allows the boundary conditions
to be incorporated naturally through ghost-state reconstruction. The nonlinear
reed model is efficiently handled by a damped Newton method, while the hybrid
RK2/Crank--Nicolson time integration ensures both accuracy and stability.

An important feature of this solver is that every component has been designed
with differentiability in mind. The numerical operations involved in the DG
discretization, the reconstruction of the ghost states and the time integration
are all compatible with automatic differentiation. As a consequence, the
complete forward solver can later be embedded into a gradient-based optimization
procedure without requiring any modification of the numerical algorithm itself.

The resulting solver therefore provides not only an accurate numerical
approximation of the physical model but also the computational foundation
required for the inverse calibration framework developed in the second part of
this manuscript.

\section*{Chapter summary}

In this chapter, a complete numerical strategy for solving the coupled
acoustic--mechanical model has been presented.

Starting from the conservative formulation derived previously, the acoustic
equations were discretized in space using the Discontinuous Galerkin method.
The radiation and reed boundary conditions were incorporated through
characteristic variables and ghost-state reconstruction, while the nonlinear
reed equation was solved iteratively using a damped Newton method.
Finally, the coupled system was integrated in time by combining a second-order
Runge--Kutta scheme for the acoustic field with a Crank--Nicolson
discretization of the reed dynamics.

The resulting solver constitutes the forward model used throughout the
remainder of this work. Before addressing the inverse calibration problem, its
accuracy and numerical properties are first assessed through several validation
experiments presented in the next chapter.


\chapter{Validation of the Forward Solver}
\label{chap:validation}

\minitoc

The previous chapters introduced the physical model governing sound generation
in reed wind instruments together with the numerical method developed to solve
the corresponding coupled acoustic--mechanical problem. Before employing this
solver within a gradient-based inverse calibration framework, its numerical
accuracy and robustness must first be carefully assessed.

The validation of the proposed solver is performed progressively through three
complementary stages. First, the Discontinuous Galerkin discretization is
validated on a simplified acoustic configuration for which an analytical
solution is available. First, the acoustic solver is validated on a simplified configuration in which
the nonlinear reed boundary condition is removed, while the radiation condition
at the bell is kept unchanged. Under these assumptions, an analytical solution
is available and provides an exact reference for assessing the accuracy of the
numerical method.

Once the acoustic solver has been validated, the complete coupled model is
compared with the OpenWind software, which provides a reliable reference for
the numerical simulation of wind instruments. Finally, the computational

\section{Validation of the acoustic solver}

The objective of this first validation is to verify the correctness of the
Discontinuous Galerkin implementation independently of the nonlinear reed
model. To this end, the acoustic equations are solved on a simplified test
configuration for which an analytical solution can be derived.

This benchmark serves two complementary purposes. First, it verifies that the
implementation correctly reproduces the exact solution of the continuous
problem. Secondly, it allows the experimental convergence order of the
numerical method to be compared with its theoretical prediction.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Test configuration}

To validate the spatial and temporal discretizations independently of the reed
model, the nonlinear reed boundary condition is removed and replaced by a
prescribed acoustic boundary condition for which an analytical solution can be
derived.

The radiation condition at the bell is, however, kept unchanged. This allows
the numerical implementation of the right boundary condition introduced in
Chapter~\ref{chap:mathematical_reformulation} to be validated together with the
DG discretization.

The computational domain is assumed to have two types of cross-sectional area:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a uniform cross-section $S(x)=S_0$,
    \item an "exponential" cross section 
\end{itemize}


so that the governing equations reduce to a linear acoustic propagation
problem. Under these assumptions, an analytical solution is available and can
be directly compared with the numerical approximation.

This benchmark therefore validates the complete acoustic solver, including the
DG discretization, the Runge--Kutta time integration and the implementation of
the radiation boundary condition, while avoiding the nonlinear coupling induced
by the reed dynamics.

important advantages. First, its smoothness allows the theoretical convergence

\subsection{Convergence analysis}

Beyond the visual agreement observed in the previous section, a quantitative
assessment of the numerical accuracy is obtained through a convergence study.

The spatial discretization employed throughout this work is based on a
Discontinuous Galerkin approximation using piecewise linear basis functions
(DG P1). Let \(h\) denote the characteristic mesh size and \(\Delta t\) the
time step. Since the simulations are performed under a CFL condition, both
quantities satisfy

\begin{equation}
\Delta t \propto h.
\label{eq:cfl_validation}
\end{equation}

The spatial approximation error of a DG method using polynomials of degree
\(k\) satisfies the classical estimate

\begin{equation}
\|u-\Pi_hu\|_{L^2}
\le
Ch^{k+1},
\end{equation}

where \(\Pi_hu\) denotes the projection of the exact solution onto the discrete
approximation space.

Since the present work employs piecewise linear polynomials
(\(k=1\)), the spatial discretization is expected to satisfy

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\|u-u_h\|_{L^2}
=
\mathcal O(h^2).
}
\label{eq:space_order}
\end{equation}

The overall accuracy of the numerical method also depends on the temporal
integration scheme.

For the Forward Euler discretization, the temporal error is first-order,

\begin{equation}
\|u-u_{\Delta t}\|
=
\mathcal O(\Delta t).
\end{equation}

Combining the spatial and temporal contributions yields

\begin{equation}
\|u-u_{h,\Delta t}\|
=
\mathcal O(h^2)
+
\mathcal O(\Delta t).
\end{equation}

Using Equation~\eqref{eq:cfl_validation}, one finally obtains

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\|u-u_{h,\Delta t}^{Euler}\|_{L^2}
=
\mathcal O(h).
}
\end{equation}

Consequently, although the DG P1 approximation is intrinsically second-order
accurate in space, the global convergence order is limited to first order by
the temporal discretization.

The situation differs for the Runge--Kutta method. Since the midpoint RK2
scheme is itself second-order accurate,

\begin{equation}
\|u-u_{\Delta t}\|
=
\mathcal O(\Delta t^2),
\end{equation}

the global error becomes

\begin{equation}
\|u-u_{h,\Delta t}\|
=
\mathcal O(h^2)
+
\mathcal O(\Delta t^2).
\end{equation}

Again using the CFL condition leads to

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\|u-u_{h,\Delta t}^{RK2}\|_{L^2}
=
\mathcal O(h^2),
}
\end{equation}

meaning that the second-order Runge--Kutta scheme fully preserves the accuracy
of the DG P1 spatial discretization.

The theoretical convergence rates are verified experimentally by computing the
relative \(L^2\)-error between the numerical and analytical solutions for a
sequence of increasingly refined meshes.

Figure~\ref{fig:validation_convergence} presents the resulting convergence
curves for both temporal integration schemes.

\caption{Experimental convergence rates for the Forward Euler and RK2 time

\label{fig:validation_convergence}
\end{figure}

The numerical results are in excellent agreement with the theoretical analysis.
The Forward Euler scheme exhibits the expected first-order convergence, whereas

This experiment constitutes an important validation of the numerical solver.

\subsection{Convergence towards the OpenWind solution}

Although no analytical solution is available for the complete coupled problem,
the convergence of the DG solver can still be assessed by comparing it with the
OpenWind solution while refining the spatial discretization.

Let \(u\) denote the exact solution of the continuous problem,
\(u_h^{DG}\) the DG approximation and
\(u_\eta^{OW}\) the OpenWind solution.

Assuming that both numerical methods are second-order accurate,

\begin{equation}
\|u_h^{DG}-u\|
\le
C_{DG}h^2,
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
\|u_\eta^{OW}-u\|
\le
C_{OW}\eta^2.
\end{equation}

Introducing the exact solution and applying the triangle inequality gives

\begin{equation}
\|u_h^{DG}-u_\eta^{OW}\|
\le
C_{DG}h^2
+
C_{OW}\eta^2.
\end{equation}

If both discretizations are refined simultaneously such that

\[
\eta\propto h,
\]

one obtains

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\|u_h^{DG}-u_\eta^{OW}\|
=
\mathcal O(h^2).
}
\end{equation}

The convergence curves obtained experimentally are presented in
Figure~\ref{fig:convergence_compare_shift_rk2}.

prediction. For both the cylindrical and the exponential geometries, the
difference between the two numerical solutions decreases quadratically as the
mesh is refined.

Since the DG implementation and OpenWind rely on independent numerical
frameworks, this agreement provides strong evidence that both solvers converge
towards the same continuous solution. Consequently, the proposed DG solver can
be considered as an accurate implementation of the complete physical model.

\section{Discussion}

The validation experiments presented throughout this chapter demonstrate the
accuracy of the proposed numerical solver at two complementary levels.

The first benchmark, based on an analytical solution, confirms the correctness
of the spatial and temporal discretizations. In particular, the expected
first- and second-order convergence rates are recovered for the Forward Euler
and Runge--Kutta schemes, respectively. This validates the implementation of
the Discontinuous Galerkin formulation as well as the treatment of the
radiation boundary condition.

The second benchmark addresses the complete reed--resonator system, for which
no analytical solution exists. The excellent agreement observed with OpenWind,
both qualitatively and quantitatively, demonstrates that the proposed solver
accurately reproduces the coupled acoustic and mechanical dynamics of the
instrument.

Taken together, these results provide strong evidence that the forward solver
constitutes an accurate numerical approximation of the physical model. This
validation is an essential prerequisite before considering gradient-based
inverse problems, where the quality of the identified parameters directly
depends on the accuracy of the forward simulations.

\chapter{Differentiable Inverse Calibration}
\label{chap:inverse}

\minitoc

The previous chapters focused on the construction and validation of a
differentiable forward solver for the simulation of reed wind instruments.
Once the direct model has been validated, it can be used as the computational
core of an inverse calibration procedure.

In practical applications, several quantities governing sound production are
not known with sufficient accuracy. The geometry of the resonator may be
available, whereas parameters associated with the excitation mechanism remain
effective quantities that are difficult to measure directly. The available
information is then an acoustic signal, either generated numerically with
OpenWind or obtained from experimental recordings supplied by Modartt.

The inverse problem considered in this work consists in recovering the unknown
quantities that make the simulated pressure signal reproduce the observation as
closely as possible. Because the acoustic field is nonlinearly coupled with the
reed dynamics, this problem is nonlinear, non-convex and potentially
non-identifiable. Different parameter combinations may produce similar acoustic
responses, while small variations of some parameters may alter the oscillation
regime or the sounding frequency.

The proposed methodology is not restricted to the estimation of scalar physical
parameters. It also allows an unknown constitutive law, namely the reed opening
function \(\ell(y)\), to be represented by a differentiable parametric model and
learned from acoustic observations. The inverse problem may therefore combine
signal-dependent physical parameters with a function shared by the complete
dataset.

This chapter introduces the mathematical formulation of the inverse problem,
the losses used to compare acoustic signals, the differentiation of the complete
numerical solver and the gradient-based optimization strategy. The final
sections describe the representation and regularization of the reed opening law,
together with the datasets and experimental protocols used in the numerical
study.

The complete inverse calibration workflow is summarized in
Figure~\ref{fig:inverse_framework}. The unknown physical parameters and the
parameters defining the reed opening law are supplied to the differentiable
forward solver. The simulated pressure is compared with the observation through
an objective function. Automatic differentiation then propagates the loss
gradient through the complete simulation, and a gradient-based optimizer updates
the unknown quantities before the next forward evaluation.

    The physical parameters and the reed opening law are supplied to the

\section{Formulation of the inverse problem}
\label{sec:inverse_formulation}

\subsection{Parameter identification}

Let
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\theta}
=
(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_{N_\theta})
\end{equation}
denote the vector collecting the physical parameters treated as unknown.
Depending on the experiment, this vector may contain a subset of
\begin{equation}
\left(
\gamma,\zeta,\kappa,\omega_r,Q_r
\right).
\end{equation}

For a fixed geometry, prescribed initial conditions and a given simulation
horizon, the numerical solver defines a discrete forward operator
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{F}_h:
\boldsymbol{\theta}
\longmapsto
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{sim}}
=
\left(
p_{\mathrm{sim}}(L,t_0),\ldots,
p_{\mathrm{sim}}(L,t_{N-1})
\right),
\label{eq:forward_map}
\end{equation}
where \(h\) collectively denotes the spatial and temporal discretization
parameters.

Given an observed pressure signal
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{obs}}
=
\left(
p_{\mathrm{obs}}(t_0),\ldots,
p_{\mathrm{obs}}(t_{N-1})
\right),
\end{equation}
the parameter-identification problem is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
\in
\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta}
\mathcal{L}
\left(
\mathcal{F}_h(\boldsymbol{\theta}),
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{obs}}
\right),
}
\label{eq:inverse_problem}
\end{equation}
where \(\Theta\) is the admissible parameter set and \(\mathcal{L}\) is a
discrepancy measure.

Two types of observations are considered:
\begin{itemize}
    \item synthetic signals generated with OpenWind, for which the reference
    parameters are known;
    \item experimental recordings provided by Modartt, for which the physical
    parameters must be inferred from measured sound.
\end{itemize}

Synthetic data make it possible to quantify reconstruction errors and evaluate
the robustness of the method. Experimental data constitute the final
application, but also introduce measurement noise, synchronization uncertainty
and model discrepancy.

\subsection{Identification of the reed law}

The reed-flow condition contains a constitutive function \(\ell(y)\) describing
the effective reed opening. In the synthetic reference model, this function is
prescribed by
\begin{equation}
\ell_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)
=
\max(y,0).
\label{eq:reference_reed_law}
\end{equation}

More generally, the law is represented by a differentiable parametric model
\begin{equation}
\ell(y)
=
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(y),
\end{equation}
where \(\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_\eta}\) denotes its trainable
parameters. The forward operator then depends on both groups of unknowns:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{F}_h:
(\boldsymbol{\eta})
\longmapsto
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{sim}}.
\end{equation}

The joint inverse problem becomes
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
 \widehat{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
\in
\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathcal{E}}
\mathcal{J}
(\boldsymbol{\eta}),
}
\label{eq:joint_inverse_problem}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{J}
=
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{obs}}
+
\lambda_{\ell}
\mathcal{R}_{\ell}.
\label{eq:joint_objective_general}
\end{equation}
Here, \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{obs}}\) compares simulated and observed pressures,
while \(\mathcal{R}_{\ell}\) introduces prior information on the shape of the
reed law.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\section{Objective functions}
\label{sec:objective_functions}

The objective function determines which discrepancies are emphasized during
calibration and directly shapes the optimization landscape. No single distance
is optimal for every experiment. This work therefore considers time-domain,
spectral and hybrid objectives.

\subsection{Time-domain discrepancy}

The most direct comparison is the mean squared error
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MSE}}
=
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}
\left(
p_{\mathrm{sim}}[n]
-
p_{\mathrm{obs}}[n]
\right)^2.
\label{eq:mse_loss}
\end{equation}

The MSE is inexpensive and preserves phase information. It is useful when the
signals are accurately synchronized and when the waveform itself is the
quantity of interest. However, a small phase shift or a slight difference in
oscillation frequency may produce a large pointwise error even when the two
sounds have similar harmonic structures.

\subsection{Short-Time Fourier Transform}

The Fourier transform provides a global frequency representation but does not
retain the temporal evolution of the spectrum. Musical sounds are
non-stationary: the attack, transient regime and established oscillation may
have different spectral contents.

The Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) applies a Fourier transform to
successive overlapping windows. As presented in \cite{allen1977stft} Given a discrete signal
\(x=(x_0,\ldots,x_{N-1})\), the STFT is defined by
\begin{equation}
X(m,k)
=
\sum_{n=0}^{L-1}
x[n+mH]\,
w[n]\,
e^{-2\mathrm{i}\pi kn/L},
\label{eq:stft}
\end{equation}
where \(L\) is the window length, \(H\) is the hop size, \(w\) is the analysis
window, \(m\) is the frame index and \(k\) is the frequency bin.

For each time frame, the STFT produces a local spectrum. The complete transform
is a complex-valued matrix
\begin{equation}
X\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times K}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Spectrogram representation}

The STFT coefficients contain both magnitude and phase information. In the
spectral objective considered in this work, only the magnitude is retained.
For a signal \(x\), its magnitude spectrogram is defined by

\begin{equation}
S_x(m,k)
=
\left|
\operatorname{STFT}(x)(m,k)
\right|.
\label{eq:spectrogram}
\end{equation}

Each column of \(S_x\) describes the frequency content of one temporal frame,
whereas each row represents the temporal evolution of a given frequency bin.
The STFT is computed using a Hann analysis window, with a prescribed window
length and hop size.

For visualization, the magnitude spectrogram is first normalized by its maximum
value,

\begin{equation}
\widetilde{S}_x(m,k)
=
\frac{
S_x(m,k)
}{
\displaystyle \max_{m,k} S_x(m,k) + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{norm}}
},
\label{eq:normalized_spectrogram}
\end{equation}

where \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{norm}}>0\) prevents division by zero. It is then
expressed in decibels as

\begin{equation}
S_{x,\mathrm{dB}}(m,k)
=
20\log_{10}
\left(
\widetilde{S}_x(m,k)
+
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{dB}}
\right).
\label{eq:log_spectrogram}
\end{equation}

The value of \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{dB}}\) is determined from a prescribed
dynamic range \(D\), expressed in decibels:

\begin{equation}
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{dB}}
=
10^{-D/20}.
\label{eq:dynamic_range_epsilon}
\end{equation}

In the experiments, \(D=60\,\mathrm{dB}\) unless stated otherwise. This lower
bound prevents the logarithm from becoming singular and limits the influence
of spectral components whose amplitudes lie far below the dominant components.

The spectrogram highlights the fundamental frequency, the harmonic structure,
transient phenomena and resonance patterns. Since phase is discarded, this
representation is less sensitive than a pointwise time-domain discrepancy to
small temporal or phase shifts. Nevertheless, its optimization properties
remain strongly dependent on the analysis window, the time--frequency
resolution, the magnitude compression and the distance used to compare the
spectrograms.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Single-resolution spectral discrepancy}

For the \(i\)-th STFT configuration, characterized by the window length \(L_i\)
and hop size \(H_i\), define

\begin{equation}
S_i^{\mathrm{obs}}
=
\left|
\operatorname{STFT}_i
\left(
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{obs}}
\right)
\right|,
\qquad
S_i^{\mathrm{sim}}
=
\left|
\operatorname{STFT}_i
\left(
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{sim}}
\right)
\right|.
\label{eq:target_predicted_spectrograms}
\end{equation}

Both spectrograms are normalized using a common scale computed exclusively from
the observed signal:

\begin{equation}
a_i
=
\max_{m,k}
S_i^{\mathrm{obs}}(m,k)
+
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{norm}},
\label{eq:spectral_normalization_scale}
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
\widetilde{S}_i^{\mathrm{obs}}
=
\frac{S_i^{\mathrm{obs}}}{a_i},
\qquad
\widetilde{S}_i^{\mathrm{sim}}
=
\frac{S_i^{\mathrm{sim}}}{a_i}.
\label{eq:normalized_target_predicted_spectrograms}
\end{equation}

Using a normalization scale that depends only on the target preserves the
relative amplitude difference between the simulated and observed signals. It
also prevents the simulated signal from independently rescaling its own
spectrogram during optimization.

The first component of the objective compares the normalized magnitudes in a
linear scale:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{lin}}^{(i)}
=
\frac{1}{N_i}
\sum_{m,k}
\left|
\widetilde{S}_i^{\mathrm{sim}}(m,k)
-
\widetilde{S}_i^{\mathrm{obs}}(m,k)
\right|,
\label{eq:magnitude_loss}
\end{equation}

where \(N_i\) denotes the total number of time--frequency coefficients at the
\(i\)-th resolution.

The second component compares the normalized magnitudes in decibels. Define

\begin{align}
D_i^{\mathrm{sim}}(m,k)
&=
20\log_{10}
\left(
\widetilde{S}_i^{\mathrm{sim}}(m,k)
+
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{dB}}
\right),
\\
D_i^{\mathrm{obs}}(m,k)
&=
20\log_{10}
\left(
\widetilde{S}_i^{\mathrm{obs}}(m,k)
+
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{dB}}
\right).
\end{align}

The corresponding discrepancy is

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dB}}^{(i)}
=
\frac{1}{N_i}
\sum_{m,k}
\left|
D_i^{\mathrm{sim}}(m,k)
-
D_i^{\mathrm{obs}}(m,k)
\right|.
\label{eq:db_magnitude_loss}
\end{equation}

The linear-magnitude term mainly emphasizes dominant spectral components,
whereas the decibel term increases the relative contribution of weaker
harmonics and low-energy structures. The spectral discrepancy associated with
one resolution is therefore

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{spec}}^{(i)}
=
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{lin}}^{(i)}
+
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dB}}^{(i)}.
}
\label{eq:single_resolution_spectral_loss}
\end{equation}

Both terms are mean absolute discrepancies. Consequently, the contribution of a
resolution does not increase simply because its spectrogram contains a larger
number of coefficients.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Multi-resolution spectral loss}

A single STFT configuration cannot simultaneously provide accurate temporal
localization and fine frequency discrimination. The multi-resolution spectral
loss therefore combines several STFT representations \cite{schwaer2023multi}:

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MR}}
=
\frac{1}{M}
\sum_{i=1}^{M}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{spec}}^{(i)},
}
\label{eq:mrstft_loss}
\end{equation}

where \(M\) denotes the number of retained STFT resolutions.

The generic set of configurations implemented in the loss is

\begin{equation}
(L_i,H_i)
\in
\left\{
(64,32),
(128,64),
(256,128)
\right\}.
\label{eq:default_stft_configurations}
\end{equation}

Only configurations satisfying \(L_i\leq N\), where \(N\) is the signal length,
are retained when zero-padding is disabled. Alternatively, shorter signals may
be zero-padded to allow the use of larger analysis windows.

Depending on the experiment, a different subset of resolutions may be supplied
to the loss. In particular, the calibration experiments may use larger windows,
such as

\begin{equation}
(L_i,H_i)
\in
\left\{
(64,16),
(128,32),
(256,64),
(512,128)
\right\},
\label{eq:experimental_stft_configurations}
\end{equation}

when the signal duration is sufficient.

The shortest windows provide better temporal localization and are therefore
more sensitive to rapid variations and attack transients. Conversely, the
longest windows offer a finer frequency resolution and better distinguish nearby
harmonic components. Averaging the loss over several resolutions forces the
simulated signal to reproduce the observation at multiple time--frequency
scales simultaneously.

A spectral objective is not necessarily easier to optimize than a time-domain
objective. Parameters that directly or indirectly control the sounding
frequency may generate irregular and highly non-convex spectral-loss
landscapes. The STFT resolutions, the Hann window, the dynamic range and the
relative weighting of the linear and decibel terms must therefore be regarded
as integral components of the optimization method rather than as purely
signal-processing choices.

\section{Differentiable forward model}
\label{sec:differentiable_forward_model}

The calibration procedure repeatedly evaluates the direct solver and computes
the derivative of the loss with respect to the unknown quantities. The numerical
model must therefore be differentiable as a complete algorithm.

\subsection{Discrete forward operator}

Let \(\mathbf{U}^n\) denote the complete discrete state at time \(t^n\). It
contains the DG degrees of freedom for pressure and acoustic volume velocity,
the radiation variable, and the variables describing the reed motion.

One time step may be written abstractly as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{U}^{n+1}
=
\Phi_{\Delta t}
\left(
\mathbf{U}^n;
\boldsymbol{\theta},
\boldsymbol{\eta}
\right),
\label{eq:time_step_operator}
\end{equation}
where \(\Phi_{\Delta t}\) includes the evaluation of the DG residual, the
boundary states and the time-integration formulas.

The complete computation is the composition
\begin{equation}
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\eta})
\longrightarrow
\mathbf{U}^0
\longrightarrow
\mathbf{U}^1
\longrightarrow
\cdots
\longrightarrow
\mathbf{U}^{N_t}
\longrightarrow
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{sim}}
\longrightarrow
\mathcal{J}.
\label{eq:computational_graph}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Differentiability of the numerical components}

The DG spatial discretization, numerical fluxes and Runge--Kutta updates are
compositions of elementary algebraic operations. They are differentiable with
respect to both the discrete state and the physical parameters wherever the
underlying constitutive relations are differentiable.

For one residual evaluation
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{K}^n
=
\mathcal{R}
\left(
\mathbf{U}^n;
\boldsymbol{\theta},
\boldsymbol{\eta}
\right),
\end{equation}
the sensitivity to a parameter \(\theta_j\) formally satisfies
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \mathbf{K}^n}{\partial\theta_j}
=
\frac{\partial\mathcal{R}}{\partial\mathbf{U}}
\frac{\partial\mathbf{U}^n}{\partial\theta_j}
+
\frac{\partial\mathcal{R}}{\partial\theta_j}.
\label{eq:stage_sensitivity}
\end{equation}

In the implementation, these relations are not assembled by hand. JAX records
the elementary operations defining the solver and automatically applies the
chain rule through the complete computational graph.

\subsection{Nonlinear reed boundary condition}

The main difficulty is the nonlinear boundary relation at the reed. At each
time step, the boundary state must satisfy an algebraic equation involving the
incoming characteristic variable, the reed displacement, the pressure
difference and the nonlinear flow function
\begin{equation}
F(\Delta p)
=
\operatorname{sign}(\Delta p)
\sqrt{|\Delta p|}.
\end{equation}

The reed displacement is updated through the time discretization of the reed
oscillator equation. Separately, the nonlinear algebraic boundary relation is
solved with a damped Newton method. If \(z\) denotes the nonlinear boundary
unknown, one iteration reads
\begin{equation}
z^{(q+1)}
=
z^{(q)}
-
\lambda_q
\frac{
G(z^{(q)};\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\eta})
}{
\partial_z G(z^{(q)};\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\eta})
},
\label{eq:newton_iteration_diff}
\end{equation}
where \(\lambda_q\in(0,1]\) is a damping factor.

Because the Newton iterations are implemented with JAX-compatible operations,
the differentiated algorithm propagates derivatives through the finite sequence
of iterations.

Near \(\Delta p=0\), the derivative of the square-root law is singular. The
numerical implementation therefore uses a regularized evaluation, for example
\begin{equation}
F_{\varepsilon_F}(\Delta p)
=
\operatorname{sign}(\Delta p)
\sqrt{|\Delta p|+\varepsilon_F},
\label{eq:regularized_flow_function}
\end{equation}
with a small \(\varepsilon_F>0\).


\subsection{Implicit differentiation of the boundary state}

The nonlinear boundary condition defines the boundary state implicitly as the
solution of a nonlinear equation

\[
w^\star = G(w^\star,\theta).
\]

Rather than differentiating through every Newton iteration, the derivatives of
the converged solution can be obtained by differentiating the fixed-point
equation itself.

Differentiating with respect to a parameter $\theta$ gives

...

which leads to

\[
\boxed{
\frac{\partial w^\star}{\partial\theta}
=
\left(
I-
\frac{\partial G}{\partial w}
\right)^{-1}
\frac{\partial G}{\partial\theta}.
}
\]

This expression provides the sensitivity of the converged boundary state with
respect to the physical parameters and constitutes the basis of implicit
differentiation.


\subsection{Automatic differentiation}

Automatic differentiation computes derivatives of the implemented numerical
algorithm by repeated application of the chain rule. It differs both from
symbolic differentiation and from finite differences.

The scalar objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{J}
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\eta})
=
\mathcal{L}
\left(
\mathcal{F}_h
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\eta}),
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{obs}}
\right).
\end{equation}

The required gradients are
\begin{equation}
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{J},
\qquad
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\mathcal{J}.
\end{equation}

By the chain rule,
\begin{equation}
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{J}
=
\left(
\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_h}
     {\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}
\right)^{\!\top}
\nabla_{\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{sim}}}
\mathcal{L},
\label{eq:chain_rule_parameters}
\end{equation}
and similarly
\begin{equation}
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\mathcal{J}
=
\left(
\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_h}
     {\partial\boldsymbol{\eta}}
\right)^{\!\top}
\nabla_{\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{sim}}}
\mathcal{L}.
\label{eq:chain_rule_law}
\end{equation}

The Jacobians of the full solver are not constructed explicitly.
Reverse-mode automatic differentiation directly evaluates the required
vector--Jacobian products.

\subsection{Reverse mode and computational cost}

Reverse mode is well suited to this problem because the output objective is
scalar, while the number of optimized quantities may be large when the reed law
is represented by a neural network.

The forward pass evaluates
\begin{equation}
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\eta})
\longmapsto
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{sim}}
\longmapsto
\mathcal{J},
\end{equation}
and the reverse pass propagates the adjoint information backward through all
time steps.

The main limitation is memory: intermediate states required by the reverse pass
must be retained or recomputed. Long simulation windows and fine temporal
discretizations are therefore expensive both in computation time and memory.

The gradients obtained here are derivatives of the discretized objective. The
algorithm first discretizes the PDE--ODE system and then differentiates the
resulting numerical program. This discretize-then-optimize viewpoint makes the
validation of the forward solver essential.

\section{Gradient-based optimization}
\label{sec:gradient_optimization}

\subsection{Optimization variables}

The framework distinguishes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item local physical parameters, which may vary from one signal to another;
    \item global parameters \(\boldsymbol{\eta}\) defining the shared reed law.
\end{itemize}

For a dataset of \(N_s\) signals, the local variables are
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(s)},
\qquad
s=1,\ldots,N_s,
\end{equation}
whereas a single vector \(\boldsymbol{\eta}\) is shared by all signals.

The dataset objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{data}}
=
\frac{1}{N_s}
\sum_{s=1}^{N_s}
\mathcal{L}_p
\left(
\mathcal{F}_h
(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(s)},\boldsymbol{\eta}),
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{obs}}^{(s)}
\right)
+
\lambda_\ell
\mathcal{R}_\ell(\boldsymbol{\eta}).
\label{eq:dataset_objective}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Initialization, multi-start strategy and parameter admissibility}

The multi-D optimization problem is non-convex, and its solution may depend strongly on
the initial parameter vector. To reduce this sensitivity, an initial multi-start
procedure is optionally performed before the main optimization stage.

Let
\[
\left\{
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{(r)}
\right\}_{r=1}^{N_{\mathrm{start}}}
\]
denote a collection of physically admissible initial guesses. For each starting
point, a short Adam optimization is performed on the long observation window.
The resulting candidates are then evaluated independently for every signal.

For signal \(s\), the selected initialization is

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{MS}}^{(s)}
=
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{r_s}^{(s)},
\qquad
r_s
=
\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{r}
\mathcal{L}^{(s)}
\left(
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{r}^{(s)}
\right).
\label{eq:multistart_selection}
\end{equation}

Consequently, different signals may select different starting points. The main
Adam stage is initialized with the signal-dependent parameters obtained from
this selection.

After the first optimization stage, signals whose individual loss remains above
a prescribed threshold are treated separately. For each difficult signal, a
second collection of starting points is generated around its current estimate,

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0,r}^{(s)}
=
\operatorname{clip}
\left(
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{current}}^{(s)}
+
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r},
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\min},
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\max}
\right),
\label{eq:relative_multistart}
\end{equation}

where \(\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}\) denotes a relative perturbation. A second
short optimization is then performed and the best candidate is retained only if
it improves the previous loss.

The physical parameters are normalized using fixed characteristic scales before
optimization. The initial multi-start values are required to be positive, and
the relative multi-start initializations are clipped to prescribed admissible
ranges. However, the standard Adam stages do not enforce positivity throughout
the complete optimization trajectory. Positivity is explicitly guaranteed only
during the Gauss--Newton refinement, through a lower projection, and during the
L-BFGS refinement, through the logarithmic parameterization

\begin{equation}
\theta_j
=
\exp(\eta_j).
\end{equation}


\subsection{Initialization and local minima}

The objective is non-convex and may contain several attraction basins.
Initialization therefore has a major influence on the recovered parameters.

For low-dimensional calibration, a multi-start strategy consists in:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item generating several admissible initial parameter vectors;
    \item performing a short optimization from each vector;
    \item ranking the candidates according to their final loss;
    \item continuing the optimization from the best candidates.
\end{enumerate}

For poorly calibrated signals, additional initializations can be generated
relative to the best current estimate rather than repeating the same global
grid. This concentrates the computational effort on difficult cases.

\section{Learning the reed opening law}
\label{sec:learning_reed_law}

\subsection{Neural representation}

The function \(\ell(y)\) is represented by a small multilayer perceptron
\begin{equation}
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}:
\mathbb{R}
\longrightarrow
\mathbb{R}.
\end{equation}

The architecture used in the experiments contains one scalar input, two hidden
layers and one scalar output:
\begin{equation}
1
\longrightarrow
8
\longrightarrow
8
\longrightarrow
1.
\label{eq:lfunc_architecture}
\end{equation}
Hyperbolic tangent activation functions are used in the hidden layers.

\subsection{Anchoring and scale ambiguity}

The product
\begin{equation}
\zeta\,\ell(y)
\end{equation}
appears in the reed-flow boundary condition. Simultaneous optimization of
\(\zeta\) and \(\ell\) therefore introduces an approximate scale ambiguity.

To reduce this ambiguity, the law is anchored at a reference displacement:
\begin{equation}
\ell(1)=1.
\label{eq:lfunc_anchor}
\end{equation}

The associated penalty is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{anchor}}
=
\left(
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(1)-1
\right)^2.
\label{eq:anchor_regularization}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Positivity and shape regularization}

The reed opening should remain non-negative in the physically relevant regime.
A positivity penalty is introduced:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{pos}}
=
\frac{1}{N_y}
\sum_{j=1}^{N_y}
\left[
\max
\left(
-\ell_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(y_j),0
\right)
\right]^2.
\label{eq:positivity_regularization}
\end{equation}

A monotonicity prior may additionally be imposed:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{mono}}
=
\frac{1}{N_y}
\sum_{j=1}^{N_y}
\left[
\max
\left(
-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y}
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(y_j),0
\right)
\right]^2.
\label{eq:monotonicity_regularization}
\end{equation}

The complete regularization is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{R}_\ell
=
\lambda_{\mathrm{anchor}}
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{anchor}}
+
\lambda_{\mathrm{pos}}
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{pos}}
+
\lambda_{\mathrm{mono}}
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{mono}}.
\label{eq:lfunc_regularization}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Identifiability from acoustic observations}

The reed displacement is not observed in the experimental setting. The
available data consist only of pressure recordings. Learning \(\ell(y)\) is
therefore indirect: the law is inferred through its influence on reed flow,
acoustic propagation and pressure at the bell.

A single signal explores only a limited range of reed displacements and cannot
identify the complete law over a broad interval. Multiple signals with
different excitation parameters are therefore required.

\section{Conclusion}

This chapter formulated the calibration of reed-instrument models as a
differentiable inverse problem. The unknown quantities may consist of scalar
physical parameters, a constitutive reed-opening law, or both.

Time-domain and multi-resolution spectral losses were introduced to compare
simulated and observed acoustic signals. The complete DG--ODE solver was then
interpreted as a discrete computational graph and differentiated with
reverse-mode automatic differentiation in JAX.

The optimization framework distinguishes local parameters associated with each
signal from a global reed law shared across the dataset. Physical constraints
are enforced through differentiable transformations, while anchoring,
positivity and monotonicity regularizations reduce the ambiguity of the learned
function.

Finally, the methodology was organized in two stages. Synthetic OpenWind data
provide controlled tests with known parameters and a known reed law. The
resulting strategy is then transferred to experimental recordings supplied by
Modartt, initially through calibration of the mouth-pressure parameter
\(\gamma\).

The next chapter presents the numerical calibration results and analyses the
accuracy, robustness and limitations of the proposed approach.

\section{Sensitivity analysis}

Before evaluating the complete calibration procedure, it is useful to study
the behavior of the objective function with respect to variations of the
physical parameters.

The purpose of this preliminary analysis is twofold.

First, it provides insight into the local geometry of the optimization
landscape. Parameters associated with sharp minima are expected to be more
easily identifiable than parameters producing flatter loss profiles.

Second, it allows the influence of each physical parameter on the acoustic
response to be analyzed independently before considering the complete
inverse problem involving several unknowns simultaneously.

For each parameter, the reference OpenWind signal is generated using the
nominal physical configuration described in Chapter~3. One parameter is
then varied while all the others are kept fixed, and the multi-resolution
spectral loss introduced in Chapter~5 is evaluated with respect to the
reference signal.

\subsection{One-dimensional Scans}

The resulting one-dimensional loss scans are presented in
Figure~\ref{fig:parameter_scans}.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../../experiments/gradient/results/scans/scan_1D_MSTS_4params_openwind.png}
    \caption{One-dimensional scans of the multi-resolution spectral loss with respect to variations of the physical parameters. 
    Each curve corresponds to a single parameter, while all other parameters are kept fixed.}
    \label{fig:parameter_scans}
\end{figure}

The loss associated with the parameter $\kappa$ exhibits a sharp and well
localized minimum around the reference value. Small deviations from the optimum
produce a rapid increase of the objective function, indicating that this
parameter has a strong influence on the generated acoustic signal. Such a
landscape is favorable for gradient-based optimization since the gradients
remain informative over a large neighborhood of the solution.

The mouth pressure parameter $\gamma$ also produces a well-defined minimum.
However, the loss increases much more rapidly for values larger than the
reference pressure than for smaller values. A noticeable discontinuity appears
around $\gamma \approx 0.6$, suggesting the existence of a transition between
two oscillation regimes of the reed model. This behavior reflects the
nonlinear nature of the excitation mechanism.

A similar behavior is observed for the parameter $\zeta$. Around the optimum,
the objective function presents a narrow valley that should facilitate the
identification process. For larger values of $\zeta$, the loss exhibits a
sudden increase before continuing its progression. This phenomenon is likely
associated with a qualitative modification of the reed dynamics rather than a
simple linear variation of the acoustic response.

The behavior of the quality factor $Q_r$ is markedly different. Although a
minimum is clearly visible near the reference value, the objective function
remains relatively flat over a broad interval. Consequently, large variations
of $Q_r$ only induce limited modifications of the acoustic signal, making this
parameter intrinsically more difficult to identify from pressure measurements
alone.

This observation is consistent with the statistical calibration experiments
presented later in this chapter, where $Q_r$ systematically exhibits larger
estimation errors than the other physical parameters.

Overall, the one-dimensional scans indicate that the proposed objective
function possesses well-defined minima for the four investigated parameters.
However, the curvature of the objective strongly depends on the considered
parameter. While $\gamma$, $\kappa$ and $\zeta$ generate steep optimization
landscapes, the loss is significantly flatter with respect to $Q_r$, suggesting
a lower sensitivity of the radiated pressure to this parameter.

These observations provide a first explanation of the different levels of
identifiability observed in the calibration experiments.

The previous one-dimensional scans investigate the sensitivity of the
objective function with respect to each parameter independently.

However, the calibration problem involves several unknowns simultaneously.
Consequently, it is also important to study the joint behavior of the loss
function with respect to pairs of parameters.

To this end, two-dimensional scans are performed by varying two parameters
while keeping all the remaining quantities fixed.
\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../../experiments/gradient/results/scans/scan_2D_gamma_zeta_kappa_Qr_MSTS_openwind_T0p050.png}
    \caption{Two-dimensional scans of the multi-resolution spectral loss with respect to variations of the physical parameters. 
    Each surface corresponds to a pair of parameters of Qr and one of the other parameters, while all other parameters are kept fixed.}
    \label{fig:parameter_scans_2D}

Figure~\ref{fig:parameter_scans_2D} reveals that the objective function exhibits a
well-defined minimum in all three parameter planes.

Interestingly, the valleys remain almost perfectly aligned with the
\(Q_r\)-axis. This indicates that the loss is much more sensitive to
variations of \(\gamma\), \(\kappa\) and \(\zeta\) than to variations of
\(Q_r\).

The minimum of the scanned objective function does not exactly coincide with 
the nominal parameter values because of numerical discretization, finite time 
integration, and the approximation introduced by the discrete loss evaluation. 
The displacement nevertheless remains small for the well-identified parameters.

No pronounced diagonal valley is observed, suggesting that the considered
parameter pairs are only weakly correlated. Consequently, the difficulty
encountered when estimating \(Q_r\) does not originate from a strong coupling
with the other reed parameters, but rather from the intrinsically low
sensitivity of the radiated pressure with respect to the quality factor.

These observations motivated the subsequent calibration strategy adopted in
this work.

Since the objective function exhibits only limited sensitivity with respect
to \(Q_r\), the statistical experiments presented in the remainder of this
chapter focus on the estimation of

\[
(\gamma,\kappa,\zeta),
\]

while the quality factor is assumed to be known.

\section{Calibration on synthetic OpenWind signals}

\subsection{Experimental Protocol}
Using a dataset of 300 synthetic OpenWind signals, the calibration procedure is 
evaluated on a controlled setting where the true physical parameters are known. 
The signals are generated by varying the parameters \(\gamma\), \(\kappa\), and \(\zeta\)
within a prescribed range, while keeping the quality factor \(Q_r\) and the other 
parameters fixed.
All the simulations are initialized with the same reference reed law $l(y)=max(y,0)$ and 
the same set of trainable parameters $(\gamma_0,\kappa_0,\zeta_0)$.

\subsection{Optimization strategy}

The calibration problem considered in this work is nonlinear and non-convex.
Consequently, the optimization process may converge towards different local
minima depending on the initial parameter values. Although the sensitivity
analysis presented in the previous section revealed well-defined minima for the
considered parameters, gradient-based methods remain sensitive to their
initialization, particularly when several parameters are estimated
simultaneously.

To improve the robustness of the optimization, a preliminary multi-start stage
is performed before the main calibration procedure. A set of physically
plausible initial parameter vectors is defined from prior knowledge of the
instrument. Starting from each initialization, a short Adam optimization is
performed on the long observation window in order to move the parameters toward
a nearby local minimum.

Rather than selecting a single initialization for the whole dataset, the
selection is carried out independently for every signal. For each candidate,
the objective function is evaluated individually, and each signal retains the
parameter vector producing the lowest loss. Consequently, the subsequent
optimization does not start from a common initialization but from the
best-performing candidate associated with each individual signal. This strategy
reduces the probability of converging toward poor local minima while preserving
the statistical independence of the calibration problems.

As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:calibration_pipeline}, once this initialization 
phase has been completed, the optimization is performed through two successive stages. 
The first stage uses the complete observation window in order to recover the global 
characteristics of the acoustic signal. Signals whose reconstruction error remains 
above a prescribed threshold are then considered separately. For these difficult 
cases, a second localized multi-start is performed around the current estimate, 
followed by a short Adam refinement. 

\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{pipeline_calibration.png}
    \caption{Schematic representation of the calibration pipeline.}
    \label{fig:calibration_pipeline}
\end{figure}
\newpage 
During the Adam optimization, a three Short-Time Fourier Transform resolutions are 
simultaneously considered. Each STFT is computed using a Hann analysis window with the 
corresponding window length and hop size. The objective function is the average of the 
associated multi-resolution spectral losses.

\section{Calibration from noisy OpenWind signals}
\label{sec:noisy_openwind_results}

The previous section evaluated the calibration procedure under idealized
conditions, using noise-free pressure signals generated with OpenWind. Although
this setting makes it possible to assess the intrinsic accuracy of the inverse
method, measured acoustic signals are inevitably affected by perturbations.
These may originate from the recording system, the surrounding environment or
unmodelled physical phenomena.

The second numerical experiment therefore investigates the robustness of the
calibration procedure when the observations are contaminated by additive
noise. The objective is to determine whether the physical parameters remain
identifiable when the target pressure signal no longer corresponds exactly to
the output of the reference model.

\subsection{Noise model and experimental protocol}

The noisy experiments are constructed from the same collection of
\(300\) OpenWind signals as in the noise-free study. Consequently, the exact
values of the physical parameters

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(s)}
=
\left(
\gamma^{(s)},
\kappa^{(s)},
\zeta^{(s)}
\right),
\qquad
s=1,\ldots,300,
\end{equation}

remain known for every signal.

Using the same signals in both experiments makes it possible to isolate the
effect of the observation noise. In particular, differences in calibration
accuracy cannot be attributed to different parameter realizations or different
oscillation regimes.

For each clean pressure signal
\(\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{clean}}^{(s)}\), a noisy observation is generated as

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{noisy}}^{(s)}
=
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{clean}}^{(s)}
+
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{(s)},
\label{eq:noisy_observation}
\end{equation}

where \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{(s)}\) is an additive white-noise realization.
Its amplitude is adjusted to obtain a prescribed signal-to-noise ratio (SNR).

The SNR, expressed in decibels, is defined by

\begin{equation}
\operatorname{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}}
=
10\log_{10}
\left(
\frac{
\left\|
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{clean}}
\right\|_2^2
}{
\left\|
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
\right\|_2^2
}
\right).
\label{eq:snr_definition}
\end{equation}

Four noise levels are considered:

\begin{equation}
\operatorname{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}}
\in
\left\{
10,\,
20,\,
30,\,
40
\right\}.
\label{eq:considered_snr}
\end{equation}

A low SNR corresponds to a strongly contaminated observation, whereas a high
SNR corresponds to a signal closer to the noise-free reference. Thus, the
\(10\,\mathrm{dB}\) case represents the most difficult configuration and the
\(40\,\mathrm{dB}\) case the least noisy one.

For each SNR value, the same calibration strategy as in the noise-free
experiment is applied. The same multi-start initialization, Adam optimization,
long observation window and targeted refinement procedure are retained. The
quality of the reconstruction is therefore evaluated under identical numerical
conditions.

The optimization objective remains the multi-resolution spectral loss. At each
STFT resolution, the normalized linear-magnitude discrepancy and the decibel
discrepancy are combined. Since the spectral representation distributes the
comparison over time and frequency, it is expected to be less sensitive than a
pointwise waveform loss to local high-frequency perturbations. Nevertheless,
the logarithmic component may increase the relative contribution of weak
spectral components, including those introduced by noise.

\(\theta\in\{\gamma,\kappa,\zeta\}\), the relative estimation error is computed

\begin{equation}
e_{\theta}^{(s)}

\frac{
\left|

-
\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(s)}
\right|
}{
\left|
\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(s)}
\right|
