\begin{abstract}
This project focuses on the automated calibration of physical models for reed musical instruments. 
We develop a numerical framework to simulate wave propagation inside instruments with variable cross-sectional areas using the Discontinuous Galerkin Method (DGM). 
Special attention is given to the implementation of boundary conditions that model radiation and reed dynamics. 
The framework is validated against analytical solutions for uniform cross-sections and extended to non-uniform geometries, such as exponential and conical profiles. 
Results demonstrate accurate wave propagation, proper handling of complex boundaries, and convergence consistent with theoretical expectations. 
The proposed approach provides a foundation for efficient parameter calibration in virtual instrument models.

\chapter*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Introduction}

During my project of the thrid semester of the CSMI Master degree, I worked on the automated calibration 
of physical models for musical instruments. 
The goal of this project was to develop a method to automatically adjust the parameters of physical models 
so that they accurately reproduce the characteristic sounds of reed instruments. \newline
I worked under the supervision of Dr Emmanuel Franck, Dr Laurent Navoret and Dr Victor Michel-Dansac from 
the Institut de Recherche Mathématique Avancée (IRMA) in Strasbourg and Dr Juliette Chabassier from Modartt. \newline
Modartt (MOdels and Data for ARTs and Technology)\cite{Modartt} is a french company specialized in the development of artistic and technological application softwares.
In 2006, they released \textit{Pianoteq}, a software that simulates the sound of various musical instruments using physical modeling techniques isntead 
of relying on pre-recorded samples. \newline
First, we present the context of reed instruments and the state of the art of the mathematical modeling of the wave equation inside these instruments.
Then, we describe the numerical methods used to solve the wave equation with different types of cross-sectional 
area (uniform, exponential, conical) using the Discontinuous Galerkin Method (DGM). We will particularly focus on the implementation of the boundary conditions
that represent a crucial aspect of the behavior of the sound produced by the instrument.
Finally, we present the results of the numerical simulations and the performance analysis of the implemented method.

\section*{Reed Instruments}
A reed is a thin, flexible lamina mounted at the mouthpiece or at the entrance of an air column resonator that acts \cite{Universalis_Anche} \cite{chaigne:hal-05370840} as a valve that controls the airflow driven by a pressure difference between the upstream region 
(the player’s mouth) and the downstream region (the mouthpiece or mouthpiece tip of the instrument). \newline
There are two principal categories of reed instruments:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Free reeds (harmonicas, accordions, ...).
    \item Beating reeds (clarinets, saxophones, ...).
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Physical Model}

The equations governing the behavior of reed instruments are mathematically described \cite{MAMAMIA2024} by a set of coupled 
differential equations (Partial Differential Equations - PDEs, Ordinary Differential Equations - ODEs, and 
algebraic equations). These equations capture the complex interactions between the pressure ($p$), the acoustic
volume velocity ($v$) \eqref{eq:1a} \eqref{eq:1b}, radiative phenomenons happening at the bell of the instrument($\phi$) \eqref{eq:1c} \eqref{eq:1d}, 
and the effects of the instrumental player \eqref{eq:1e} \eqref{eq:1f} :

% Système d'équations décrivant un modèle réducteur pour un instrument à anche
\begin{subequations}
\begin{empheq}[left=\empheqlbrace]{align}
\frac{S(x)}{c S^\ast}\,\partial_t p(x,t) + \partial_x v(x,t) &= 0 \label{eq:1a}\\[4pt]
\frac{S^\ast}{c S(x)}\,\partial_t v(x,t) + \partial_x p(x,t) &= 0 \label{eq:1b}\\[4pt]
Z^\ast_T\,\partial_t \phi + \sqrt{\alpha}\,p(L,t) &= 0 \label{eq:1c}\\[4pt]
-\frac{\beta}{Z^\ast_T}\,p(L,t) + v(L,t) + \sqrt{\alpha}\,\phi &= 0 \label{eq:1d}\\[4pt]
-v(0,t) + \zeta \ell(y)\,F(\gamma - p(0,t)) + \varepsilon\frac{\kappa}{\omega_r}\,\partial_t y &= 0 \label{eq:1e}\\[6pt]
\frac{1}{\omega_r^2}\,\partial_{tt} y + \frac{1}{Q_r\omega_r}\,\partial_t y + y - 1 
&= \varepsilon(\gamma - p(0,t)) \label{eq:1f}
\end{empheq}
\end{subequations}

where $S(x)$ is the cross-sectional area of the instrument at position $x$, $S^\ast$ is a reference area,
$c$ is the speed of sound, $Z^\ast_T$ is a reference acoustic impedance, 
$\alpha$ and $\beta$ are parameters related to the radiation effects, $L$ is the length of the instrument,
$\zeta_\ell(y)$ is a function representing the lip-reed coupling, $F$ is a nonlinear function ($F(\Delta p)=\sqrt{|\Delta p|} \, \text{sign} (\Delta p)$),
$\gamma$ is the dimensionless blowing pressure, $\varepsilon$ =-1 for a reed instrument, 
$\kappa$ is a parameter related to the lip dynamics, $\omega_r$ is the reed's (or lips') frequency ($=2\pi f_r$), 
$Q_r$ is the quality factor of the reed, and $y$ is the dimensionless reed opening. \newline
This kind of system of equation can already be solved numerically with Openwind \cite{OpenwindInria}.

\section{Conservative formulation}
The main issue with the latter formulation is that the first two equations \eqref{eq:1a} and \eqref{eq:1b} are not in conservative form, 
which makes it difficult to apply the Discontinuous Galerkin Method (DGM) for numerical resolution.
To address this issue, we introduce the following change of variables:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{u} = 
\begin{pmatrix}
    \tilde{p} \\ \tilde{v}
\end{pmatrix} 
= 
\begin{pmatrix}
    \frac{S(x)}{c S^\ast}\,p \\ \frac{S^\ast}{c S(x)}\,v
\end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}

The system of equations can then be rewritten in a conservative form as follows:
\begin{equation}
    \partial_t \tilde{u} + \partial_x F(\tilde{u}) = \partial_t \tilde{u} + \partial_x (A(x)\tilde{u}) = 0
\end{equation}
where $A(x) = \begin{pmatrix}
    0 & \frac{c S(x)}{S^\ast} \\ \frac{c S^\ast}{S(x)} & 0
\end{pmatrix}$ is the flux matrix. 
\newline

From this change of variables, we can also compute the Riemann invariants of the system. \newline
Fist, we compute the eigenvalues of the flux matrix $A(x)$:
$$\lambda^\pm = \pm c$$
Then, we compute the corresponding left-hand side eigenvectors $l_{\pm}$ with $l=(l_1, l_2)$:
\begin{equation}
    l_{\pm} A(x) = \lambda^\pm l_{\pm}
\end{equation}
which gives for $\lambda^+ = c$:
\begin{equation}
    l_2 = \frac{S^\ast}{S(x)} l_1
\end{equation}
and for $\lambda^- = -c$:
\begin{equation}
    l_2 = -\frac{S^\ast}{S(x)} l_1
\end{equation}

So, we can choose the left-hand side eigenvectors as follows:
\begin{equation}
    l^+ = (1, \frac{S^\ast}{S(x)}),
    \qquad
    l^- = (1, -\frac{S^\ast}{S(x)}).
\end{equation}

The Riemann invariants $w^\pm$ are then computed as the projection of the state vector $\tilde{u}$ onto the left-hand side eigenvectors:    
\begin{equation}
    w^\pm = \tilde{v} \pm \kappa\tilde{p}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa(x)=\frac{S^\ast}{S(x)}$. \newline
These Riemann invariants represent the characteristic variables of the system 
They appear to be very useful for understanding the wave propagation and for implementing boundary conditions.

\section{Radiation Boundary Conditions}

The right boundary condition at $x=L$ is modeled by the coupled system
\begin{equation}
\begin{cases}
Z^\ast_T\,\partial_t \phi(t)
+ \sqrt{\alpha}\, p(L,t) = 0,
\\[0.4em]
-\dfrac{\beta}{Z^\ast_T}\, p(L,t)
+ v(L,t)
+ \sqrt{\alpha}\,\phi(t) = 0.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:bc_ode_pde}
\end{equation}

The first equation governs the temporal evolution of the boundary variable
$\phi(t)$, while the second equation defines a dynamic impedance relation
between pressure and velocity at the boundary. Rewriting the second equation yields
\begin{equation}
v(L,t)
=
\frac{\beta}{Z^\ast_T} p(L,t)
-
\sqrt{\alpha}\,\phi(t).
\tag{BC}
\end{equation}

Introducing the transformed variables
\[
\tilde p
=
\frac{S(x)}{cS^\ast}p,
\qquad
\tilde v
=
\frac{S^\ast}{cS(x)}v,
\]
the boundary condition becomes
\begin{equation}
\frac{cS_L}{S^\ast}\tilde v(L,t)
=
\frac{\beta}{Z^\ast_T}
\frac{cS^\ast}{S_L}\tilde p(L,t)
-
\sqrt{\alpha}\,\phi(t),
\end{equation}
where $S_L = S(L)$.

Dividing by $\dfrac{cS_L}{S^\ast}$ gives
\begin{equation}
\tilde v(L,t)
=
\frac{\beta}{Z^\ast_T}
\left(
\frac{S^\ast}{S_L}
\right)^2
\tilde p(L,t)
-
\frac{S^\ast}{cS_L}
\sqrt{\alpha}\,\phi(t).
\end{equation}

We now introduce the riemann invariants at the boundary $x=L$:
\begin{equation}
w^\pm
=
\tilde v
\pm
\kappa \tilde p,
\qquad
\kappa
=
\left(
\frac{S_{\ast}}{S_R}
\right).
\end{equation}
with $S_R = S(L)$.
Hence,
\begin{equation}
\tilde v
=
\frac{w^+ + w^-}{2},
\qquad
\tilde p
=
\frac{w^+ - w^-}{2\kappa}.
\end{equation}

Substituting these expressions into the boundary condition yields
\begin{equation}
\frac{w^+ + w^-}{2}
=
\frac{\beta}{Z^\ast_T}
\left(
\frac{S^\ast}{S_R}
\right)^2
\frac{w^+ - w^-}{2\kappa}
-
\frac{S^\ast}{cS_R}
\sqrt{\alpha}\,\phi(t).
\end{equation}

Defining
\[
K
=
\frac{\beta}{Z^\ast_T}
\left(
\frac{S^\ast}{S_R}
\right)^2,
\qquad
r
=
\frac{K}{\kappa},
\qquad
C
=
\frac{S^\ast}{cS_R}\sqrt{\alpha},
\]
we obtain
\begin{equation}
w^-
\left(
r+1
\right)
=
w^+
\left(r-1
\right)
-
2C\phi(t),
\end{equation}
and therefore
\begin{equation}
w^-
=
\frac{r-1}
     {r+1}
\, w^+
-
\frac{2C}
     {r+1}
\, \phi(t).
\end{equation}

This relation provides the incoming characteristic variable $w^-$
as a function of the outgoing characteristic $w^+$ wich we impose 
to be $w^+= \tilde{v}(L) + \kappa \tilde{p}(L)$ and the boundary 
state variable $\phi(t)$.

\section{Reed Model}
The left-hand side boundary conditions \eqref{eq:1e} and \eqref{eq:1f} 
represent the coupling between the acoustic field inside the instrument and the dynamics of the reed.
At \(x=0\), the reed boundary condition is described by the coupled system
\begin{equation}
\begin{cases}
-v(0,t)
+
\zeta \ell(y)\,F(\gamma - p(0,t))
+
\varepsilon\frac{\kappa}{\omega_r}\,\partial_t y
= 0,
\\[0.5em]
\frac{1}{\omega_r^2}\,\partial_{tt} y
+
\frac{1}{Q_r\omega_r}\,\partial_t y
+
y - 1
=
\varepsilon(\gamma - p(0,t)).
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

Using the transformed variables
\begin{equation}
\tilde p
=
\frac{S(x)}{cS^\ast}p,
\qquad
\tilde v
=
\frac{S^\ast}{cS(x)}v,
\end{equation}
the first boundary equation becomes
\begin{equation}
\frac{cS(0)}{S^\ast}\tilde v(0,t)
=
\zeta \ell(y)\,
F\!\left(
\gamma
-
\frac{cS^\ast}{S(0)}\tilde p(0,t)
\right)
+
\varepsilon\frac{\kappa}{\omega_r}\,\partial_t y.
\end{equation}

Hence,
\begin{equation}
\tilde v(0,t)
=
\frac{S^\ast}{cS(0)}
\left[
\zeta \ell(y)\,
F\!\left(
\gamma
-
\frac{cS^\ast}{S(0)}\tilde p(0,t)
\right)
+
\varepsilon\frac{\kappa}{\omega_r}\,\partial_t y
\right].
\end{equation}

We now introduce the characteristic variables
\begin{equation}
w^\pm
=
\tilde v
\pm
k_c \tilde p,
\qquad
k_c = \frac{S^\ast}{S(0)}.
\end{equation}

The outgoing characteristic at the left boundary is therefore
\begin{equation}
w^-
=
\tilde v(0,t)
-
k_c \tilde p(0,t),
\end{equation}
while the incoming characteristic \(w^+\) is determined by the nonlinear reed condition.

Using
\begin{equation}
\tilde v
=
\frac{w^+ + w^-}{2},
\qquad
\tilde p
=
\frac{w^+ - w^-}{2k_c},
\end{equation}
the boundary equation becomes
\begin{equation}
\frac{w^+ + w^-}{2}
=
\frac{S^\ast}{cS(0)}
\left[
\zeta \ell(y)\,
F\!\left(
\gamma
-
\frac{cS^\ast}{S(0)}
\frac{w^+ - w^-}{2k_c}
\right)
+
\varepsilon\frac{\kappa}{\omega_r}\,\partial_t y
\right].
\end{equation}

Rearranging yields the nonlinear equation
\begin{equation}
w^+
=
-w^-
+
2\frac{S^\ast}{cS(0)}
\left[
\zeta \ell(y)\,
F\!\left(
\gamma
-
\frac{cS^\ast}{S(0)}
\frac{w^+ - w^-}{2k_c}
\right)
+
\varepsilon\frac{\kappa}{\omega_r}\,\partial_t y
\right].
\end{equation}

This equation is solved iteratively using a fixed-point method in order to determine the incoming characteristic \(w^+\).

Once \(w^+\) has been computed, the boundary state is reconstructed from
\begin{equation}
\tilde v
=
\frac{w^+ + w^-}{2},
\qquad
\tilde p
=
\frac{w^+ - w^-}{2k_c}.
\end{equation}

\minitoc

\section{Discontinuous Galerkin Method}

The Discontinuous Galerkin method is used to discretize the acoustic PDE in space. 
It is well suited for hyperbolic conservation laws because it combines local polynomial
approximations with numerical fluxes at cell interfaces \cite{HesthavenWarburton2008}.

\subsection{Original Mixed Formulation}

We consider the mixed formulation of the one-dimensional acoustic equations:
\begin{equation}
\frac{S(x)}{cS^\ast}\partial_t p+\partial_x v=0,
\qquad
\frac{S^\ast}{cS(x)}\partial_t v+\partial_x p=0.
\label{eq:mixed_system}
\end{equation}

Here, \(p\) is the acoustic pressure, \(v\) the volume velocity, \(S(x)\) the
cross-sectional area, \(S^\ast\) a reference section, and \(c\) the speed of sound.

\subsection{Weak Formulation and Mass Matrix Issue}

Let \(K_j=(x_{j-1},x_j)\) be a mesh cell. Multiplying by test functions
\(\psi_p,\psi_v\in\mathbb P_1(K_j)\) and integrating by parts gives
\begin{equation}
\int_{K_j}\frac{S(x)}{cS^\ast}\partial_t p\,\psi_p\,dx
-\int_{K_j}v\,\partial_x\psi_p\,dx
+v\psi_p\Big|_{\partial K_j}=0,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\int_{K_j}\frac{S^\ast}{cS(x)}\partial_t v\,\psi_v\,dx
-\int_{K_j}p\,\partial_x\psi_v\,dx
+p\psi_v\Big|_{\partial K_j}=0.
\end{equation}

A direct DG discretization would therefore lead to geometry-dependent mass matrices:
\begin{equation}
(M_p)_{ij}
=
\int_{K_j}
\frac{S(x)}{cS^\ast}\psi_i\psi_j\,dx,
\qquad
(M_v)_{ij}
=
\int_{K_j}
\frac{S^\ast}{cS(x)}\psi_i\psi_j\,dx.
\end{equation}

This is inconvenient because the mass matrices depend on the local geometry.

\subsection{Conservative Change of Variables}

To obtain uniform mass matrices, we introduce
\begin{equation}
\tilde p=\frac{S(x)}{cS^\ast}p,
\qquad
\tilde v=\frac{S^\ast}{cS(x)}v.
\end{equation}

With
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf u}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\tilde p\\
\tilde v
\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
the system becomes
\begin{equation}
\partial_t\tilde{\mathbf u}
+
\partial_x\left(A(x)\tilde{\mathbf u}\right)=0,
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
A(x)=
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & \dfrac{cS(x)}{S^\ast}\\[0.4em]
\dfrac{cS^\ast}{S(x)} & 0
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}

The physical flux is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf F(\tilde{\mathbf u})
=
A(x)\tilde{\mathbf u}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
v\\
p
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}

The eigenvalues of \(A(x)\) are
\begin{equation}
\lambda_\pm=\pm c,
\end{equation}
independently of the geometry. The corresponding Riemann invariants are
\begin{equation}
w^\pm
=
\tilde v
\pm
\frac{S^\ast}{S(x)}\tilde p.
\end{equation}

\subsection{DG Approximation Space}

We seek
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf u}_h=(\tilde p_h,\tilde v_h)^T\in (V_h^1)^2,
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
V_h^1=
\left\{
u\in L^2(0,L)
\;\middle|\;
u|_{K_j}\in\mathbb P_1(K_j)
\right\}.
\end{equation}

The local mass matrix is now the standard P1 mass matrix:
\begin{equation}
M
=
\frac{h}{6}
\begin{pmatrix}
2&1\\
1&2
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Local DG Formulation}

For each element \(K_j\), the DG formulation reads
\begin{equation}
\int_{K_j}
\partial_t\tilde{\mathbf u}_h\cdot\boldsymbol\psi\,dx
-
\int_{K_j}
\left(A(x)\tilde{\mathbf u}_h\right)\cdot\partial_x\boldsymbol\psi\,dx
+
\widehat{\mathbf F}_{j+\frac12}\cdot\boldsymbol\psi(x_j^-)
-
\widehat{\mathbf F}_{j-\frac12}\cdot\boldsymbol\psi(x_{j-1}^+)
=0.
\label{eq:dg_local_vector}
\end{equation}

\section{Numerical Fluxes}

At each interface, the numerical flux is chosen as a Rusanov flux:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathbf F}
=
\frac12
\left(
\mathbf F^-+\mathbf F^+
\right)
-
\frac{c}{2}
\left(
\tilde{\mathbf u}^+ - \tilde{\mathbf u}^-
\right).
\end{equation}

Writing
\[
\tilde{\mathbf u}^\pm
=
(\tilde p^\pm,\tilde v^\pm)^T,
\]
one obtains
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathbf F}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\widehat F_p\\
\widehat F_v
\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\widehat F_p
&=
\frac12
\left(
\frac{cS}{S^\ast}\tilde v^-
+
\frac{cS}{S^\ast}\tilde v^+
\right)
-
\frac{c}{2}
(\tilde p^+-\tilde p^-),
\\[0.5em]
\widehat F_v
&=
\frac12
\left(
\frac{cS^\ast}{S}\tilde p^-
+
\frac{cS^\ast}{S}\tilde p^+
\right)
-
\frac{c}{2}
(\tilde v^+-\tilde v^-).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Boundary fluxes are obtained by constructing ghost states from the characteristic
variables \(w^\pm\), using the reed condition at \(x=0\) and the radiation condition
at \(x=L\).

\section{Time Integration}

\subsection{Semi-discrete Formulation}

After spatial discretization, the coupled problem can be written abstractly as
\begin{equation}
\frac{dX}{dt}=F(X),
\qquad
X=(\boldsymbol U,\phi,y,z),
\end{equation}
where \(\boldsymbol U\) contains the DG degrees of freedom, \(\phi\) is the radiation
boundary variable, \(y\) is the reed opening, and \(z=\dot y\).

More explicitly,
\begin{equation}
F(X)=
\begin{pmatrix}
M^{-1}\mathcal L(\boldsymbol U,\phi,y,z)\\
G(\boldsymbol U,\phi)\\
z\\
K(\boldsymbol U,y,z)
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Explicit Euler Scheme}

The explicit Euler scheme is used as a simple first-order reference:
\begin{equation}
X^{n+1}=X^n+\Delta t\,F(X^n).
\end{equation}

It is straightforward to implement, but it is very restrictive for wave propagation
problems because of its small stability region.

\subsection{Second-order Runge--Kutta Scheme}

For the acoustic field and the radiation variable, we use a second-order explicit
Runge--Kutta midpoint scheme:
\begin{equation}
X^{n+\frac12}
=
X^n+\frac{\Delta t}{2}F(X^n),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
X^{n+1}
=
X^n+\Delta t\,F(X^{n+\frac12}).
\end{equation}

In practice, the reed variables are treated separately using a Crank--Nicolson
step, while the acoustic and radiation variables are advanced with RK2.

\subsection{Crank--Nicolson Scheme for the Reed Dynamics}

The reed equation is
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{\omega_r^2}\ddot y
+
\frac{1}{Q_r\omega_r}\dot y
+
y-1
=
\varepsilon(\gamma-p_L).
\end{equation}

Introducing \(z=\dot y\), we obtain
\begin{equation}
\dot y=z,
\qquad
\dot z=
\omega_r^2\left[\varepsilon(\gamma-p_L)+1-y\right]
-\frac{\omega_r}{Q_r}z.
\end{equation}

With \(p_L\) frozen during one reed update, the Crank--Nicolson discretization gives
\begin{equation}
z^{n+1}
=
\frac{Bz^n+R}{A},
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
A=
1+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\frac{\omega_r}{Q_r}
+\frac{\Delta t^2}{4}\omega_r^2,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
B=
1-\frac{\Delta t}{2}\frac{\omega_r}{Q_r}
-\frac{\Delta t^2}{4}\omega_r^2,
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
R=
\Delta t\,\omega_r^2
\left[
\varepsilon(\gamma-p_L)+1-y^n
\right].
\end{equation}

The reed opening is then updated by
\begin{equation}
y^{n+1}
=
y^n
+
\frac{\Delta t}{2}
\left(
z^n+z^{n+1}
\right).
\end{equation}

This semi-implicit treatment improves stability for the reed oscillator, especially
when \(\omega_r\) is large.

\subsection{Coupling Strategy}

At each time step, the acoustic state is evaluated at both boundaries. The radiation
condition at \(x=L\) is imposed by reconstructing a ghost state from the outgoing
characteristic and the auxiliary variable \(\phi\). At \(x=0\), the reed condition
provides a boundary velocity depending on \(y\), \(z\), and the local pressure.

The reconstructed ghost states are then used in the numerical fluxes. In the RK2
scheme, this procedure is repeated at the midpoint stage in order to preserve
second-order accuracy for the acoustic field.

\subsection{CFL Stability Discussion}

The CFL restriction depends on both the DG spatial discretization and the time
integration scheme. After spatial discretization, one may consider the linearized
system
\begin{equation}
\frac{dU}{dt}=LU.
\end{equation}

For an eigenmode satisfying \(\dot u=\lambda u\), an explicit method is stable when
\begin{equation}
|R(\Delta t\lambda)|\le 1,
\end{equation}
where \(R\) is the stability function of the time scheme.

For explicit Euler,
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{Euler}}(z)=1+z,
\end{equation}
whereas for RK2,
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{RK2}}(z)=1+z+\frac{z^2}{2}.
\end{equation}

For purely oscillatory modes \(z=i\omega\Delta t\), Euler satisfies
\begin{equation}
|R_{\mathrm{Euler}}(i\omega\Delta t)|^2
=
1+(\omega\Delta t)^2>1,
\end{equation}
so it amplifies oscillatory modes. RK2 gives
\begin{equation}
|R_{\mathrm{RK2}}(i\omega\Delta t)|^2
=
1+\frac{(\omega\Delta t)^4}{4}.
\end{equation}

Thus, RK2 also slightly amplifies purely imaginary modes, but only at order
\((\omega\Delta t)^4\), whereas Euler amplifies them at order
\((\omega\Delta t)^2\). This explains the much better practical stability observed
with RK2 for acoustic wave propagation.

For a DG method of degree \(k\), the time step typically scales as
\begin{equation}
\Delta t
\le
C_{\mathrm{CFL}}
\frac{h}{(2k+1)c},
\end{equation}
where \(C_{\mathrm{CFL}}\) depends on the chosen time integration scheme.

\section{JAX Implementation and Differentiability}

The numerical solver was implemented in JAX. This choice was motivated by two main
objectives: efficient execution through JIT compilation and differentiability of the
complete simulation pipeline.

Most computational kernels were compiled using \texttt{jax.jit}, which reduces Python
overhead during long time integrations. Local DG operations were vectorized over the
mesh using \texttt{jax.vmap}, allowing element-wise computations to be expressed without
explicit Python loops.

Time integration was implemented using \texttt{jax.lax.scan}, which provides a compiled
loop compatible with automatic differentiation. This is particularly important for
future inverse problems, where gradients of a loss function with respect to physical
parameters must be computed through the whole simulation.

The implementation was designed in a functional style, avoiding in-place mutations and
using JAX-compatible operations for ghost-cell construction, boundary conditions and
time stepping. For long simulations, reverse-mode differentiation can lead to high
memory consumption, so checkpointing strategies may be used to reduce the memory cost
during gradient computation.
